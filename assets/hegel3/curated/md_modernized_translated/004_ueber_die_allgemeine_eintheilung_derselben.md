## On Its General Division

What needed to be said about the concept of this science, and about where its justification falls, has been said in the introduction. From that concept there also follows the provisional general division of the science.

Logic, as the science of pure thinking, or quite generally as the pure science, has for its element that unity of the subjective and the objective which is absolute knowledge, and to which spirit has raised itself as to its absolute truth. The determinations of this absolute element have the sense of being neither merely thoughts nor merely objective determinations, neither empty abstractions and concepts moving about beyond actuality, nor yet essentialities foreign to the I and an objective in-itself, nor again mere external conjunctions and minglings of the two. The element of this science is rather the unity in which being is pure concept in its own self, and only the pure concept is the true being.

{{{ 2 }}} Now inasmuch as this unity determines and develops itself, its determinations must have the form of that separation, for the unity is precisely unity of that difference, and its development is the exhibition of what it contains within itself, and so of that difference of being and of thinking. Yet since knowledge consists in this, that the truth of this difference lies in its unification, then, once knowledge develops that difference in and out of its own self by its determining, the difference no longer has the sense it had ***on its way***, or while it was ***outside its truth***; it can rather come forward only as a determination of this unity, as a moment ***within its own self***, and this unity cannot again dissolve itself into it.

Logic can therefore be divided quite generally into the logic of ***being*** and of ***thinking***, into ***objective*** and ***subjective*** logic.

The ***objective logic*** would, as regards its content, answer in part to what in Kant [^1] is ***tran*** {{{ 3 }}} ***scendental logic***. He marks it off from what he calls general logic, or from what is ordinarily called logic as such, in this way: that the former considers the concepts that refer a priori to objects and thus does not abstract from all content of objective cognition, or that it holds the rules of the pure thinking of an object, and inquires at the same time into the origin of our cognition, insofar as this cognition cannot be ascribed to the objects. — Kant's chief thought is that of vindicating the categories for self-consciousness, as the ***subjective I*** itself. For this reason he still speaks, over and above the empirical, the side of feeling and of intuition, expressly of objects, or of something that is not posited and determined by self-consciousness. Were the category the form of absolute thinking, then no ***thing-in-itself***, nothing foreign and external to thinking, could be left over. When other Kantians have expressed the determining of the object by the I in this way, that the I's objectifying is to be regarded as an original and necessary doing of consciousness, so that within this original doing the representation of the I itself is not yet present, — such a representation being only a consciousness of that same consciousness, or itself an objectifying of it, — then this objectifying doing, once freed from the opposition of consciousness, is more precisely what may be taken quite generally as ***absolute thinking***. But this doing should then no longer be called consciousness, for consciousness includes in itself the opposition of the I and of its object, an opposition not at hand in that original doing; and the name consciousness lends it even more of a shine of subjectivity than does the expression thinking, which here is to be understood throughout in an absolute sense, or, if this should supposedly {{{ 4 }}} be more intelligible, as infinite thinking.

[^1]: I note that I so often take the Kantian philosophy into account in this work, (which to some may seem superfluous) because — however its more precise determinateness, as well as the particular parts of its execution, may be judged elsewhere and in this work too — it makes up the foundation and the point of departure of the more recent philosophy, and this merit of it is left undiminished by whatever objection may be raised against it. There is reason too to consider it frequently, at least in the objective logic, since it goes more closely into important and more determinate aspects of the logical, while later presentations of philosophy have taken little notice of these and have in part often displayed nothing but a crude, — though not unavenged —, contempt for them.

The ***objective logic***, moreover, comprises within itself not merely the determinations of thought belonging to ***immediate being***, but also those of mediated being, the determinations of reflection proper, or the ***doctrine of essence***; namely insofar as essence is not yet the concept itself, but first makes up the territory of reflection as the movement toward the concept, since, coming as it does out of being, it is still a differentiated being-within-itself.

The objective logic thus takes, quite generally, the place of the former ***metaphysics***. First of all immediately the place of ***ontology***, the first part of that metaphysics, which was to set forth the nature of the ***Ens*** in general; — the Ens comprises within itself ***being*** as well as ***essence***, a difference for which our language has luckily rescued distinct expressions. — But then the objective logic also comprises within itself the remaining metaphysics, insofar as this contained the pure forms of thought applied to particular substrates drawn in the first place from representation, the soul, the world, God, and these determinations of thinking made up what was essential in the metaphysical manner of consideration. Logic considers these forms free of those substrates, and considers their nature and worth in and for themselves. That metaphysics left this undone and so drew upon itself the just reproach of having used them without critique, without the prior inquiry whether and how they are fit to be determinations of the thing-in-itself, as Kant expresses it, — or rather of the rational. — The objective logic is therefore the genuine critique of these forms, — a critique {{{ 5 }}} that considers them not merely under the general form of apriority, over against the aposteriori, but considers them themselves in their own particular content.

***The subjective logic*** is the logic of the concept, — of the essence that has sublated its reference to a being, or to its shine, and that in its determination is no longer external, but is the free, self-subsistent subjective, or rather the subject itself.

But since the subjective carries with it the misunderstanding of something contingent and arbitrary, as of determinations generally belonging to the form of consciousness, no special weight is to be laid here upon the difference between subjective and objective, a difference that will be developed more closely later on within logic itself. — Logic does indeed break up, broadly speaking, into objective and subjective logic. More precisely, however, it has the three parts: I. ***the logic of being***; II. ***that of essence*** and III. ***that of the concept***.
