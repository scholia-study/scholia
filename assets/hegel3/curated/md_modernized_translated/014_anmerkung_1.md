## Remark 1

***Nothing*** is what one habitually sets over against ***something***; something, however, is a determinate being, one that marks itself off from other somethings; and so the nothing set over against something, the nothing of some something or other, is a determinate nothing as well. The nothing that must be taken here, however, is the nothing in its indeterminate simplicity; the nothing purely in and for itself. — ***Non-being*** contains the reference to being; it is therefore not the pure nothing, but the nothing as it already is within becoming.

{{{ 24 }}} The simple thought ***of pure being*** was pronounced first by ***Parmenides***, as the absolute and as the sole truth, and in the fragments left to us from him, with the pure exaltation of a thinking that lays hold of itself for the first time in its absolute abstraction: ***being alone is, and the nothing is not at all***. — The profound ***Heraclitus***, in opposition to that simple and one-sided abstraction, held up the higher, total concept of becoming, and said: ***being is just as little as the nothing is***, or again that all things ***flow***, that is, that all is ***becoming***. — The popular sayings, oriental ones especially, that whatever is has within its very birth the germ of its own ceasing-to-be, and that death is inversely the doorway into fresh life, give voice at bottom to this same uniting of being and nothing. Yet these expressions carry a substratum on which the transition takes place; in time being and nothing are held apart from one another, represented as alternating within it, rather than thought in their abstraction, and hence not thought in such a way that in and for themselves they are the same.

Ex nihilo nihil fit — this is one of those propositions to which the metaphysics of former times ascribed great significance. But what is to be seen in it is either only the empty tautology: nothing is nothing; or else, if ***becoming*** were supposed to have any real significance there, then, seeing that out of ***nothing*** only ***nothing*** comes to be, no becoming is in fact at hand in it, for nothing stays nothing. What becoming contains is that nothing shall not stay nothing, but shall pass over into its other, into being. — When the later metaphysics, the Christian above all, threw out the proposition that out of nothing nothing comes, it thereby asserted a transition of nothing into being; however synthetically, or however merely representationally, it took this {{{ 25 }}} proposition, even the most imperfect union still holds within it a point at which being and nothing come together and their distinctness vanishes.

If the result, that being and nothing are the same, strikes one as odd or looks paradoxical, no further heed need be paid to it; what deserves astonishment is rather that astonishment itself, which shows itself as something so new in philosophy and forgets that views of a wholly different kind occur in this science than occur in ordinary consciousness and in so-called common human understanding. It would be no hard matter to exhibit this unity of being and nothing in any example one likes, in anything actual or in any thought. But such an empirical elucidation would at the same time be wholly and utterly superfluous. Since this unity now lies at the ground of everything once and for all, and makes up the element of all that follows, becoming itself apart, all further logical determinations — existence, quality, indeed every concept of philosophy — are examples of this unity.

The confusion into which ordinary consciousness throws itself in the face of such a logical proposition has its ground in this, that it brings along with it representations of some concrete something, and loses sight of the fact that nothing of that sort is under discussion, but rather the pure abstractions alone, those of being and of nothing, and that these are what have to be held fast in and for themselves.

Being and non-being are the same; ***therefore*** it makes no odds whether I am or fail to be, whether this house stands or does not, whether these hundred thalers belong to my financial estate or not. — This inference, or this application of that proposition, alters its sense completely. {{{ 26 }}} What the proposition contains are the pure abstractions of being and nothing; the application, though, turns these into a determinate being and a determinate nothing. But determinate being, as was said, is not what is under discussion here. A determinate being, a finite one, is a being that bears reference to an other; it is a content that stands with other content, with the world entire, in a relation of necessity. With regard to the reciprocally determining interconnection of the whole, metaphysics was able to make the — at bottom tautological — claim that if a single mote of dust were absolutely destroyed, the whole universe would collapse. But take from the determinate content its ***interconnection*** with other things, and represent it in isolation, then its necessity is sublated, and it makes no odds whether this isolated thing, this isolated human being, exists or not. Or again, when this whole interconnection is drawn together into one, the determinate existence that refers itself to an other likewise vanishes, for the universe has no further other, and it is no difference whether it is or is not.

Something appears, then, as not indifferent to whether it be or not be, yet not for the sake of the being or the non-being, but for the sake of its determinateness, of its content, which links it up with other things. If the sphere of ***being*** is presupposed, and within it a determinate content, some determinate existence or other, is assumed, then this existence, because it is ***determinate***, stands in manifold reference to other content; and for it it is by no means indifferent whether some certain other content with which it stands in reference is or is not; for only by way of such reference is it essentially what it is. The same holds in the case of ***representing*** (when we take non-being in the more determinate {{{ 27 }}} sense of representing as against actuality), in whose interconnection the being or the absence of a content that stands determinately in reference to another is not indifferent. — For in general the real difference first begins only with determinateness: indeterminate being and nothing do not yet have it upon them, but only the difference that is merely supposed.

This consideration contains the same as what makes up a main moment in the Kantian critique of the ontological proof of the existence of God; more closely, though, that critique is to be considered only at the opposition of concept and concrete existence. — As is well known, in this so-called proof the concept of a being was presupposed to which all realities accrue, hence concrete existence too, which was likewise assumed to be one of the realities. The Kantian critique fastened above all on this, that ***concrete existence*** is no ***property*** and no ***real predicate***, which is to say, not a concept of anything that could accrue to the concept of a thing. — Kant means by this to say that being is no determination of content. — Hence, he goes on, the possible contains no more than the actual; a hundred actual thalers contain not the least bit more than a hundred possible ones; — namely, the former have no other determination of content than the latter. For this content considered as isolated it is indifferent to be or not to be; there lies in it no difference of being or non-being, this difference does not touch it at all; the hundred thalers do not become fewer if they are not, and not [m]ore if they are. The difference must first come from […]elsewhere. — “On the other hand, Kant recalls, in my financial estate there is more with a hundred actual thalers {{{ 28 }}} than with the mere concept of them, or with their possibility. For where actuality is concerned the object is not merely contained analytically within my concept, but accrues to my concept, (that concept being a ***determination*** of my ***state***,) ***synthetically***, without these hundred thalers as thought being in the least augmented by this being outside my concept.”

Two sorts of states are here presupposed, to keep to the Kantian expressions, the pure one, which Kant calls the concept, by which representation is to be understood, and another, the financial estate. For the one as for the other a hundred thalers are a further determination of content, or they accrue, as Kant expresses it, synthetically; and I as ***possessor*** of a hundred thalers, or as non-possessor of them, or again I as ***representing*** a hundred thalers to myself or not representing them, is a different content. On the one hand it makes a difference whether I merely represent these hundred thalers to myself or possess them, whether they are therefore to be found in the one state or in the other, because I have once and for all presupposed these two states as different determinations. On the other hand, taking each of these states by itself, within it they are a particular determination of content, one that enters into reference to something else, and whose vanishing is not a mere non-being but makes up an ***otherness***. It is a deception that we push the difference off merely onto being and non-being, onto whether I ***have*** the hundred thalers or ***do not have*** them. This deception rests upon the one-sided abstraction which drops the ***determinate existence*** that is always at hand in such examples and clings merely to being and non-being. As was recalled just now, {{{ 29 }}} it is ***existence*** that first is the real difference of being and nothing, a ***something*** and an ***other***. — It is this real difference, that of something and an other, which hovers before representation, in place of pure being and pure nothing.

As Kant puts it, through concrete existence something enters the context of experience as a whole; we get by this one more object of perception, yet our concept of the object gains nothing thereby. — This means, as follows from what has been elucidated, in fact this much: through concrete existence, essentially because something is determinate concrete existence, it enters into interconnection ***with an other***, or stands within it, and among other things with something perceiving. — The concept of the hundred thalers, says Kant, is not augmented by perceiving. — The concept here means the hundred thalers as represented in isolation, outside the context of experience and of perceiving. In this isolated fashion they are indeed a determination of content, and a very empirical one at that, but a severed one, without interconnection and without determinateness over against ***an other***; the form of identity with self, of the simple determinateness that refers only to itself, lifts them above the reference to an other and leaves them indifferent as to whether they be perceived or not. But if they are genuinely considered as determinate and as referred to an other, and the form of simple self-reference, which does not belong to such a determinate content, is taken from them, then they are no longer indifferent to existence and non-existence, but have entered the sphere in which the difference of being and non-being holds, not indeed as such, but as the difference of ***something*** and an ***other***.

{{{ 30 }}} The thinking, or rather the representing, before which only a determinate being, or existence, hovers, into which the ***real*** diversity of being and nothing falls, is to be referred back to that beginning of pure science which Parmenides made, who among human beings appears to have been the first to purify and raise his representing, and with it the representing of the time that followed, into the pure thought of being, and thereby to have created the element of science.

But it must be recalled, to return to the main matter, that the expression of the result which issues from the consideration of being and of nothing, through the proposition: ***being and nothing are one and the same***, is imperfect. For the accent is laid preferentially on their being ***one and the same***, and the sense therefore seems to be that the difference is denied, which nevertheless occurs immediately in the proposition itself at the same time; for the proposition enunciates the two determinations, being and nothing, and holds them within it as distinguished. — Nor can it be meant, at the same time, that one should abstract from them and cling only to the unity. Such a sense would give itself out as one-sided, since what is to be abstracted from is nonetheless present in the proposition. — Insofar as the proposition: ***being and nothing are the same***, enunciates the identity of these determinations, but in fact contains them equally as distinguished, it contradicts itself in itself and dissolves. A proposition is therefore posited here which, more closely considered, has the movement of vanishing through itself. With that there happens to it just what is supposed to make up its proper content, namely becoming.

{{{ 31 }}} The proposition accordingly contains the result, it is ***in itself*** the result itself; but the result is not ***expressed*** in it in its truth; it is an external reflection that recognizes the result in it. — The proposition, in the form of a judgment, is in general not immediately suited to express speculative truths. The judgment is an ***identical*** reference between subject and predicate; even if the subject has still more determinatenesses than that of the predicate, and to that extent is something other than the predicate, these accrue only as added on, and do not sublate the identical reference of this predicate with its subject, which remains its ground and bearer. But if the content is speculative, then the non-identical element of subject and predicate is likewise an essential moment, and the transition or the vanishing of the first into the other is their reference. That paradoxical and bizarre light in which so much of more recent philosophy shows itself to anyone not conversant with speculative thinking falls in many cases upon the form of the simple judgment, whenever that form is used for expressing speculative results.

The genuine result that has emerged here is ***becoming***, which is not merely the abstract or one-sided unity of being and of nothing. Rather, it consists in this movement: that the pure being is immediate and simple, that for that very reason it is just as much the pure nothing, that the difference of the two is, yet just as much sublates itself and is not. The result therefore asserts the difference of being and of nothing just as much, but as a merely ***supposed*** one. — One supposes that being is rather the simply other over against what nothing is, and that nothing is more evident than their absolute difference, and nothing seems easier than to specify it. But one may just as easily {{{ 32 }}} convince oneself that this is impossible. For if being and nothing possessed any determinateness whatever by which they were distinguished, then, as was recalled earlier, what they would be is a determinate being and a determinate nothing, not that pure being and pure nothing which they still are here. Their difference is therefore entirely empty, each of the two is in like manner the indeterminate; it consequently does not subsist on them themselves, but only in a third, in supposing. But supposing is a form of the subjective, which does not belong in this series of the exposition. The third, however, in which being and nothing have their subsistence, must occur here too; and it has occurred, it is becoming. In it they are as distinguished ones; becoming is only insofar as they are diverse. This third is an other than they are; — they subsist only in an other, which means equally that they do not subsist for themselves. Becoming is the subsistence of being quite as much as of non-being; or their subsistence is nothing other than their being in ***one***; and it is precisely this subsistence of theirs that just as much sublates their difference.

One is also apt to represent being under the image, say, of light in its purity, as the clearness of undimmed seeing, but nothing as pure night, and to tie their difference to this well-known sensuous diversity. In fact, however, if one represents this seeing to oneself more precisely, it is easily grasped that one sees as much and as little in absolute clarity as in absolute darkness, that the one seeing is quite as much as the other a pure seeing, a seeing of nothing. Light in its purity and darkness in its purity are two vacuities, and these are the same. Only in determinate light — and light is determined by darkness, — hence in dimmed light, and equally only in determinate darkness, — and darkness is {{{ 33 }}} determined by light, — in illuminated darkness, can something be distinguished; because only dimmed light and illuminated darkness have the difference upon themselves, and are thereby determinate being, existence.
