## {{{ III }}} Preface

The complete transformation which philosophical thinking has undergone among us over the last twenty-five years or so, the higher standpoint that the self-consciousness of spirit has attained over itself in this period, has so far had little influence on the shape of ***logic***.

What bore the name of ***metaphysics*** before this period has been eradicated, so to speak, stem and stump, and has dropped out of the roster of the sciences. In what quarter do the tones of the erstwhile ontology, of rational psychology, of cosmology, or indeed of the erstwhile natural theology, still get leave, or still have leave, to make themselves audible? Investigations into the soul's immateriality, for instance, or into mechanical causes and final ones: what interest could they still be expected to arouse? the remaining proofs of the existence of God, too, are adduced only historically, or for the purposes of edification and the elevation of the heart. It is a fact that interest has been lost partly in the content, partly in the form of the former metaphysics, partly in both at once. Remarkable as it is {{{ IV }}} when a people finds, say, the science of its own constitutional law, when it finds its convictions, its ethical habits and virtues, become useless to it, at least as remarkable is it when a people loses its metaphysics, when the spirit that occupies itself with its own pure essence has no actual existence in that people any more.

The exoteric doctrine of the Kantian philosophy, — that the ***understanding may not fly beyond experience***, since otherwise the faculty of cognition becomes ***theoretical reason***, which of itself gives birth to nothing but ***chimeras***, has justified from the side of science the renunciation of speculative thinking. This popular doctrine was met halfway by the clamor of modern pedagogy, by the distress of the times which fixes its gaze on immediate need, the clamor, namely, that just as for cognition experience is what comes first, so for competence in public and in private life theoretical insight is even harmful, while practice and practical training generally are the essential thing, the only thing that furthers us. — Since science and common human understanding thus worked into each other's hands to bring about the downfall of metaphysics, there seemed to be staged the odd spectacle of ***a cultivated people without metaphysics***; — like a temple otherwise adorned in every variety but without a holy of holies. — Theology, which in earlier times was the keeper of the speculative mysteries {{{ V }}} and of a metaphysics that was, to be sure, dependent upon it, had surrendered it in exchange for feelings, for what is practically popular, and for learned history. Corresponding to which change is the fact that elsewhere those ***solitaries***, who were sacrificed by their people and set apart from the world so that the contemplation of the eternal, and life serving it alone, should be present, not for the sake of any use but for the sake of the blessing, — disappeared; a disappearance which, in another connection, may be regarded as essentially the same phenomenon as the one just mentioned. — So that, once these darknesses had been driven off, the colourless occupation of the inwardly turned spirit with itself, existence seemed to have been transformed into the cheerful world of flowers, among which, as everyone knows, there is no ***black*** one.

Things have not gone quite so badly for ***logic*** as for metaphysics. That through it one ***learns to think***, which formerly counted as its use and thereby as its purpose, — as though one first had to learn to digest and to move about through the study of anatomy and physiology —, this prejudice has long since faded away, and the spirit of practicality no doubt intended no better a fate for it. Notwithstanding this, and probably for the sake of some formal usefulness, logic was still left a rank among the sciences, indeed it was even retained as a subject of public instruction. This {{{ VI }}} better lot, however, concerns only its outer fortune; for its shape and content have remained the same as they were handed down through a long tradition, though in that transmission they had been thinned out and starved more and more; the new spirit that has dawned upon science no less than upon actuality has not yet made itself felt in logic. But it is once and for all in vain, when the substantial form of spirit has reshaped itself, to want to keep the forms of an earlier culture; they are withered leaves, pushed off by the new buds that are already being generated at their roots.

The ***ignoring*** of the general change is by now, in the scientific sphere too, beginning to run out. Unnoticed, even the opponents have grown familiar with the other ways of representing things and made them their own, and though they persist in acting coy toward their source and their principles and conduct themselves contradictorily toward them, they have had to put up with the consequences all the same and have not been able to fend off their influence; for their ever more insignificant negative stance they know no other way of gaining a positive importance and a content than by joining in the talk of the new ways of representing things.

From the other side, the time of ferment with which a new creation begins seems to be over. In its first appearance such a creation is apt {{{ VII }}} to behave with fanatical hostility toward the far-flung systematizing of the earlier principle; apt in part also to be fearful, losing itself in the extension of the particular, and in part to shy away from the labor that scientific elaboration requires and, in the need for it, to reach first for an empty formalism. What is demanded now, with so much the more urgency, is that the material be worked up and elaborated. An age, no less than an individual, passes through a phase of formation in which everything turns chiefly on acquiring the principle and holding fast to it in its still undeveloped intensity. What is required at the higher level, however, is that the principle should turn into science.

Whatever may already have been done in other respects for the substance and for the form of science, logical science, which makes up metaphysics proper or pure speculative philosophy, has hitherto still found itself greatly neglected. What I understand more precisely by this science and by its standpoint I have indicated provisionally in the ***Introduction***. Equitable judges will, I hope, take into account that this science had once again to be begun over from scratch, that the subject matter is of the kind it is, and that no preliminary studies lay ready that might have been drawn upon, even though the labor of many years was unable to lend this attempt any higher degree of completeness. — Seen from the vantage point that is essential, what is at stake throughout {{{ VIII }}} is a new concept of scientific treatment. Given that philosophy is to be science, it cannot, as I have recalled in another place, take over for this purpose a method on loan from some science ranked beneath it, mathematics for example; just as little may it settle for categorical assurances of an inner intuition, or avail itself of ratiocination proceeding from grounds supplied by external reflection. Rather, it can only be ***the nature of the content*** ***which*** in scientific cognition ***moves*** itself, since at the same time it is this ***own reflection*** of the content ***which*** first posits and ***generates its determination*** itself.

The ***understanding determines*** and holds its determinations fast; ***reason*** is negative and ***dialectical***, because it resolves the understanding's determinations into nothing; it is ***positive***, because it generates the ***universal*** and subsumes the particular under it. Just as the understanding is taken as something separate from reason in general, so too dialectical reason is apt to be taken as something separate from positive reason. But in its truth reason is ***spirit***, which is higher than both, the reason that understands, or the understanding that reasons. It is the negative, that which makes up the quality both of dialectical reason and of the understanding; — it negates the simple, and so posits the determinate difference of the understanding, and it just as much dissolves that difference, and so is dialectical. {{{ IX }}} It does not, however, hold to the nothing of this result, but is therein just as much positive, and has thereby restored the first simple, but as a universal; a given particular is not subsumed under this universal, rather in that determining and in the dissolution of it the particular has already determined itself along with it. This movement of spirit, which in its very simplicity confers determinateness upon itself and, within that determinateness, confers upon itself its own self-sameness, and which accordingly constitutes the immanent unfolding of the concept, is cognition's absolute method and at once the soul immanent in the content itself. — Only on this self-constructing path, I maintain, is philosophy capable of being objective, demonstrated science. — In this manner I have attempted to present ***consciousness***, in the ***Phenomenology of Spirit***. Consciousness is spirit as a concrete object; but its onward movement rests solely, like the development of all natural and spiritual life, on the nature of the ***pure essentialities*** that make up the content of logic. Consciousness, as spirit in its appearing, which on its way frees itself from its immediacy and its concretion, becomes pure knowledge, which has those pure essentialities themselves, as they are in and for themselves, as its object. They are the pure thoughts, spirit thinking its own essence. Their self-movement is their spiritual life, and it is that through which science constitutes itself and of which science is the exposition.

{{{ X }}} The reference of the science that I call the ***Phenomenology of Spirit*** to ***logic*** is herewith indicated. — As regards the external relation, the first part ***of the System of Science*** (Bamberg and Würzburg, at Göbhard's, 1807.), the part that contains the phenomenology, was designated to have a second part follow it, one that was to contain the logic together with philosophy's two real sciences, that of nature and that of spirit, and that would have brought the system of science to its close. But the necessary extent that the logic had to acquire in its own right has led me to let it come out on its own; within an expanded plan it therefore makes up the first sequel to the Phenomenology of Spirit. Later I shall let the elaboration of philosophy's two real sciences just named follow. — This first volume of the logic, however, contains as its first book ***the doctrine of being***; the second book, ***the doctrine of essence***, as the second section of the first volume, is already in the press; the second volume, on the other hand, will contain the ***subjective logic***, or the ***doctrine of the concept***.

Nuremberg, 22 March 1812.
