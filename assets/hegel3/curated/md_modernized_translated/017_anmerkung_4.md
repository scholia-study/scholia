## Remark 4

It emerges from what has gone before what the case is with the ***ordinary dialectic*** against {{{ 41 }}} ***becoming***, or against beginning and going under, coming-to-be or ceasing-to-be. — Kant's antinomy over whether the world is bounded or unbounded in space and in time comes up for closer examination further on, under the concept of infinity. — That simple and customary dialectic has its basis in clinging to the opposedness of being and of nothing. The proof that no beginning, whether of the world or of a something, is possible runs as follows:

Nothing can begin, neither insofar as something is nor insofar as it is not; for insofar as it is, it is not just then beginning; but insofar as it is not, it does not begin either. — If the world or something were to have begun, it would have begun in nothing, but in nothing, or the nothing, is not a beginning; for beginning includes a being within itself, whereas the nothing contains no being. — For the same reason something also cannot cease. For then being would have to contain the nothing, but being is only being, not the opposite of itself.

Becoming, however, or beginning and ceasing, is precisely this ***unity*** of being and nothing, against which this dialectic brings forward nothing except that it assertorically denies the unity and ascribes truth to being and to nothing, each separated from the other. — To ordinary reflecting representation it counts as complete truth that being and nothing are not one; but on the other side it lets beginning and ceasing count as just as genuine determinations; yet in these it does in fact take a unity of being and nothing to be genuine.

Since the absolute severance of being from nothing is presupposed, then — as one so often hears — {{{ 42 }}} beginning or becoming is indeed something incomprehensible; for one makes a presupposition which sublates the beginning or the becoming that one nonetheless also concedes.

What has just been cited is the same dialectic which the understanding turns against the concept that higher analysis holds of ***infinitely small magnitudes***. The phrase ***infinitely small*** is, for that matter, an awkward one, and the concept receives fuller treatment further on. — Magnitudes of this kind have received the determination of being ones that ***are in the act of vanishing***, neither ***prior to*** that vanishing, since at that point they would still be finite magnitudes; — nor ***subsequent to*** it, since at that point they would be nothing at all. Against this pure concept the well-known objection has been raised, and endlessly reiterated, that magnitudes of this kind must be ***either*** a something ***or*** else nothing; that no intermediary state (state is here an inept, barbarous term) exists between being and non-being. — What underlies this too is the assumption that being and nothing stand absolutely apart. The showing has gone the other way, however: that the two coincide in fact, or, to put it in the idiom just used, that there ***is*** simply nothing which fails to be a becoming, which fails to be an ***intermediary state between being*** and ***nothing***.

Since the reasoning adduced makes the false presupposition of the absolute separatedness of being and non-being, it is also to be called not ***dialectic*** but ***sophistry***; for sophistry is a reasoning from a groundless presupposition which one lets count uncritically and without reflection; by dialectic, however, we mean the higher rational movement into which such utterly separate-seeming terms pass over through themselves {{{ 43 }}} and therein into one another. It is the dialectical nature of being and nothing themselves that they exhibit their unity, becoming, as their truth.
