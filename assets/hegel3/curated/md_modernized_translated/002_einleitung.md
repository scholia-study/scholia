## {{{ I }}} Introduction

In no science is the need felt more strongly to begin from the subject matter itself, without any preceding reflections, than in the science of logic. In every other science the object it treats and the scientific method are distinguished from each other; and its content, too, does not make an absolute beginning but depends on other concepts and hangs together on all sides with other material. These sciences are therefore granted leave to speak of their ground and its connections, and likewise of method, in lemmatic fashion, to apply without further ado the forms of definition and the like that are presupposed as known and accepted, and to make use of the ordinary manner of argumentation in fixing their general concepts and fundamental determinations.

Logic, by contrast, cannot presuppose any of these forms of reflection, or rules and laws of thinking, for they constitute a part of its content and first have to be grounded within it. {{{ II }}} The concept itself of science as such, and not merely of scientific method, belongs to its content, and indeed constitutes its final result; what it is, therefore, it cannot say in advance, but rather its entire treatise brings forth this knowledge of itself only as its last word and as its completion. Likewise its object, thinking or more precisely conceptual thinking, is essentially dealt with within it; the concept of thinking generates itself in the course of it, and therefore cannot be sent on ahead. What is sent on ahead in this introduction, then, is not meant to establish the concept of logic or to vindicate beforehand and scientifically its content and its method; the intent is rather, by way of a few clarifications and reflections offered in a ratiocinative and historical mode, to bring nearer to representation that vantage point out of which this science has to be viewed.

To accept logic as the science of thinking in general carries with it the understanding that such thinking amounts to ***the mere form*** of a cognition, that logic leaves all content out of account, that the so-called second ***constituent*** belonging to a cognition, the matter, has to be supplied from some other quarter, and hence that logic, a science from which this matter is utterly independent, can do no more than state the formal conditions of true cognition, while real truth itself it neither contains nor even furnishes the ***road*** to, since {{{ III }}} the very thing that makes truth truth, namely the content, falls outside it.

To begin with, there is already something inept in the claim that logic leaves every content aside, that its teaching covers only the rules of thinking, incapable of going into what gets thought or of heeding how the latter is constituted. For thinking, along with the rules of thinking, is precisely what is meant to be its object, and in that object logic already possesses, without mediation, a content all its own; there too it possesses that second constituent of cognition, a matter whose constitution is very much its concern.

But secondly, the representations on which the concept of logic has rested up to now have in part already perished, and in part it is time that they should vanish altogether, that the standpoint of this science should be grasped at a higher level, and that it should gain a completely altered shape.

Ordinary consciousness takes for granted, once and for all, a separation between the content of cognition and its form, between truth and certainty, and it is on this separation that logic's concept has rested until now. It is presupposed, ***first***, that the material of cognition is present in and for itself as a finished world outside thinking, that thinking for itself is empty, that it comes to that matter externally as a form, fills itself with it, only thereby gains a content and becomes a real cognizing.

These two components then stand toward each other, — (for they are supposed to have the relation of components, and {{{ IV }}} cognition is compounded out of them mechanically or at most chemically —) in the following order of rank: that the object is something complete and finished for itself, something that can entirely dispense with thinking for its actuality, whereas thinking is something deficient which has first to complete itself on some material and, as a pliable indeterminate form, has to make itself adequate to its matter. Truth is the agreement of thinking with the object, and in order to bring this agreement about, — for it is not present in and for itself, — thinking is supposed to yield and accommodate itself to the object.

Third, inasmuch as the diversity of matter and form, of object and thinking, is not left in that misty indeterminacy but is taken more determinately, each is a sphere severed from the other. In its receiving and forming of the material, therefore, thinking does not get beyond itself; its receiving and its accommodating itself to the material remain a modification of itself, and it does not thereby become its own other; moreover, self-conscious determining belongs to it alone in any case; and so, even in its reference to the object, it does not come out of itself over to the object, which remains a thing in itself, simply a beyond of thinking.

What such opinions about how subject and object stand to one another put into words are the determinations of that relation which make up the nature of our everyday consciousness, {{{ V }}} consciousness as it appears; yet once these preconceptions are transferred to reason, as though the very same relation held good there, as though that relation possessed truth in and for itself, they turn into the errors whose refutation, carried through every region of the spiritual and the natural universe, is philosophy itself, or rather, since they block the doorway into philosophy, errors to be discarded before one enters.

In this respect the older metaphysics had a higher concept of thinking than has become current in more recent times. For it laid down as its basis that what is cognized through thinking of and in things is alone what is genuinely true in them; hence not things in their immediacy, but things first raised into the form of thinking, as things thought. On this metaphysics' reckoning, then, neither thinking nor the determinations of thinking stand foreign to objects; they are rather what those objects essentially are, so that things and the thinking of them, — (as our language, too, expresses a kinship between them, —) tally in and for themselves, thinking in its immanent determinations and the genuine nature of things making up one and the same content.

But once plain common sense had taken possession of philosophy, it pressed its own view: that truth rests upon sensuous reality, that thoughts are ***merely*** thoughts, in the sense that substance and reality accrue to them only from sensuous perception, and that reason, so long as it {{{ VI }}} stays in and for itself, brings forth nothing but figments of the brain. In this renunciation by reason of itself the concept of truth has been lost; reason has restricted itself to cognizing only subjective truth, only appearance, only something to which the nature of the subject matter itself does not correspond; ***knowledge*** has sunk back into ***opinion***.

But this turn which cognition has taken, and which appears as a loss and a step backwards, has at its basis something deeper, on which the elevation of reason into the higher spirit of modern philosophy rests altogether. For the ground of that representation, now become general, is to be sought in the insight into how the determinations of the understanding necessarily conflict with themselves. — Reflection goes beyond the concrete immediate, and separates it by determining it. But it must just as much go beyond these its separating determinations, and in the first instance relate them. At the standpoint of this relating their conflict comes to the fore. This relating of reflection belongs to reason; the elevation above those determinations, which arrives at the insight into their conflict, is the great negative step toward the true concept of reason. But insight not carried through falls into the misunderstanding that it is reason which lands in contradiction with itself; it fails to recognize that the contradiction is precisely the raising of reason above the limitations of the understanding and the dissolving of them. Instead of taking the last {{{ VII }}} step upward from here, cognition has fled back from the unsatisfactoriness of the determinations of the understanding to sensuous actuality, supposing that in it it has what is firm and self-consistent. But since on the other side this cognition knows itself as cognition only of what appears, its unsatisfactoriness is conceded, yet at the same time it is presupposed that although things in themselves are not correctly cognized, still within the sphere of appearance there is correct cognizing; as though it were, as it were, only the kind of cognized objects that differed, and indeed as though the one kind, namely things in themselves, did not fall within cognition, while the other kind, namely appearances, did. It is as if correct insight were attributed to a man, with the addition that he is nevertheless capable of having insight into nothing true, but only into what is untrue. As preposterous as the latter would be, so preposterous is a true cognition that would not cognize its object such as it is in itself.

The result adduced above from the ***critique of the forms of the understanding*** was that these forms have no ***application to things in themselves***. — This can mean nothing else than that these forms are in their own selves something untrue. Yet inasmuch as they are allowed to go on holding good for subjective reason and for experience, the critique has effected no change in them themselves, but leaves them for the subject in the same shape in which they otherwise held good for the object. Should they not suffice for the thing in itself, then the understanding they are said {{{ VIII }}} to belong to has all the less reason to tolerate them or make do with them. Determinations of the thing in itself they cannot be; determinations of the understanding they can be still less, given that the understanding ought at any rate to be granted the dignity of a thing in itself. The determinations of the finite and the infinite stand in the same conflict, be it that they are applied to time and space, to the world, or that they are determinations within spirit; just as black and white give a grey whether they are brought together on a wall or while still on the palette; if our representation of the world dissolves once the determinations of the infinite and the finite are transferred onto it, then spirit itself, which contains them both within it, is all the more something contradicting itself, something dissolving itself. — It is not the constitution of the material or of the object, to which they would be applied or in which they would be found, that can make a difference; for the object has the contradiction in it only through and according to those determinations.

That critique has therefore only removed the forms of objective thinking from the thing, but left them in the subject just as it found them. For in doing so it did not consider these forms in and for themselves, according to their own peculiar content, but simply took them over lemmatically from subjective logic; so that there was no question of a derivation of them in their own selves, or of a derivation of the subjective-logical forms, still less {{{ IX }}} of a dialectical consideration of them.

Transcendental idealism, carried through more consistently, recognized the nullity of the spectre of the ***thing-in-itself*** still left over by the critical philosophy, that abstract shadow cut off from all content, and had the purpose of destroying it completely. This philosophy also made the beginning of letting reason exhibit its determinations out of itself. But the subjective posture of this attempt did not let it come to completion. Furthermore, along with this posture that beginning toward the development of pure science was abandoned as well.

What is commonly comprehended under logic, however, is considered entirely without regard to metaphysical significance. This science, in the condition in which it still finds itself, has of course no content of the sort that counts as reality and as a genuine subject matter in ordinary consciousness. But it is not for this reason a formal science, lacking truth rich in content. In that material which is missed in it, and to whose lack its unsatisfactoriness is usually ascribed, the domain of truth is in any case not to be sought. Rather, the emptiness of the logical forms lies solely in the manner of considering and treating them. For inasmuch as they fall apart as fixed determinations and are not held together in organic unity, they are dead forms, and do not have dwelling {{{ X }}} within them the spirit that would constitute the living concrete unity. But with that they lack solid content, a matter that would be substance in its own self. For the content that is missed in the logical forms is nothing other than a firm foundation and concretion of the abstract determinations; and such a substantial essence is customarily sought on the outside. But reason itself is the substantial or the real, which holds all abstract determinations together within itself and is their solid, absolutely concrete unity. One need not, then, go hunting far afield for whatever tends to get called a matter; should logic be devoid of substance, the blame lies not with logic's object but purely with the way that object is grasped.

This vantage point leads me nearer to the view according to which I hold that logic is to be considered, in what respect that view differs from the manner in which this science has been treated hitherto, and to the sole genuine standpoint on which logic is henceforth to be placed for good.

In the ***Phenomenology of Spirit*** (Bamberg and Würzburg 1807) I presented consciousness in its forward movement from the first immediate opposition of itself and the object up to absolute knowledge. Every form assumed by the relation of consciousness to the object lies along this road, and what issues from it is the ***concept of science***. This concept therefore requires no {{{ XI }}} justification here (apart from the fact that it emerges within logic itself), because it has received one there; and it is capable of no other justification than just this bringing forth of it by consciousness, for which all its shapes dissolve into this concept as into their truth. — A ratiocinative grounding or clarification of the concept of science can at the very most achieve this much, that the concept be set before representation and a historical familiarity with it be brought about; but a definition of science, or more narrowly of logic, has its proof nowhere but in that necessity with which it comes forth. A definition with which some science makes an absolute beginning can contain nothing other than the determinate, correct expression of what, ***by general admission and acknowledgment***, one represents to oneself as the object and purpose of the science. That one represents precisely this to oneself under it is a historical assurance, in regard to which one can only appeal to this or that recognized view, or, properly speaking, can only plead that one might let this and that pass as recognized. There is no end to it: one adduces from here, another from there, a case and instance according to which something more and other still is to be understood by this or that expression, so that a closer or a more general determination has to be taken up into its definition and the science arranged accordingly. — Furthermore, it depends here on argumentation what all is to be drawn in or excluded and up to what limit and extent; but argumentation itself lies open to the {{{ XII }}} most manifold and most varied opinions, on which in the end arbitrary choice alone can settle a fixed determination. In this procedure of beginning the science with its definition, however, there cannot even be any talk of the ***necessity*** of its object, and thereby of itself, being exhibited.

Here, accordingly, the concept of pure science together with its deduction counts as presupposed, to the extent that the Phenomenology of Spirit amounts to precisely that deduction and nothing besides. Every mode of consciousness has its truth in absolute knowledge, since, as the course consciousness ran brought forth, nowhere but in absolute knowledge has the separation of the object from certainty of itself come wholly undone, truth having turned equal to that certainty and that certainty equal to truth.

Pure science thus presupposes liberation from the opposition of consciousness. It contains ***the thought, insofar as it is just as much the subject matter in its own self***, or the subject matter in its own self, insofar as it is just as much pure thought. Or the concept of science is that truth is pure self-consciousness and has the shape of the self, in that ***what is in itself is the concept***, and ***the concept is what is in itself***.

This objective thinking, then, is the ***content*** of pure science. So little, therefore, is it formal, so little {{{ XIII }}} does it want for the matter that an actual and true cognition demands, that its content is on the contrary the absolutely true and nothing else, or, should one still care to employ the word matter, the genuine matter, — a matter, however, to which form is nothing external, since this matter is rather pure thought and therewith absolute form itself. Logic is accordingly to be grasped as pure reason's system, as the realm of pure thought. This realm is truth itself, such as it is in and for itself without any covering; one may therefore put it this way, that the portrayal of God is what this content is, God such as he is in his eternal essence, prior to the creation of nature and of a finite spirit.

***Anaxagoras*** is extolled as the one who first pronounced the thought that the ***nous, thought***, is the world's principle, that the world's essence is to be determined as thought. With this he laid the ground for an intellectual view of the world, whose pure shape must be ***logic***. What is at issue in logic is not a thinking about something that would lie at its base for itself outside thinking, not forms that would yield mere marks of truth; rather, the necessary forms and the proper determinations of thinking are the highest truth itself.

But in order at least to take this up into representation, the opinion must be set aside that truth has to be something palpable. One must also give up, for example, the peculiar way of grasping the {{{ XIV }}} Platonic ideas that are in God's thinking, namely as though they were, so to speak, existing things, but in another world or region, outside which the world of actuality is to be found, possessing a substantiality distinct from those ideas and real only through this distinctness. The Platonic idea is nothing other than the universal, or more precisely the concept of the object; a something has actuality only in its concept; insofar as it is distinct from its concept, it ceases to be actual and is something null; the side of palpability and of sensuous being-outside-itself belongs to this null side. — From the other side, however, one can appeal to ordinary logic's own representations; for it is assumed that definitions, for example, do not contain determinations that fall only within the cognizing subject, but the determinations of the object, which constitute its most essential, its ownmost nature. Or when other determinations are inferred from determinations already given, it is assumed that what has been inferred is not something external and foreign to the object, but rather belongs to the object itself essentially, that being corresponds to such thinking. — It underlies the use of the forms of concept, judgment, syllogism, definition, division and so forth quite generally that these are not merely forms of self-conscious thinking, but also of objective understanding. — ***Thinking*** is an expression that attributes the determination contained in it preeminently to consciousness. {{{ XV }}} But insofar as it is said that ***understanding***, that ***reason is in the world of objects***, that spirit and nature have laws according to which their life and their alterations take place, it is thereby conceded that the determinations of thinking have objective worth and concrete existence just as much.

The critical philosophy did indeed already turn ***metaphysics*** into ***logic***, but it, like the later idealism, out of dread before the object, as was already recalled a moment ago, at the same time gave the logical determinations an essentially subjective significance, by which they remained encumbered with the very object they were fleeing, and left over for themselves a thing-in-itself, an infinite impetus, as a beyond. But that liberation from the opposition of consciousness which science must be able to presuppose raises science above this timid, unfinished standpoint, and demands the consideration of the forms of thinking as they are in and for themselves, without any such limitation and deference, the logical, the purely rational.

Kant elsewhere counts logic, namely the aggregate of determinations and propositions that in the ordinary sense goes by the name of logic, fortunate in that so early a completion has fallen to its lot in advance of other sciences; since Aristotle, he says, it has taken no step backward, but no step forward either, the latter because to all appearances it seems to be closed and complete. — If logic has undergone no change since Aristotle, — as indeed, {{{ XVI }}} in fact, the changes consist almost entirely in nothing but omissions — then what should rather be inferred from this is that it stands all the more in need of a total reworking; for two thousand years of continued labour by spirit must have procured for it a higher consciousness of its thinking and of its pure essentiality within itself. Compare the shapes to which the spirit of the world and the spirit of science have raised themselves in every kind of real and ideal consciousness with the shape that logic, spirit's own consciousness of its pure essence, currently wears, and the difference proves too great for even the most superficial consideration to miss at once that this latter consciousness is utterly inadequate to those earlier elevations and unworthy of them.

The need for a reshaping of logic has in fact long been felt. In the form and the content in which it presents itself in the textbooks, it has, one may say, fallen into contempt. It is still dragged along more from a feeling that a logic of some kind cannot be done without, and from a still persisting habituation to the tradition of its importance, than from any conviction that that ordinary content and the occupation with those empty forms have value and use.

The expansions given to it for a time by psychological, pedagogical and even physiological material have since been fairly generally acknowledged to be disfigurements. In and for {{{ XVII }}} itself a large part of these psychological, pedagogical, physiological observations, laws and rules must appear very stale and trivial, whether they stand in logic or wherever else. Above all, such rules as, for example, that one should think through and examine what one reads in books or hears spoken; that if one does not see clearly one should come to the aid of one's eyes with spectacles — rules which the textbooks of so-called applied logic gave, and indeed in all seriousness divided into paragraphs, so that one might attain truth, — must strike everyone as superfluous, except at most the writer or the teacher who is at a loss how to pad out by something or other the otherwise too short and lifeless content of logic [^1].

[^1]: A newest treatment of this science that has only just appeared, „***System of Logic by Fries***,“ returns to anthropological foundations. The shallowness of the representation or opinion lying at its base, taken in and for itself, and of the execution, spares me the trouble of taking any account whatever of this insignificant appearance.

As for this content itself, the ground has already been given above for why it is so devoid of spirit. Its determinations hold good unbudged in their fixity, and are brought into merely external reference with one another. Since with judgments and syllogisms the operations are chiefly traced back to, and founded upon, the quantitative side {{{ XVIII }}} of the determinations, everything comes to rest upon an external difference, upon mere comparison, and becomes a wholly analytic procedure and a conceptless calculating. The derivation of the so-called rules and laws, above all of inferring, is not much better than a fingering of little sticks of unequal length in order to sort and join them according to their size, — than the children's pastime of picking out from pictures cut up in various ways the pieces that fit. — Hence this thinking has not unjustly been equated with reckoning, and reckoning in turn with this thinking. In arithmetic numbers are taken as the conceptless, which apart from its equality or inequality, that is, apart from its wholly external ratio, has no significance; which is a thought neither in its own self nor in its reference. When it is worked out in a mechanical way that three-quarters multiplied by two-thirds makes one-half, this operation contains roughly as much and as little thought as the calculation whether this or that kind of syllogism can occur in a given figure.

Besides the fact that logic has to receive spirit into its dead content, its ***method*** must be the one through which alone it is capable of being pure science. In the condition in which it finds itself, hardly an inkling of scientific method is to be discerned. It has roughly the form of an empirical science. Empirical sciences have {{{ XIX }}} found, for what they are supposed to be, their own peculiar method of defining and classifying their material as well as this can be done. Pure mathematics likewise has its method, one fitted to its abstract objects and to the quantitative determination that is the sole respect under which it regards them. What is essential on the score of this method, and generally on the subordinate rank of the scientific character attainable in mathematics, I said in the preface to the Phenomenology of Spirit; the method will nevertheless come up for closer consideration inside logic itself. Spinoza, Wolff and others let themselves be seduced into applying it to philosophy as well, and into making the external course of conceptless quantity into the course of the concept, which is in and for itself contradictory. Philosophy has hitherto not yet found its method; it looked with envy at the systematic edifice of mathematics and borrowed the method from it, as was said, or made do with the method of sciences that are only mixtures of given material, empirical propositions and thoughts, — or helped itself out by the crude discarding of all method. The closer account of what alone can serve as the true method of philosophical science belongs to the treatment of logic itself; for the method is consciousness of the form taken by its inner self-movement. In the Phenomenology of Spirit I set out an example of this method on a more concrete object, on consciousness. There are here shapes of consciousness, {{{ XX }}} each of which in its realization at the same time dissolves itself, has its own negation for its result, — and has thereby passed over into a higher shape. The one thing needed in order to gain scientific advance is the recognition of the logical proposition that the negative is just as much positive, or that what contradicts itself does not dissolve into a zero, into abstract nothing, but essentially into nothing more than the negation of its ***particular*** content, or that a negation of this kind is not negation of everything but the ***negation of the determinate subject matter*** that is dissolving itself, and hence is determinate negation; that therefore the result essentially harbours that from which it results; — which is strictly speaking a tautology, for otherwise it would be an immediate and not a result. Inasmuch as what results, the negation, is ***determinate*** negation, it has a content. What it amounts to is a new concept, though a higher and richer concept than the preceding one; for it has become richer by that concept's negation or opposite; it therefore contains it, but also more than it, and is the unity of it and of its opposite. — In this way the system of concepts too now has to form itself, — and to complete itself in an unstoppable, pure course that takes in nothing from outside.

I recognize that the method I have followed in this system of logic, — or rather that this system follows in its own self, — is capable of much further perfecting; but I know at the same time that it is the only true one. And this is readily evident from the fact that it {{{ XXI }}} is nothing distinct from its object and content; — for it is the content within itself, ***the dialectic which it has in its own self***, that moves it forward. It is clear that no expositions can count as scientific which do not go the course of this method and are not in keeping with its simple rhythm, for it is the course of the subject matter itself.

In conformity with this method I would recall that the divisions and headings of the books, sections and chapters which occur in the following treatise of logic itself, together with any statements attached to them, have been made to serve a preliminary survey and are properly speaking only of historical worth. Neither the content nor the body of the science itself includes them; they are assemblages put together by external reflection, a reflection that has already traversed the whole of the execution and therefore announces the sequence of its moments in advance, before they are yet brought on by the subject matter itself.

In the other sciences such advance determinations and divisions are likewise nothing other; there it merely runs assertorically, even in logic for example, „logic has two main parts, the doctrine of elements and methodology,“ and then under the doctrine of elements there stands without further ado the ***heading***: laws of thinking; — then ***first chapter***: on concepts. ***First section***: on the clarity of concepts and so forth. — But these determinations and {{{ XXII }}} divisions, arrived at with no deduction and no justification at all, make up the framework and the whole coherence of such sciences. Such a logic itself declares that concepts and truths have to be derived from principles; yet in what it names method, deriving is not so much as distantly contemplated. The order consists, say, in putting like things together, in sending the simpler ahead of the composite, and in other external considerations. But as regards an inner, necessary connectedness, the divisional determinations stand side by side in no other manner than in a register, and the whole transition consists in its now saying: ***Second chapter***; — or ***we come now*** to judgments, and the like.

Thus the headings and divisions occurring in this system too have no other significance than that of a table of contents. Apart from that, however, the necessity of the connectedness and the immanent emergence of the differences must be present, which falls within the treatment of the subject matter itself and within the concept's own further determining.

But that through which the concept leads itself further is the negative which it has within its own self; this constitutes the genuinely dialectical. ***Dialectic***, which has hitherto been regarded as a separate part of logic and, with respect to its purpose and standpoint, one may say, entirely misconstrued, thereby acquires an altogether different position. — Platonic dialectic too, even in the Parmenides, and elsewhere in any case {{{ XXIII }}} still more directly, has in part only the intention of dissolving and refuting restricted assertions through themselves, and in part simply the nothing for its result. Dialectic commonly appeared as an external and negative doing that does not belong to the subject matter itself, and that has its ground in mere vanity, as a subjective craving to set what is firm and true into wavering and to dissolve it, or that at least leads to nothing but the vanity of the object treated dialectically.

Kant placed dialectic higher, — and this aspect belongs among the greatest of his merits, — in that he took from it the semblance of arbitrariness which it had according to the ordinary representation, and exhibited it as a necessary doing of reason. Since it passed for nothing but the art of conjuring up deceptions and producing illusions, it was simply presupposed that it plays a false game and that its whole force rests solely on its concealing the fraud; that its results are merely got by trickery and are a subjective shine. Kant's dialectical expositions in the antinomies of pure reason certainly deserve no great praise when they are examined more closely, as will be done at greater length with several of them in the course of this treatise; but the general idea that he laid down as basis and thereby made prevail is the objectivity of shine and the necessity of the contradiction that belongs to the nature of the determinations of thinking: in the first instance, namely, insofar as these determinations are applied by {{{ XXIV }}} reason to things in themselves; but precisely what they are in reason and in regard to what is in itself, that is their nature. This result, ***grasped in its positive side***, is nothing other than the inner ***negativity*** of those determinations, or their self-moving soul, the principle of all natural and spiritual liveliness as such. But as soon as one halts at the negative side of the dialectical alone, the result is only the familiar claim that reason is incapable of cognizing the infinite; — an odd result, given that the infinite is what is rational, to say that reason is not capable of cognizing the rational.

In this dialectical element, as it is taken here, and hence in the grasping of the opposed in its unity, or of the positive in the negative, consists ***the speculative***. It is the most important aspect, but for a power of thinking still unpractised and unfree the most difficult. If such a power is still engaged in tearing itself loose from sensuously concrete representing and from argumentation, it has first to practise itself in abstract thinking, to hold concepts fast in their ***determinateness*** and to learn to cognize from them. An exposition of logic for this purpose would have to keep, in its method, to the division spoken of above and, with respect to the closer content itself, to the determinations that emerge for the individual concepts, without entering into the dialectical. In outward shape it would come to resemble {{{ XXV }}} the ordinary presentation of this science, while in content it would differ greatly from it, and it would still serve to practise abstract if not speculative thinking, a purpose which the logic made popular by psychological and anthropological ingredients cannot even fulfil. To spirit it would give the picture of a whole ordered methodically, even though the soul of the edifice, the method, which has its life in the dialectical, would not itself show up in it.

With respect to the formation of the individual and its relation to logic, I note finally that logic presents itself, as grammar does, under two distinct aspects, with two distinct values. To whoever first steps up to it and to the sciences at large it is one thing, and to whoever comes back to it from them, another. Whoever begins to get acquainted with grammar finds in its determinations and laws dry abstractions, contingent rules, in general an isolated multitude of determinations which display only the value and the significance of what lies in their immediate sense; cognition at first cognizes in them nothing but them. To one who, by contrast, has command of a language and knows other languages in comparison with it, to him alone can the spirit and the formation of a people show itself expressed in the grammar of its language. Those very rules and forms now have a filled-out, rich, living value. And finally, through the grammar, he can cognize the expression of spirit as such, {{{ XXVI }}} logic. Just so, whoever steps up to science finds in logic at first an isolated system of abstractions which, restricted to itself, does not reach over the other bodies of knowledge and sciences. Rather, held up against the riches of one's representation of the world, against the content of the other sciences that appears real, and compared with absolute science's promise to unveil the essence of these riches, the inner nature of spirit and of world, this science in its abstract shape, in the simplicity of its pure determinations, has rather the look of delivering anything sooner than this promise, and of standing devoid of substance over against those riches. The first acquaintance with logic restricts its significance to itself alone; its content counts only as an isolated occupation with the determinations of thinking, ***alongside*** which the other scientific occupations are a material and content of their own, upon which the logical has only a formal influence, and indeed one that comes about more of itself, and for which the scientific shape and the study of it can at a pinch be done without. The other sciences have on the whole thrown off the regular method of being a sequence of definitions, axioms, theorems and their proofs and so forth; the inborn form of thinking, so-called natural logic, makes itself felt in them on its own account, and gets along without any special cognition directed at it. Above all, however, the material and content of these sciences holds itself distinct {{{ XXVII }}} from the logical and entirely independent of it, and is more appealing to sense, representation, and practical interest of every kind.

So logic must indeed first be learned as something that one understands and sees into well enough, but in which scope, depth and further significance are at first missed. Only out of a deeper knowledge of the other sciences does the logical rise up for subjective spirit as something not merely abstractly universal but as the universal that embraces the wealth of the particular within itself; — just as the same moral maxim, in the sense a youth gives it who understands it quite correctly, does not possess the significance and the scope that it has in the spirit of a man experienced in life, for whom the whole force of what is contained in it thereby finds expression. Thus the logical first receives the appreciation of its worth when it has become the result of the experience of the sciences; out of that it presents itself to spirit as universal truth, not as a ***particular*** piece of knowledge ***alongside*** other material and realities, but as the essence of all this other content.

Granted that at the outset of study the logical is not there for spirit with this conscious force, spirit nonetheless receives through it the inward force by which it is guided into all truth. The system of logic is the realm of shades, the world of simple essentialities, set free from every sensuous concretion. The study of this science, {{{ XXVIII }}} the sojourn and the labour in this realm of shadows, is the absolute cultivation and discipline of consciousness. In it consciousness carries on a business remote from sensuous purposes, from feelings, from the merely supposed world of representation. Considered from its negative side, this business consists in keeping at a distance the contingency of argumentative thinking and the arbitrariness of letting these or the opposite grounds occur to one and pass as valid.

Above all, however, thought thereby gains self-subsistence and independence from the concrete. It becomes at home in the abstract and in advancing through concepts without sensuous substrates, and thereby becomes the unconscious force for taking up the remaining manifoldness of bodies of knowledge and sciences into the rational form, for grasping and holding fast what is essential in them, for stripping off the external and in this way drawing the logical out of them, — or, what is the same, for filling the abstract foundation of the logical acquired beforehand through study with the substance of all truth, and for giving it the value of a universal that no longer stands as a particular alongside other particulars but reaches over it and is its essence, the absolutely true.
