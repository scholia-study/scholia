## {{{ 95 }}} c.) Ideality

Being-for-itself is simple equality with itself. It has the two distinguished moments within it because simple equality with itself is not the immediate, being, but only is as sublating of otherness; it therefore contains at the same time a separation, or otherness, yet as a vanishing separation, as self-sublating otherness. The two moments are therefore inseparable. The infinite reference to itself is only as negation of the negation, and this sublating of otherness is immediately a unity referring itself to itself.

Being-for-itself in this determination, that it refers itself to itself in that the other is in it only as something sublated, is ***ideality***.

Ideality is therefore the same as what ***infinity*** is, or it is the positive and reflected, determinate expression of infinity. What is ***infinite*** is ***ideal***; it is without restriction only insofar as the other only is ***for it***. If the other had an existence, then it would not be merely ***a for-one***, but would constitute a limit.

***Ideality and reality are one and the same*** — that is one of the skewed expressions already censured. Ideality is rather the truth of reality, or, if by reality one wishes to understand the substantial, the true itself, then ideality is the genuine reality; namely insofar as existence or reality has determined itself into ideality.

Just as reality seemed to have distinguished {{{ 96 }}} meanings according to its two sides, that of being-in-itself and that of being-for-other, so too the ideal seems to be distinguished in the sense of being-in-itself, as ***infinite reference to itself***, and in the sense of being-for-other, namely as ***being-for-one***.

— Thus spirit, God, the absolute in general, is something ***ideal***, as infinite reference to itself, as unity with itself which is not lost in externality and in otherness, but ***for*** which all determinateness is. — The ***Leibnizian representing*** essence, the ***monad***, is essentially something ideal. Representing is a being-for-itself in which the determinatenesses are not limits but only moments. Representing is admittedly a more concrete determination, one belonging to consciousness, but here it has no further meaning than that of ideality; for even what is wholly without consciousness is something representing. In this system, then, otherness is sublated altogether; spirit and body, or the monads in general, are not others to one another, they set no limit to each other, they exert no influence upon each other; every relation whatever having an otherness for its ground drops away. That there are ***several monads***, and that they are thereby determined as others as well, is no concern of the monads themselves; it is the reflection of a third party falling outside them; they are not ***others in their own selves***. — Yet precisely here lies at the same time what is unfinished about this system. Only ***in themselves***, or ***in God***, the monad of monads, or ***else in the system***, are the monads something representing. But otherness is likewise at hand; let it fall where it will, into representation itself, or however that third be determined which regards them as others. The other is therefore not sublated in its own {{{ 97 }}} self; it is only excluded, and the monads are posited as such that are not others only through abstraction. Or if it is a third that posits their otherness, then it is also a third that sublates their otherness; but this entire ***movement, which makes them into something ideal***, falls outside them.

Other idealism, for instance the Kantian and Fichte's, does not get beyond the ***ought*** or the ***infinite progress***, and with this does not attain idealism and being-for-itself. In these systems the thing-in-itself, or the infinite check, does indeed step immediately into the I and becomes a mere ***for-it***; but it starts out from a free otherness. The I is therefore indeed determined as the ideal from the side of being-in-itself, as infinite reference to itself; but the side of ***being-for-one*** is not completed, and therefore neither is that first side.

The ideal is ***secondly*** also ***being-for-one***. This sense is distinguished from the first, the infinite reference to itself. In the former sense God, the I and so forth are called something ideal, and being-for-itself proper, infinity, is restricted to that sense, so that God, the I would in this way be only something ideal in that they simply would not be ***for-one***. — In this other sense an empty theory is called something ***merely*** ideal. The ideal then has roughly the meaning of a mere fancy, at least of a mere representation to which nothing actual corresponds, whose content is nothing ***for itself***.

{{{ 98 }}} Insofar, however, as this difference is held fast, ***existence*** and a ***something*** have remained over for representation, or have returned precisely through that very determining. As if, namely, a something were at hand which subsisted as ground or subject, and ***for which*** the other is, just as there were a something which would be only what is referred; the former the something that is for itself, the latter only the something that is for other. But ***being-for-one*** and ***being-for-itself*** do not constitute genuine determinatenesses over against one another. ***Being-for-one*** expresses the sublatedness of otherness; it is therefore essentially one with being-for-itself. Being-for-itself is infinite reference to itself in that it is sublated otherness. Insofar as the difference is assumed for a moment, and something ***that is for itself*** is already spoken of here, then what is for itself is itself that to which it refers as to the sublated other, which is accordingly ***for-one***. Being-for-itself is reference to itself, but infinite reference; negation is therefore contained in it. Or what is for itself is not something immediate, not something that is; but this non-being is simply sublated; it is therefore itself the sublated other, ***being-for-one***; through this it refers, in its other, only to itself. The ideal is therefore necessarily ***for-one***, but it is not for an ***other***; or the one for which it is, is only itself.

The I, then, spirit in general, or God, are ideal because they are infinite; but they are not ideal as beings-for-themselves distinguished from that which is for-one. For in that case they would be only immediate ones, or more precisely they would be existence, a being-for-other, since what would be for them would not be themselves but an {{{ 99 }}} other, if the moment of being for-one were not to belong to them. God is therefore ***for himself*** insofar as he himself is that which is ***for him***.

Being-for-itself and being-for-one, then, do not signify different meanings of ideality; they are its essential and inseparable moments.
