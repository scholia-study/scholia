## γ. World History

## {{ 548 }} § 548.

Because the determinate spirit of a people is actual, and its freedom is as nature, geographical and climatic determinateness is a moment it has on this natural side; it stands in _time_, and, as to content, must essentially run through a _particular_ principle and a development of its consciousness and actuality determined by that principle; – within itself it has a _history_. As limited spirit its self-subsistence is something subordinate; it passes over into _universal_ _world_ _history_, whose events present the dialectic of the particular spirits of peoples, the _judgment_ _of_ _the_ _world_.

## {{{ 20.524 }}} {{ 549 }} § 549.

This movement is the path of liberation of the spiritual substance, the deed by which the world's absolute final purpose is accomplished in it: spirit, which at first is only _in_ _itself_, brings itself to consciousness and self-consciousness, and so to the revelation and actuality of its essence that is in and for itself, and becomes also externally _universal_, becomes _world_ _spirit_. Because this development is in time and in existence, and so is history, its single moments and stages are the spirits of peoples; each, as single and natural, in a qualitative determinateness, is destined to fill only _one_ _stage_ and to carry out only one task of the whole deed.

::: remark
{{ 549 A }} It has been called an a _priori_ consideration of history that a _purpose_ in and for itself, and the determinations that develop out of it in accordance with the concept, are presupposed in it, and philosophy has been reproached for writing history a priori; a closer remark is called for on this and on historiography in general. That history, essentially world history, rests on a final purpose in and for itself, which has in actuality been realized in it and is still being realized, – the plan of providence, – and that _reason_ is in history at all: this must be settled philosophically in its own right, and hence as necessary in and for itself. What merits blame is only to presuppose arbitrary representations or thoughts, and to wish to find, and to portray, events and deeds as fitting them. Chiefly to blame for such an a priori procedure today, however, are those who give themselves out as wanting to be pure historians while at the same time, on occasion, expressly declaring against philosophizing, either generally or in history; philosophy is for them a troublesome neighbor, in that it opposes whatever is arbitrary and whims. This a priori writing of history has at times {{{ 20.525 }}} broken in from a quarter where one would least expect it, chiefly the philological, and more in Germany than in France and England, where historiography has refined itself into a firmer and riper character. In place of the pragmatizing inventions of psychological grounds and connections have stepped fictions – that of a primeval condition and its primeval people, said to have owned true knowledge of God and of all the sciences, that of priestly peoples, and more specifically, for instance, that of a Roman epic supposed to have been the source of the reports counted as historical on early Roman history, and so forth; and across a wide circle it appears to be regarded as a requirement of a _learned_ and _ingenious_ historiography that draws on the sources, to hatch such hollow notions and to combine them boldly out of a learned heap of sweepings of remote external circumstances, in defiance of the best attested history.

If we set this subjective treatment of history aside, the demand that is properly its opposite, namely that history not be considered according to an _objective_ _purpose_, comes on the whole to the same as the demand, which seems still more justified, that the historian proceed with _impartiality_. This demand is made in particular of the _history_ _of_ _philosophy_, where no inclination to any one notion or opinion is to show itself, just as a judge is not to have a special interest in either of the two parties to a dispute. Yet a judge is at the same time assumed to be discharging his office stupidly and badly if he has no interest, indeed no exclusive interest, in right, if he does not make it his purpose and sole purpose, and if he refrains from judging. This requirement of the judge one may call _partiality_ for right {{{ 20.526 }}} and one knows quite well here how to tell it from a _subjective_ partiality. However, in the demand for impartiality addressed to the historian, sober, self-satisfied chatter wipes out that difference and throws out both kinds of interest, requiring as it does that the historian bring along no determinate purpose or view by which he sorts out, arranges and judges the events, but narrate them just as he happens upon them, in their particularity that lacks relation and thought. This much is conceded, that a history must have an _object_, for instance Rome, its fortunes, or the decline of the greatness of the Roman empire. It takes little reflection to see that this is the presupposed purpose lying at the ground of the events themselves, and of the judgment that decides which of them matter, that is, which stand in a nearer or remoter relation to it. A history without such a purpose and such a judgment would be only a feeble-minded ramble of representing, not even a children's tale, for even children demand an interest in stories, that is, a purpose at least given to be divined and the relation of events and actions to it. For the existence of a _people_, the substantial purpose lies in being a state and maintaining itself as such; properly, no history belongs to a people that has not formed a state (a _nation_ as such), as peoples existed before forming states and others exist even now as savage nations. Whatever happens to a people and goes on within it gets its essential significance from its relation to the state; the object that belongs to history lies furthest from the mere particularities of individuals. If the universal spirit of an age in general is impressed in the character of a period's distinguished individuals, and even their particularities are the {{{ 20.527 }}} remoter and murkier media in which it still plays in weakened colors, and if even single details of a small event, of a word, express, not a subjective peculiarity, but an age, a people, a culture, with striking vividness and brevity, – to pick out such things being the business only of an ingenious historian, – then, by contrast, the mass of other single details is a superfluous mass, whose faithful collection oppresses and obscures the objects worthy of history; the essential characterization of spirit and of its age is always contained in the great events. A right sense has led to relegating such depictions of the particular, and the gathering up of its traits, to the _novel_ (such as the famous ones by Walter Scott and the like); good taste is to be seen in joining pictures of inessential, particular liveliness to an inessential material, of the sort the novel draws from private events and subjective passions. But in the interest of so-called _truth_ to weave the individual trifles of the age and of persons into the representation of the universal interests is contrary not only to judgment and taste but to the concept of _objective_ _truth_, in whose sense only what is substantial, and not the emptiness of external concrete existences and contingencies, is what is true for spirit, and in whose sense it is perfectly indifferent whether such insignificances are formally attested or else, as in the novel, characteristically invented and ascribed to this or that name and circumstance. – The interest of _biography_, to mention it here, seems directly opposed to a universal purpose, yet it too has the historical world as its background, with which the individual is entangled; even what is subjectively original, humorous and so forth strikes up a note with that import, and thereby heightens its interest; what is {{{ 20.528 }}} merely heartfelt, however, has a different ground and interest than history.

The demand of _impartiality_ on the _history_ _of_ _philosophy_, and likewise, one may add, on that of _religion_, partly in general and partly on church history, usually involves a still more explicit exclusion of any presupposition of an objective purpose. Just as the state was previously named as the matter to which judgment would have to relate the events in political history, so here _truth_ would have to be the object to which the single deeds and events of spirit would have to be related. Instead, the opposite presupposition is made: those histories are supposed to have for their content only subjective purposes, that is, only opinions and notions, and not the object that is in and for itself, truth – on the simple ground that no truth exists. Under that assumption, even interest in truth shows itself as nothing but partiality in the ordinary sense, partiality, namely, for opinions and notions that, equally empty of import, all count as indifferent. Historical truth itself thereby has only the sense of _correctness_, an exact report of what is external, with no judgment except on this correctness itself, by which only qualitative and quantitative judgments, no judgments of necessity and of the concept (compare the Remarks to §§ 172 and 178), are admitted. In fact, however, Rome or the German empire and so forth in political history are a real and genuine object and purpose, toward which the phenomena are to be directed and by which they are to be assessed; but in universal history, all the more, universal spirit itself – its consciousness of itself and of its essence – is a genuine and real object, content and purpose, which all other phenomena serve in and for itself, so that they owe their worth, and even their existence, solely to the relation they bear to it, that is, to the {{{ 20.529 }}} judgment by which they are subsumed under it, while it inheres in them. This much – that in the course of spirit (and spirit does not merely hover _over_ history as over the waters; it is what weaves in it and alone moves it) freedom, the development determined through its concept, is the determining factor, and only its concept is the final purpose, namely truth, spirit being consciousness; or, put otherwise, that _reason_ is in history – will in part be a plausible belief at the least, in part, however, it is philosophy's cognition.

## {{ 550 }} § 550.

This liberation of spirit, in which it goes toward coming to itself and realizing its truth, and the work of it, is the highest and absolute _right_. In the self-consciousness of a particular people, the stage that universal spirit has for the time being reached in its existence has its bearer, and this self-consciousness is also the objective actuality in which spirit lays its will. Against this absolute will the will of the other particular spirits of peoples is without right, and that people is the world-dominating one; yet spirit goes beyond whatever it owns at the moment too, treating it as a mere particular stage, and thereafter hands it over to chance and to judgment.

## {{ 551 }} § 551.

Since such work of actuality appears as action and thereby as a work of _single_ _ones_, these, with regard to the substantial content of their labor, are _instruments_, and their subjectivity, which is what is peculiarly theirs, is the empty form of activity. What they thereby obtain for themselves, from the individual share they took in a substantial business whose preparation and determination did not depend on them, is only a formal universality of subjective representation, – _fame_, which is their reward.

## {{{ 20.530 }}} {{ 552 }} § 552.

The spirit of a people contains natural necessity and stands in external existence (§ 483); the ethical substance, infinite in itself, is for itself a particular and limited one (§§ 549 and 550), and contingency afflicts its subjective side, together with unconscious custom and a consciousness of its content as something temporally present, standing in relation to an external nature and world. Yet the spirit _thinking_ in ethical life sublates within itself the finitude it has as spirit of a people in its state and the state's temporal interests, the system of laws and customs, and raises itself to the knowing of itself in its essentiality; this knowing, however, itself carries the immanent limitedness of the spirit of a people. The thinking spirit of world history, however, having at once stripped off those limitednesses of the particular spirits of peoples and its own worldliness, grasps its concrete universality and raises itself to the _knowing_ _of_ _absolute_ _spirit_, as the eternally actual truth, in which knowing reason is free for itself, and necessity, nature and history only serve its revelation and are vessels of its honor.

::: remark
{{ 552 A }} The formal aspect of the elevation of spirit to God has been spoken of in the Introduction to the Logic (compare in particular § 51 Remark). – Of this elevation's starting points, _Kant_ has, in so far as he sees faith in God as issuing from _practical_ _reason_, seized in general upon the most correct. For the starting point contains implicitly the _content_, or material, that makes up the content of the concept of God. Neither _being_ (the cosmological proof's choice) nor merely _purposive_ _activity_ (the physico-theological proof's) makes up the genuine concrete material, however, but _spirit_, whose absolute determination, effective reason – that is, the concept determining and realizing itself – is freedom. That the elevation of the subjective spirit to God, taking place in this determination {{{ 20.531 }}}, is in Kant's presentation lowered once more to a _postulate_, a mere _ought_, is the skewedness discussed earlier: it reinstates immediately as true and valid the opposition of finitude, whose sublation into truth is that very elevation.

It was shown earlier of the _mediation_ that is the elevation to God (§ 192, compare § 204 Remark) that attention must go above all to the moment of _negation_, by which the essential content of the starting point is cleansed of its finitude and thereby emerges free. This moment, abstract in its logical form, has now received its most concrete meaning. What serves here as the finite point of departure is real ethical self-consciousness; it lifts its spirit to its truth by a _negation_ that consists in an ethical world's _actual_ achievement: its knowing cleansed of subjective opinion, its willing freed from the self-seeking of desire. Only out of ethical life do true religion and true religiosity come forth; they are ethical life thinking, that is, ethical life that has come to be conscious of how freely universal its concrete essence is. Only out of it and from it is the idea of God known as free spirit; outside ethical spirit it is therefore vain to look for true religion and religiosity.

Yet this issuing at the same time gives itself, as everywhere in the speculative, the meaning that what is first set as the following and as what has issued is rather the absolute prior of that through which it appears mediated, and here, in spirit, is also known as the truth of that.

This, then, is the place to enter more closely into the _relation_ _of_ _state_ _and_ _religion_, and to throw light in doing so on categories current about it. The immediate consequence of what has gone before is that {{{ 20.532 }}} ethical life is the state traced back to its substantial inner being, the state develops and realizes it, and it is religion that supplies substantiality to ethical life itself and to the state. According to this relation the state rests on ethical disposition, and this on religious disposition. Religion being consciousness of _absolute_ _truth_, nothing can hold as right and justice, duty and law – that is, as _true_ in the world of free will – except in so far as it has a _share_ in that truth, is _subsumed_ _under_ _it_ and _follows_ _from_ _it_. For true ethical life to be a consequence of religion, however, religion must have the true _content_, which is to say that the idea of _God_ known within it must be the true one. The divine spirit, in so far as it dwells within self-consciousness in its actual presence, as that of a people and of its individuals, is ethical life; when this self-consciousness withdraws into itself from its empirical actuality and brings its truth to consciousness, nothing is present in its _faith_ and its _conscience_ but what it possesses in the _certainty_ of itself, in its spiritual actuality. Neither can be separated from the other; conscience cannot be double, a religious one and, differing from it in import and content, an ethical one. In form, however, which is to say for thinking and knowing, – and both religion and ethical life belong to intelligence, being a thinking and a knowing, – it is for the religious content, as the pure and therefore highest truth in and for itself, to give sanction to the ethical life that stands in empirical actuality; self-consciousness thus has in religion the basis of ethical life and of the state. Our times have committed the monstrous error of wanting to treat these inseparables as separable, even as indifferent toward each other. The relation of religion to the state has thus been viewed as if the state already existed for itself otherwise, from some power and force, and the religious, as the subjective side of individuals, had only to be added to strengthen it, as something desirable, or {{{ 20.533 }}} were even a matter of indifference, with the state's ethical life, that is, rational right and constitution, standing firm on its own ground. Because the two sides are inseparable, it is worth making noticeable the separation that shows itself on religion's side. It affects first the _form_, that is, how self-consciousness relates to the content of truth. That content being the substance, as the spirit that dwells in self-consciousness in its actuality, self-consciousness possesses certainty of itself in it and is free within it. A relation of unfreedom can, however, obtain as regards form, even though the content of religion that is _in_ _itself_ is absolute spirit. To cite something more determinate: this great difference is to be found even inside the Christian religion, where no natural element constitutes the content of God and none enters his import as a moment, but God, known in _spirit_ and in _truth_, is the content. And yet in the Catholic religion this spirit is set rigidly over against the self-conscious spirit in actuality. First, in the host God is presented to religious adoration as an _external_ _thing_ (whereas in the Lutheran church the host as such is consecrated and raised to present God only and solely in _enjoyment_, that is, in the annihilation of its externality, and in _faith_, that is, in spirit at once free and certain of [itself]). All the other relations that are external, and so unfree, unspiritual and superstitious, spring from that first and highest relation of externality; in particular a _lay_ _estate_ that receives the _knowing_ of divine truth, and the direction of _will_ and _conscience_ as well, from outside and from another estate, which itself reaches possession of that knowing not by a purely spiritual way {{{ 20.534 }}} but essentially needs an external consecration for it. Further: the manner of praying, in part merely moving the lips and in part spiritless in that the subject renounces a direct turning to God and asks others to pray; – the directing of devotion to miracle-working images, even to bones, and the expectation of miracles from them; – in general, justice through external works, a merit supposed to be earned by actions and even capable of being transferred to others, and so forth. – All this binds spirit beneath a _being-outside-itself_, whereby its concept is misjudged and perverted at its innermost, and right and justice, ethical life and conscience, accountability and duty are corrupted at their root.
:::

Only a legislation and constitution of juridical and ethical unfreedom, and in the actual state a condition of wrongfulness and unethicalness, correspond to such a principle and to this development of the unfreedom of spirit in the religious. Consistently, the Catholic religion has been praised as loudly, and is often still praised, as the one by which alone the stability of governments is assured, – in fact of governments connected with institutions that rest on the unfreedom of a spirit that ought to be free in right and ethics, that is, on institutions of wrong and a condition of ethical corruption and barbarism. These governments do not know, however, that in fanaticism they have a fearsome power which refrains from turning hostile to them only for as long as, and only on condition that, they stay caught in the servitude of wrong and immorality. But in spirit another power is also present; against that being-outside-itself and being torn apart, consciousness collects itself in its inner free actuality; _worldly_ _wisdom_ stirs in the spirit of governments and peoples, that is, wisdom about whatever is right and rational in and for itself within actuality. Rightly has the production of thinking, and more determinately philosophy, been called _worldly_ _wisdom_, for thinking makes the truth of spirit present, introduces it into the world, and so frees it in its actuality and in itself.

::: remark
{{{ 20.535 }}} {{ 552 A2 }} With this the content takes on a wholly different shape. The unfreedom of _form_, that is, of knowing and subjectivity, has for the ethical content the consequence that self-consciousness does not find it immanent, that it is represented as removed from self-consciousness, so that it is supposed to be truly only as negative toward self-consciousness's actuality. In this untruth the ethical import is called a _holy_ thing. But once the divine spirit has introduced itself into actuality and actuality has been liberated to it, what in the world is to be _holiness_ is displaced by _ethical_ _life_. In place of the vow of chastity, _marriage_ now first counts as what is ethical, and with it, as the highest on this side of the human being, the _family_; in place of the vow of poverty (to which, entangling itself in contradiction, there corresponds the merit of giving one's goods away to the poor, that is, of enriching them) there counts the _activity_ of earning for oneself by understanding and diligence, and _uprightness_ in this traffic and in the use of property, ethical life in civil society; in place of the vow of obedience there counts _obedience_ _to_ _the_ _law_ and to lawful state institutions, which is itself true freedom, because the state is one's own reason realizing itself; this is _ethical_ _life_ _in_ _the_ _state_. Right and morality can be at hand only on this footing. It is not enough that religion commands: _Render_ _unto_ _Caesar_ _the_ _things_ _that_ _are_ _Caesar's_, _and_ _unto_ _God_ _the_ _things_ _that_ _are_ _God's_; for what is at issue is precisely to determine what is Caesar's, that is, what belongs to worldly government; and it is known well enough what worldly government has arrogated to itself in its arbitrary will, as spiritual government has on its side. The divine spirit must penetrate the worldly immanently; in that way wisdom is concrete within it and its entitlement is determined in itself. That concrete indwelling, however, consists in the configurations of ethical life already cited: the ethical life of marriage against the holiness of the celibate estate, the ethical life of activity in property and earning against the holiness of poverty and its idleness, the ethical life of obedience devoted to the right of the state against the holiness of an obedience without duty or right, the servitude of conscience. As soon as spirit's free nature is seen into and right and ethical life are felt as needs, the quarrel with the {{{ 20.536 }}} religion of unfreedom breaks out. It would help nothing for laws and the order of the state to be remade into a rational organization of right if the principle of unfreedom is not given up in religion. The two are incompatible with one another; it is a foolish notion to want to assign them separate domains in the belief that their difference will keep quiet toward each other and not turn into contradiction and struggle. Principles of juridical freedom can only be abstract and superficial, and the state institutions derived from them must be untenable in themselves, if the wisdom behind those principles misjudges religion so far as not to know that reason's principles find their ultimate and highest confirmation, regarding actuality, in the religious conscience, by being subsumed under the consciousness of absolute truth. Suppose that, however it came about, a legislation had come into being, a priori so to speak, resting on the principles of reason yet contradicting a national religion founded on principles of spiritual unfreedom: its activation would then rest with the _individuals_ of the government as such and of the whole administration that ramifies through all classes; and it is merely an abstract, empty notion, a delusion, that these individuals could act solely in accord with the sense or letter of the legislation and not in accord with the spirit of their religion, in which their innermost conscience and highest obligation lie. Set against what religion declares holy, the laws look like human handiwork; sanctioned though they be and introduced from without, they could not long withstand the contradiction and the assaults that the religious spirit directs at them. Hence such laws, though their content were the true one, run aground on conscience, whose spirit differs from the laws' spirit and gives them no sanction. Only as a folly of more recent times can it be reckoned that the constitution and legislation of a system of corrupted ethical life were changed without any change in religion, that a revolution was made without a {{{ 20.537 }}} reformation, and that people supposed a constitution opposed to the old religion and its holinesses could enjoy peace and harmony within itself, and that stability could be secured for the laws by external guarantees, for instance so-called chambers and the power granted them to determine the financial estimate (compare § 544 Remark) and the like. Wanting to detach rights and laws from religion, in the powerlessness to descend into the depths of religious spirit and lift it to its truth, can be seen as no more than a makeshift. Those guarantees are rotten props against the _consciences_ of the subjects who are to administer the laws, among which the guarantees themselves belong; it is rather the highest, the most unholy contradiction to want to bind the religious conscience, to which worldly legislation is something unholy, to that legislation and subject it to it.

In _Plato_ the recognition of the disunion had dawned more determinately that had arisen in his time between the existing religion and constitution of the state on one side, and on the other the deeper demands made on religion and on the political condition by freedom, now becoming conscious of its inwardness. _Plato_ grasps the thought that true constitution and political life are more deeply grounded on the idea, on the principles of eternal justice that are universal and true in and for themselves. To know and cognize these is indeed the vocation and business of _philosophy_. From this standpoint _Plato_ breaks out into the famous or notorious passage in which he has _Socrates_ declare most emphatically that _philosophy_ and _state_ _power_ must coincide in one, that the idea must be the ruler, if the misery of peoples is to see an end. _Plato_'s determinate conception here was that the idea, although it is _in_ _itself_ free thought determining itself, can reach consciousness solely in the shape of thought: as an import which, in order to be true, has to be raised out into universality and made conscious in that universality in its most abstract form.

{{{ 20.538 }}} A comparison of the Platonic standpoint, in its full determinacy, with the viewpoint adopted here, in which the state is considered in its relation to religion, calls for a reminder of the conceptual differences that matter essentially at this point. The first consists in this: with natural things, the genus, which is their substance, differs from their concrete existence, in which it is as _subject_; but this subjective concrete existence of the genus is further distinguished from what the genus, or the universal in general, lifted out _as_ _such_ for itself, comes to have in the _representing_, thinking being. This further individuality, the ground of the _free_ concrete existence of the universal substance, is the _self_ of the thinking spirit. The import of natural things does not get the form of universality and essentiality from itself, nor is their individuality itself the _form_; that is only subjective thinking for itself, which in philosophy gives that universal import its concrete existence for itself. The _human_ _import_, by contrast, is free spirit itself and comes to concrete existence in its self-consciousness. Precisely in having the form, thinking, itself for its content does this absolute import, spirit concrete in itself, consist; _Aristotle_ lifted himself to the height of thinking consciousness of this determination, beyond the Platonic idea (the _genus_, the _substantial_), with his concept of the entelechy of thinking, which is νόησις τῆς νοήσεως. Thinking in general, however, contains, and precisely by reason of the determination just stated, the immediate _being-for-itself_ of _subjectivity_ as well as universality, and the true idea of spirit concrete in itself is {{{ 20.539 }}} just as essentially in the one of its determinations, subjective consciousness, as in the other, universality, and is in the one as in the other the same substantial content. To _that_ form, however, belong feeling, intuition, representation, and it is rather necessary that the consciousness of the absolute idea be first grasped in this shape in time, and be there earlier as religion in its immediate actuality than as philosophy. The latter grows only afterwards from that foundation: Greek philosophy, for one, is later than Greek religion, and reached its completion precisely by grasping and comprehending, in its whole and determinate essentiality, the principle of spirit that first manifests itself in religion. Greek philosophy, however, could not help setting itself against its religion; for thought's unity and the idea's substantiality could only meet with hostility the polytheism of fantasy, the serene and frivolous playfulness of that poetizing. The _form_ in its infinite truth, the _subjectivity_ of spirit, at first broke forth only as subjective free _thinking_, not yet identical with _substantiality_ itself, which accordingly was not yet grasped as _absolute_ _spirit_. Religion could therefore show itself purified only by pure thinking existing for itself, by philosophy; but what philosophy fought was the _form_ _immanent_ _in_ _the_ _substantial_, that poetizing fantasy. The _state_, which develops out of religion in the same way but earlier than philosophy, presents in actuality, as _corruption_, the _one-sidedness_ that its idea, true _in_ _itself_, has in it. Plato, who with all his thinking contemporaries recognized this corruption of democracy and the deficiency of even its principle, brought out the substantial, but could not build into his idea of the state the infinite form of subjectivity, still hidden from his spirit; his state is therefore in itself {{{ 20.540 }}} _without_ _subjective_ _freedom_ (§ 503 Remark, 513 and so forth). The truth that was to dwell in the state, to constitute and govern it, he accordingly grasps only in the form of _thought_ truth, philosophy, and so made the pronouncement that until philosophers govern in the states, or those now called kings and rulers philosophize thoroughly and comprehensively, there will be no release from evils for the state, nor for the human race; until then the idea of his constitution cannot come to be possible and see the light of the sun. It was not granted to Plato to go further and say that until true religion emerges in the world and comes to rule in the states, the true principle of the state has not come into actuality. Until then, however, this principle also could not enter thought, and the true idea of the state – of substantial ethical life, with which the freedom of self-consciousness existing for itself is identical – could not be grasped by thought. Only in the principle of spirit that knows its essence, that is absolutely free _in_ _itself_ and has its actuality in the activity of its liberating, is there the absolute possibility and necessity that state power, religion and the principles of philosophy coincide in one, that the reconciliation of actuality in general with spirit, of the state with religious conscience as well as with philosophical knowing, is accomplished. Because subjectivity existing for itself is absolutely identical with substantial universality, absolute truth is contained in religion as such and in the state as such, which are forms in which the principle exists; so that truth, in being as philosophy, is itself found in only one of its forms. But religion too develops, in its own development, the differences contained in the idea (§ 566 and following); hence the existence can, indeed must, appear – in its first immediate, that is, itself one-sided manner, and its concrete existence be corrupted into sensuous externality and, further on, into the suppression of the freedom of spirit and into the perversity of political life. The principle, however, holds the infinite elasticity of the absolute form, to overcome this corruption of its {{{ 20.541 }}} determinations of form and of the content through them, and to effect the reconciliation of [the] spirit in itself. Thus at last the principle of religious and of ethical conscience becomes one and the same, in Protestant conscience – free spirit knowing itself in its rationality and truth. Constitution and legislation, like their activations, have as their content the principle and development of ethical life, which issues from the truth of religion restored to its original principle and thereby first actual as such, and can issue from it alone. Thus both stand surety for each other, firmly and reciprocally: the state's ethical life, and the state's religious spirituality.
