## Ways of Considering Nature

## {{ 245 }} § 245.

Practically, the human being relates to nature as to something immediate and external, being himself an immediately external and hence sensuous individual, who in so doing rightly comports himself toward natural objects as a _purpose_. Considered by this relation, they yield the _finite-teleological_ standpoint (§ 205.). Here we find the correct presupposition (§§ 207–211.) that nature does not contain the absolute ultimate purpose within itself; yet when this way of considering starts from particular _finite_ purposes, it turns them partly into presuppositions whose contingent content may in itself even be insignificant and insipid, and partly the relation of purpose by itself demands a deeper way of grasping than one according to external and finite relations, – the way of considering that belongs to the concept, which by its nature is immanent in general and hence immanent in nature as such.

## {{{ 20.236 }}} {{ 246 }} § 246.

What is called _physics_ was formerly called _philosophy of nature_, and is likewise a theoretical, indeed _thinking_, consideration of nature; on one side it does not start from determinations external to nature, such as those of the purposes just mentioned, and on the other side it is directed at cognizing nature's _universal_, so that this is at the same time _determinate_ _in_ itself – the forces, laws and genera, a content that must furthermore not be a mere aggregate but, arranged in orders and classes, must present itself as an organization. Since the philosophy of nature is a _comprehending_ consideration, it takes this same _universal_ as its object _for_ _itself_ and considers it in its _own_ _immanent_ _necessity_, according to the self-determination of the concept.

::: remark
{{ 246 A }} The relation of philosophy to the empirical has been discussed in the Introduction. Philosophy must not only agree with the experience of nature; the _origin_ and _formation_ of the philosophical science also has empirical physics as its presupposition and condition. The course by which a science comes to be and the preparatory work for it are one thing, however, and the science itself another; in the latter the former can no longer appear as a foundation, since here the foundation is to be the necessity of the concept. – It has already been remarked that, besides stating the object in the philosophical course according to its _determination_ _by_ _the_ _concept_, one must go on to name the _empirical_ appearance corresponding to that determination and to show of it that it does in fact correspond. With regard to the necessity of the content, however, this is no appeal to experience. Still less is an appeal admissible to what has been called _intuition_, which used to be nothing other than a procedure of representation and fantasy (even of fantasizing) following _analogies_, which can be more contingent or more significant and which impress determinations and schemata on the objects only _externally_ (§ 231, Remark).
