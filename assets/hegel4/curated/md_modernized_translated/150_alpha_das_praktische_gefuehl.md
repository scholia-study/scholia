## α) Practical Feeling

## {{ 471 }} § 471.

Practical spirit has its self-determination in it first in an immediate way, thus _formally_, so that it _finds_ _itself_ as singularity determined in _its_ inner _nature_. It is thus _practical_ _feeling_. In this it has, since it is, _in_ _itself_, subjectivity simply identical with reason, indeed the content of reason, but as _immediately_ _singular_, hence also as _natural_, _contingent_ _and_ _subjective_ content, which is determined just as much out of the particularity of need, of supposing and so forth, and out of subjectivity positing itself for itself against the universal, as it can be in itself commensurate with reason.

::: remark
{{ 471 A }} When an appeal is made to the _feeling_ of right and morality, as of religion, that a human being has within, to benevolent inclinations and so forth, to the {{{ 20.468 }}} _heart_ generally, that is, to the subject insofar as all the various practical feelings are united in it, the first thing to say is that this is correct in so far as these determinations are 1) its _own_, _immanent_ ones, 2) and then, insofar as feeling is opposed to the _understanding_, that against the latter's one-sided abstractions it _can_ be the _totality_. But feeling _can_ just as well be _one-sided_, inessential, bad. What is _rational_, present in the shape of rationality as something thought, has the same content as the _good_ practical feeling, but with universality and necessity, objectivity and truth.

On the one hand, then, it is _foolish_ to imagine that content and excellence get lost when feeling passes over into right and duty; it is this passage that first brings feeling to its truth. It is equally foolish to consider intelligence superfluous, even harmful, for feeling, heart and will; heart and will have their truth, which is the same as their actual rationality, solely in the _universality_ of intelligence, and not in the singularity of feeling as such. If feelings are of a true kind, they are so through their determinateness, that is, their content, and this is true only insofar as it is universal in itself, that is, has thinking spirit as its source. The difficulty for the understanding consists in freeing itself from the separation that it has once made arbitrarily between the faculties of the soul, feeling, thinking spirit, and in coming to the representation that in the human being there is only One reason, in feeling, willing and thinking. Connected with this, a difficulty is found in the fact that the ideas that belong solely to thinking spirit, God, right, ethical life, can also be _felt_. Feeling, however, is simply the form of the subject's immediate, peculiar singularity, and that content can be set into it just as any other objective content can, which consciousness also credits with objecthood.

On the other hand, it is _suspicious_, and very much more than that, to hold fast to feeling and heart against rationality as thought, right, duty, {{{ 20.469 }}} law, because what is _more_ in the former than in the latter is only particular subjectivity, vanity and arbitrary will. – For the same reason it is awkward, in the scientific consideration of feelings, to go into more than their _form_ and to consider their content, since this, as thought, rather constitutes the self-determinations of spirit in their universality and necessity, the rights and duties. For the peculiar consideration of the practical feelings, as of inclinations, only the selfish, bad and evil ones would remain; for only they belong to the singularity that holds itself fast against the universal; what they contain is the reverse of rights and duties, but precisely thereby they receive their closer determinateness only in opposition to these.
:::

## {{ 472 }} § 472.

Practical feeling contains the _ought_, its self-determination as _existing_ _in_ _itself_, _related_ to an _existent_ singularity that is to be valid only in conformity to the former. Since in this immediacy objective determination is still lacking to both, this relation of the _need_ to existence is the wholly subjective and superficial _feeling_ of the _agreeable_ or _disagreeable_.

::: remark
{{ 472 A }} Pleasure, joy, pain and so forth, shame, remorse, contentment and so forth are in part only modifications of formal practical feeling generally, but in part differentiated by their content, which constitutes the determinateness of the ought.

The famous question of the _origin_ of _ill_ in the world arises, at least insofar as by ill is understood at first only the disagreeable and _pain_, at this standpoint of the formal practical. Ill is merely _being_'s failure to conform to the _ought_. The ought carries many meanings, and since contingent _purposes_ also take the form of the ought, it carries infinitely many. With regard to them, ill is only the right that is exercised on the vanity and nullity of their conceit. They themselves are already ill. – The finitude of life and of spirit falls into their {{{ 20.470 }}} _judgment_, in which they have the other, separated from them, at the same time as their negative within them, and are thus the contradiction that is called ill. In what is dead there is neither ill nor pain, because in inorganic nature the concept neither stands opposed to its existence nor, within the difference, remains at once the subject of that existence. In life already, and still more in spirit, this immanent differentiation is present, and an _ought_ thereby enters; and negativity, subjectivity, the I, freedom are the principles of ill and of pain. – Jacob _Böhme_ grasped _I-hood_ as the _torment_ and _agony_ and as the _source_ of nature and of spirit.
:::
