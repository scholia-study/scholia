## {{{ 20.367 }}} α) The Genus and the Species

## {{ 368 }} § 368.

In its universality existing in itself, the genus first _particularizes_ itself into _species_ in general. The _distinct_ _formations_ and _orders_ _of_ _animals_ have as their underlying ground the universal _type_ _of_ _the_ _animal_, determined by the concept, which nature presents partly in the different _stages_ _of_ _its_ _development_, from the simplest organization up to the most perfected, in which it is the instrument of spirit, partly under the different _circumstances_ and _conditions_ of _elementary_ _nature_. Developed to singularity, the species of the animal distinguishes itself from the others in and through itself, and through the negation of them is _for_ _itself_. Thus, in hostile conduct degrading others to inorganic nature, _violent_ _death_ is the natural fate of individuals.

::: remark
{{ 368 A }} In _zoology_, as in the natural sciences in general, the concern has been more to find for subjective cognition secure and simple _marks_ of the classes, orders and so forth. Only since this aim of so-called artificial systems has been put more out of sight in the cognition of animals has a larger view opened, which goes to the _objective_ nature of the formations themselves; among the empirical sciences there is hardly one that {{{ 20.368 }}} has in recent times gained such great extensions, not chiefly in the mass of observations, for that has been lacking in no science, but in the respect that its material has worked itself toward the concept, as zoology has through its auxiliary science, _comparative_ _anatomy_. As the thoughtful observation of nature (above all that of French naturalists) took up the division of plants into monocotyledons and dicotyledons, so it took up the striking difference that the absence or presence of _vertebrae_ makes in the animal world; the basic division of animals has in this way been brought back in essentials to the one that _Aristotle_ already saw. – More closely, then, the _habitus_ has partly been made the chief point in the individual formations, as a connection determining the construction of _all_ _parts_, so that the great founder of comparative anatomy, _Cuvier_, could boast of being able to cognize the essential nature of the whole animal from a single bone. Partly the universal type of the animal has been followed through the different formations, however imperfect and disparate they appear, and has been recognized in its significance in the barely beginning intimation as well as in the intermixture of organs and functions, and has thereby been raised above and out of particularity into its universality. – A chief side of this consideration is the cognition of how nature forms and fits this organism to the particular element into which it casts it, to climate, circle of nourishment, in general to the world in which it lives (which can also be a single species of plant or of another animal). But for the special determination a right instinct has hit upon taking the distinguishing determinations also from the teeth, claws and the like – from _the_ _weapons_, for it is through these that the animal itself posits and maintains itself against the others as something existing for itself, that is, distinguishes itself.

It is the _immediacy_ of the idea of life that the concept does not _exist_ as such in _life_, so that its concrete existence subjects itself to the manifold conditions and circumstances of external nature, and can appear in the poorest forms; the _fertility_ of the earth lets life sprout _everywhere_ and in all ways. The animal world can, still less perhaps than the other spheres of nature, present a rational system of organization {{{ 20.369 }}} independent in itself, hold fast to the _forms_ that would be determined by the concept and preserve them against the imperfection and intermixture of the conditions, against confusion, stunting and transitions. – This weakness of the concept in nature in general subjects not only the formation of individuals to external contingencies (the developed animal, and man most of all, is exposed to monstrosities), but also the genera entirely to the alterations of the external universal life of nature, whose change the animal lives through with it (compare Remark to § 392), and which is thereby only a change of health and disease. The surroundings of external contingency contain almost only what is alien; they exert upon its feeling a continuing violence and threat of dangers, and this feeling is an _insecure_, _anguished_, _unhappy_ one.
