## {{{ 20.100 }}} C. Third Position of Thinking with Respect to Objectivity. Immediate Knowing

## {{ 61 }} § 61.

The critical philosophy conceives thinking as _subjective_, its _ultimate_, insurmountable determination being _abstract_ _universality_, formal identity; thinking is thereby opposed to truth as universality concrete in itself. In this highest determination of thinking, called reason, the categories play no role. – The opposed standpoint conceives thinking as the activity of _the_ _particular_ alone, and in this way likewise declares it unable to grasp truth.

## {{ 62 }} § 62.

As the activity of the particular, thinking has nothing but the _categories_ for its product and content. As the understanding holds them fast, they are _limited_ determinations, that is, forms belonging to the _conditioned_, the _dependent_, the _mediated_. For thinking confined to them, the infinite, the true, is not; no transition to it can be made (against the proofs for God's existence). Such determinations of thought are also called _concepts_, and so to _comprehend_ an object means no more than to take it up as something _conditioned_ and _mediated_; to the extent that the object is the true, infinite, unconditioned, comprehending it therefore means turning it into something conditioned and mediated, and thus perverting the true into the untrue instead of grasping it in thought.

::: remark
{{ 62 A }} Here is the only simple polemic brought by the standpoint that asserts an exclusively immediate knowing of God and of the {{{ 20.101 }}} true. At an earlier stage, the so-called anthropopathic representations of every sort were removed from God as finite and therefore unworthy of the infinite, and he was thereby already reduced to a fairly empty essence. The determinations of thought, however, were not as yet comprised under the anthropopathic at all; thinking was rather credited with stripping finitude from the representations of the absolute, – following the prejudice of all times noted above, that truth is reached only by reflective thinking. Now finally even the determinations of thought as such have been declared anthropopathism, and thinking an activity of _only_ _finitizing_. – _Jacobi_ gave this polemic its most determinate statement in the seventh Appendix to the Letters on Spinoza, having drawn it, incidentally, from Spinoza's own philosophy and turned it against cognition as such. On the basis of this polemic cognition is grasped only as cognition of the finite, as thinking's advance through _series_ of the _conditioned_ to the _conditioned_, where _each_ term that is a condition is itself in turn just a conditioned thing; – by way of _conditioned_ _conditions_. To explain and comprehend, on this view, is to point out something as _mediated_ by an _other_; so all content is merely _particular_, _dependent_ and _finite_, whereas the infinite, the true, God, lies beyond the mechanism of the connection to which cognition is said to be confined. – It matters that, whereas Kantian philosophy located the finitude of the categories mainly just in the formal determination of their _subjectivity_, this polemic brings the categories up for discussion in respect of their determinateness, and recognizes the category as such to be finite. – Jacobi had before his eyes especially the splendid achievements of the natural sciences (the sciences exactes) in cognizing natural forces and laws. Remaining within this ground of the finite, one admittedly cannot find the infinite; thus _Lalande_ declared that he had scoured the whole of heaven without finding God (compare the Remark to § 60.). On this ground the end result was the _universal_ as the _indeterminate_ aggregate of the external {{{ 20.102 }}} finite, _matter_; and Jacobi was right to see no other way out along the path of mere advance through _mediations_.
:::

## {{ 63 }} § 63.

At the same time it is asserted that _truth_ _is_ _for_ _spirit_, so much so that _reason_ alone is what sustains the human being, and that reason is _knowledge_ _of_ _God_. Yet since mediated knowing is supposed to be confined to finite content, reason is _immediate_ _knowing_, _faith_.

::: remark
{{ 63 A }} The categories that occur at this standpoint are _knowledge_, _faith_, _thinking_, _intuiting_; presupposed as _familiar_, they are far too often used arbitrarily, according to mere psychological representations and distinctions, while what their nature and concept is, the only thing that would matter, remains uninvestigated. Hence _knowledge_ is quite commonly set against _faith_, even though faith is at the same time determined as immediate knowing and therefore acknowledged at once as a knowing too. It will probably also be found, as an empirical fact, that what one believes is in consciousness, so that one at least _knows_ _about_ _it_; and likewise that what one believes is present in consciousness as something _certain_, and one therefore knows it. – Thinking, moreover, is principally set against immediate knowing and faith, and in particular against intuiting. Should intuiting be determined as _intellectual_, it can be called nothing but _thinking_ intuiting, unless one means by the intellectual here, where God is the object, perhaps fantasy-representations and images as well. In the language of this philosophizing, faith is said also of the ordinary things of _sensible_ presence. Jacobi's formulation is that we _believe_ ourselves to have a _body_, and _believe_ in the _concrete_ _existence_ of _sensible_ _things_. But when what is at issue is faith in the true and eternal, the claim that God is revealed, given, in immediate knowing, in intuiting, then these are no sensible things but a content universal in _itself_, objects solely for _thinking_ spirit. Likewise, where _singularity_ as I, {{{ 20.103 }}} _personality_, is at issue, – not an _empirical_ I or a _particular_ personality being meant, – and above all where the personality of God is before consciousness, the talk is of _pure_ personality, that is, one universal in _itself_; such a personality is thought and belongs to thinking alone. – Pure _intuiting_, further, is wholly the same as pure thinking. At first, intuiting and faith express the determinate representations that ordinary consciousness links with these words; as such they are indeed different from thinking, and nearly everyone can understand this difference. Now, though, faith and intuiting are supposed to be taken in a higher sense too, as faith in God, as intellectual intuiting of God, that is, abstraction is to be made precisely from what constitutes the difference of intuiting and faith from thinking. It cannot be said how faith and intuiting, once moved into this higher region, are still different from thinking. One supposes that by means of such emptied distinctions one has said and maintained something very weighty, and is combating determinations that are the same as those maintained. – The expression _faith_, however, has the particular advantage of recalling _Christian-religious_ faith, of seeming to include it or even easily to be the same, so that this believing philosophizing looks essentially pious and Christian-pious, and, on the strength of that piety, grants itself the freedom to make its arbitrary assurances all the more with pretension and authority. Yet one must not let appearance deceive one about what can creep in through mere likeness of words, and must keep the difference firmly in view. Christian faith includes the authority of the church, while the faith of that philosophizing standpoint is merely the authority of one's own subjective revelation. Moreover, Christian faith has an objective content, rich in itself, a system of doctrine and cognition; the content of this other faith, however, is so indeterminate in itself that {{{ 20.104 }}} it may well admit that content, but equally takes in the belief that the Dalai Lama, the bull, the ape and the like are God, and by itself limits itself to _God_ _in_ _general_, the _highest_ _being_. Faith in that would-be philosophical sense is merely the dry _abstraction_ of immediate knowing, an entirely formal determination, not to be confused with, nor taken for, the spiritual fullness of Christian faith, whether in respect of the believing heart and the Holy Spirit dwelling in it, or in respect of the doctrine rich in content.

What is called faith and immediate knowing here is, incidentally, quite the same as what has elsewhere been named inspiration, revelation in the heart, a content implanted in human beings by nature, and, in particular, healthy human understanding, common sense, communal sense. All these forms alike make into the principle the immediacy with which a content is found in consciousness, is a fact in it.
:::

## {{ 64 }} § 64.

What this immediate knowing knows is that the infinite, the eternal, God, who is in our _representation_, also _is_, – that the certainty of its _being_ is joined immediately and inseparably in consciousness to this _representation_.

::: remark
{{ 64 A }} Philosophy can least of all think of contradicting these propositions of immediate knowing; it might rather congratulate itself that these old propositions of _its_ own, which express even its whole universal content, have become in a manner general prejudices of the age, though admittedly in an unphilosophical way. One can only marvel, rather, that these propositions could have been thought opposed to philosophy, – the propositions that what is held true is immanent in spirit (§ 63.) and that for spirit there is truth (in the same place). The proposition is interesting above all in its formal aspect, namely that the _thought_ of God is joined immediately and inseparably to his _being_, and the {{{ 20.105 }}} _objectivity_ to the _subjectivity_ which thought has at first. The philosophy of immediate knowing carries its abstraction so far that it declares the determination of their _concrete_ _existence_ to be joined inseparably too, in intuition, not only to the thought of God but also to the _representation_ of my _body_ and of _external_ things. – Should philosophy seek to prove such a unity, that is, to show that inseparability from being, or objectivity, lies in the nature of thought, or of subjectivity, itself, such proofs may stand or fall as they will; philosophy must in any event be thoroughly pleased to have it asserted and shown that its propositions are _facts_ _of_ _consciousness_ as well, and so accord with _experience_. – What separates the assertion of immediate knowing from philosophy comes down solely to the fact that immediate knowing takes up an _exclusive_ position, or solely to its setting itself against philosophizing. – Yet even in the manner of immediacy, the proposition around which, one may say, the whole interest of the new philosophy turns was pronounced at once by its originator: Cogito, ergo sum. To take that proposition for a syllogism, one would have to know hardly more about the nature of the syllogism than that an Ergo occurs in it; where would the medius terminus be? and a middle term surely belongs to a syllogism more essentially than the word: Ergo. But if one wants, to justify the name, to call that connection in Descartes an _immediate_ syllogism, then this superfluous form signifies only a _connection_ of _distinct_ determinations in which nothing _mediates_. In that case, though, the connection of being with our representations that the proposition of immediate knowing expresses is just as much a syllogism, neither more nor less. – From {{{ 20.106 }}} Mr. _Hotho's_ dissertation on _Cartesian_ philosophy, published in 1826, I take the citations in which _Descartes_ himself expressly states that the proposition cogito ergo sum is no syllogism; the passages are Respons. ad II\. Objekt\. De Methodo IV\. Ep\. I. 118. Of the first passage I quote the closer wording: Descartes says to begin with that our being thinking beings is prima quaedam notio quae ex nullo syllogismo concluditur, and goes on: neque cum quis dicit: ego cogito, ergo sum sive existo, existentiam ex cogitatione per syllogismum deducit. Because Descartes knows what a syllogism requires, he adds that were a derivation by syllogism to occur in that proposition, the major premise would have to figure in it: illud omne, quod cogitat, est sive existit. That latter proposition, though, is one that is rather first derived from the former.

Descartes' expressions about the proposition of the inseparability of my being as a thinker from being – that this connection is contained and stated in the _simple_ _intuition_ of consciousness, that it is utterly first, a principle, the most certain and evident thing, such that no skepticism can be imagined so enormous as to refuse it – are so eloquent and determinate that the modern propositions of Jacobi and others on this immediate connection can pass only as superfluous repetitions.
:::

## {{ 65 }} § 65.

This standpoint is not content with having shown that _mediated_ knowing, taken _in_ _isolation_, is insufficient for truth; what is peculiar to it is that it holds _immediate_ knowing, taken _only_ _in_ _isolation_, with mediation _excluded_, to have truth for its content. – In such exclusions the standpoint at once declares itself a relapse into the metaphysical understanding and its _Either_ – _Or_, hence in fact into the very relation of external mediation, which rests on holding fast to the finite, {{{ 20.107 }}} that is, to one-sided determinations, and which that view wrongly thinks it has risen above. Let us, however, leave this point undeveloped; exclusively immediate knowing is asserted merely as _a_ _fact_, and here in the introduction it is to be taken up only in terms of this external reflection. In itself, what matters is the logical aspect of the opposition between immediacy and mediation. That standpoint, though, refuses to consider the nature of the matter, that is, the concept, since such consideration leads to mediation and even to cognition. True consideration, consideration of the logical, must find its place within the science itself.

::: remark
{{ 65 A }} The whole second part of the _Logic_, the doctrine of _essence_, treats of the essential, self-positing unity of immediacy and mediation.
:::

## {{ 66 }} § 66.

Let us therefore stay with the point that immediate knowing is to be taken as a _fact_. This brings the consideration, however, onto the field of _experience_, to a _psychological_ phenomenon. – In this regard it should be mentioned that it is among the commonest experiences for truths which one knows perfectly well to result from the most intricate and highly mediated reflections to present themselves _immediately_ in the consciousness of someone to whom such cognition has become familiar. A mathematician, like anyone trained in a science, has immediately to hand solutions to which a very intricate analysis has led; every cultured person has immediately present in their knowledge a host of general viewpoints and principles that arose only from much reflective thinking and long experience of life. The fluency we have attained in some kind of knowledge, in art, in technical skill as well, consists just in having such items of knowledge, such kinds of activity, _immediately_ in consciousness when the occasion arises, even in outward activity and in one's limbs. – In all these cases the immediacy of {{{ 20.108 }}} knowing, far from excluding its mediation, is linked with it in such a way that immediate knowing is actually the product and result of mediated knowing.

::: remark
{{ 66 A }} Just as trivial an insight is the connection between immediate _concrete_ _existence_ and its mediation: germs and parents are immediate, initiating existence relative to children and so forth, who are begotten. Yet germs and parents, however much, as existing in general, they _immediately_ _are_, are likewise begotten, and children and so forth, whatever the mediation of their existence, are now immediate, for they _are_. That I _am_ in Berlin, my _immediate_ presence, is _mediated_ by the journey made here, and so forth.
:::

## {{ 67 }} § 67.

As for the _immediate_ _knowing_ of _God_, of _what_ _is_ _right_ and of the _ethical_, – and here belong also the other determinations of instinct, implanted and innate ideas, common sense, natural reason and so forth, – whatever form is given to this originality, universal experience shows that for what it contains to come to consciousness, _education_ and development are essentially required (even for _Platonic_ _recollection_); (Christian baptism, though a sacrament, itself carries the further obligation of a Christian education) that is, religion and ethical life, however much they are a _faith_, an _immediate_ knowing, are altogether conditioned by the _mediation_ called development, education, culture.

::: remark
{{ 67 A }} Where innate ideas have been asserted and opposed, a similar opposition of exclusive determinations has prevailed to the one considered here: the opposition between a connection that might be called an essential, _immediate_ one of certain universal determinations with the _soul_, and a different connection that would occur externally and be mediated by _given_ objects and representations. The empirical objection raised against the assertion of _innate_ ideas was that all human beings must then have these ideas, the proposition of contradiction for example, in their {{{ 20.109 }}} consciousness and must know it, this proposition having been counted among the innate ideas with others of its kind. A misunderstanding may be ascribed to this objection, in that the determinations meant, being innate, are not thereby supposed already to be in the _form_ of ideas, of representations of something known. Against immediate knowing, though, the objection is wholly to the point, for it expressly asserts its determinations in so far as they are in consciousness. – If the standpoint of immediate knowing should concede that, for religious faith especially, development and a Christian or religious education are _necessary_, then it is arbitrary to ignore this again when speaking of faith, or else it is thoughtlessness not to know that in conceding the necessity of education one has already stated the essentiality of mediation.
:::

## {{ 68 }} § 68.

The experiences cited appeal to what shows itself to be _linked_ to immediate knowing. Should this link at first be taken as merely an _external_ empirical connection, it proves, even for empirical consideration, essential and inseparable, since it is constant. Further, when according to experience this immediate knowing is taken for itself, as knowing of God and the divine, such consciousness is generally described as a _rising_ _above_ the sensible and finite, and above the immediate desires and inclinations of the natural heart, – a rising that passes into faith in God and the divine and ends in it, so that this faith, though an immediate knowing and holding-true, nonetheless has that course of mediation as its presupposition and condition.

::: remark
{{ 68 A }} It has already been remarked that the so-called proofs for the existence of God, which start from finite being, express this rising, and that they are not the inventions of a contriving reflection but the spirit's own necessary mediations, even though in the {{{ 20.110 }}} usual form of these proofs they lack their full and correct expression.
:::

## {{ 69 }} § 69.

The transition from the subjective idea to being, marked out (§ 64.), is what makes up the principal interest for the standpoint of immediate knowing, and it is asserted essentially as an original connection devoid of mediation. Taken wholly without regard to empirically seeming connections, this very center shows mediation _within_ _itself_, and indeed in its true determination: not as mediation with and through something external, but as closing together within itself.

## {{ 70 }} § 70.

What this standpoint maintains is that neither the _idea_ as a merely _subjective_ thought, nor mere _being_ for itself, is the true; – being only for itself, being that is not of the idea, is the sensible, finite being of the world. It is thus asserted directly that the idea, only _by_ _way_ _of_ being, and being conversely only _by_ _way_ _of_ the idea, _is_ _the_ _true_. The proposition of immediate knowing has a right to want, not empty indeterminate immediacy, abstract being or pure unity for itself, but the unity _of_ _the_ _idea_ with being. It is, however, thoughtless not to see that what is posited in the unity of _distinct_ determinations, a unity that is not merely pure immediate unity, that is, wholly indeterminate and empty, is precisely this: that one of the determinations has truth only as mediated through the other – or, if one likes, each is mediated with truth only through the other. – That the determination of mediation is contained in that very immediacy is thus shown as a _fact_, against which, by immediate knowing's own principle, the _understanding_ may raise no objection. Only the ordinary abstract understanding takes the determinations of immediacy and of mediation, each by itself, as {{{ 20.111 }}} absolute, and thinks it has in them something _firm_ by way of distinction; thus it produces for itself the insuperable difficulty of uniting them; – a difficulty that, as shown, is no more present in the fact than it persists in the speculative concept.

## {{ 71 }} § 71.

This standpoint is one-sided, and that carries determinations and consequences with it, whose main features remain to be pointed out now that the groundwork has been discussed. _In_ _the_ _first_ _place_: what is erected as the criterion of truth is not the _nature_ of the _content_ but the _fact_ of _consciousness_, and what is passed off as true therefore rests on _subjective_ knowing and on my _assurance_ that a certain content turns up in _my_ consciousness. Whatever I find in _my_ consciousness is thus elevated into something found in the consciousness of _all_, and given out as the _nature_ of consciousness itself.

::: remark
{{ 71 A }} The consensus gentium once found a place among the so-called proofs of God's existence; Cicero already appealed to it. The consensus gentium is a weighty authority, and it is an easy step from a content's being found in the consciousness of _all_ to its lying in the nature of consciousness itself and being necessary to it. Within this category of _universal_ agreement lay the essential awareness, which escapes not even the least cultivated human sense, that the individual's consciousness is at once something _particular_ and _contingent_. Unless the nature of this consciousness is itself investigated, that is, its particular and contingent side set apart, – the laborious operation of reflective thinking by which alone its in-and-for-itself universal can be discovered, – only the agreement of _all_ on a content can found a respectable prejudice that it belongs to the nature of consciousness itself. For the need of thinking to know what shows itself as _universally_ present to be _necessary_, the consensus gentium is admittedly not sufficient; yet even supposing that this universality of the {{{ 20.112 }}} fact were a satisfying proof, experience has led to its being given up as a proof of this faith, since there are individuals and nations in whom no faith in God is to be found.[^1] Nothing, however, is shorter and {{{ 20.113 }}} handier than to have to make only the mere _assurance_ that I find a content in my consciousness along with the certainty of its truth, and that this certainty therefore belongs not to me as a particular subject but to the nature of spirit itself.
:::

## {{ 72 }} § 72.

From the claim that _immediate_ _knowing_ is to be the criterion of truth it follows, _in_ _the_ _second_ _place_, that all superstition and idolatry is declared truth and that the most unrighteous and unethical content of the will is justified. Indians take the cow, the monkey, the Brahmin or the Lama for God not on the strength of so-called mediated knowing, of ratiocinations and syllogisms, but because they _believe_ in it. Natural desires and inclinations, for their part, place their interests in consciousness of their own accord, and immoral purposes too are found in it quite immediately; a good or evil character would express the _determinate_ _being_ of the will, a being known in its interests and purposes, and known with the greatest immediacy of all.

## {{ 73 }} § 73.

Finally, the immediate knowing of God is to reach only as far as _that_ God is, not _what_ God is, since the latter would be a cognition and lead to mediated knowing. God as the object of religion is thereby expressly confined to _God_ _in_ _general_, to the indeterminate supersensible, and religion is cut down in its content to its minimum.

::: remark
{{ 73 A }} Were it really necessary to bring about no more than that the belief _that_ _there_ _is_ _a_ _God_ be kept alive, or even come into being, one could only wonder at the poverty of an age that lets the barest scrap of religious knowledge count as a gain, and has got as far, in its church, as returning to the altar that stood long ago in _Athens_ and was dedicated _to_ _the_ _unknown_ _God_!
:::

## {{{ 20.114 }}} {{ 74 }} § 74.

The universal nature of the _form_ _of_ _immediacy_ still needs brief statement. It is this very form which, being _one-sided_, makes its content one-sided too, and so _finite_. It endows the _universal_ with the one-sidedness of an _abstraction_, with the result that God is made into a being without determination; yet God can only be called spirit when he is known as _mediating_ _himself_ _with_ _himself_ within himself. Only thus is he _concrete_, living and spirit; _knowledge_ of God as spirit accordingly contains mediation within itself. – To the _particular_, the form of immediacy gives the determination of _being_, of _relating_ _to_ _itself_. But the particular is just this, relating to an _other_ outside it; by that form the _finite_ is posited as absolute. Because this form, wholly abstract, is _indifferent_ _to_ _any_ _content_ and for that very reason open to any content, it can sanction idolatrous and immoral content just as well as its opposite. Only insight into the content, that it is not self-subsistent but _mediated_ _through_ _an_ _other_, reduces it to its finitude and untruth. Since the content brings mediation with it, such insight is a knowledge that contains mediation. A content can be cognized as true, however, only in so far as it is not mediated with something else and not finite, that is, mediates itself with itself, and so is at once mediation and immediate self-relation. – That understanding which believes itself to have freed itself from finite knowledge, _the_ _identity_ _of_ _understanding_ found in metaphysics and the Enlightenment, itself again makes this _immediacy_, that is, _the_ _abstract_ _relation-to-itself_, abstract identity, the principle and criterion of truth. _Abstract_ _thinking_ (the form of reflecting metaphysics) and _abstract_ _intuiting_ (the form of immediate knowing) are not different.

## {{ 75 }} § 75.

This third position that is given to thinking in relation to truth could be assessed only in the way that {{{ 20.115 }}} the standpoint itself directly indicates and grants. It has thereby been shown to be _factually_ false that there _is_ immediate knowing, knowing that is without mediation either with an other or within itself with itself. It has likewise been pronounced a factual untruth that thinking advances _only_ _along_ determinations _mediated_ by an _other_, finite and conditioned ones, and that in the mediation this mediation does not equally sublate itself. The _example_ of the _fact_ of such cognition, which proceeds neither in one-sided immediacy nor in one-sided mediation, is _logic_ itself and the _whole_ _of_ _philosophy_.

## {{ 76 }} § 76.

Considered in relation to its starting point, the so-called _naive_ metaphysics mentioned above, the principle of immediate knowing proves, on comparison, to have _returned_ to the beginning which this metaphysics took in modern times as _Cartesian_ philosophy. Both assert:

+ 1) The simple inseparability of _thinking_ and the _being_ of the thinker, – Cogito ergo sum, is wholly the same as the immediate revelation to me in consciousness of the being, reality, existence of the I; (Cartesius at the same time declares expressly, Princ. phil. I. 9, that by thinking he understands _consciousness_ in general as such,) and this inseparability is the absolutely _first_ (neither mediated nor proven,) and _most_ _certain_ cognition.
+ 2) Likewise the inseparability of the representation of _God_ from his _existence_, in such a way that existence is contained in the very representation of God, this representation being absolutely not without the determination of existence, which is therefore necessary and eternal.[^2]
+ {{{ 20.116 }}} 3) The likewise immediate consciousness of the existence of _external_ things means nothing other than _sensible_ consciousness; that we have it is the slightest of cognitions; the only interest lies in knowing that this immediate knowing of the _being_ of external things is delusion and error, with no truth in the sensible as such, and that the _being_ of these external things is rather something contingent and transient, a _shine_, – that they are essentially this, possessing merely an existence separable from their concept, their essence.

## {{ 77 }} § 77.

The two standpoints differ, however:

1) Cartesian philosophy proceeds _on_ from these unproven presuppositions, held to be unprovable, _to_ _further_ developed cognition, and has in this way given the sciences of the modern age their origin. The modern standpoint, by contrast, has reached the result, important in itself (§ 62.), that cognition advancing through _finite_ mediations cognizes only the finite and holds no truth; {{{ 20.117 }}} and it requires the consciousness of God to stop at that faith, which is wholly abstract.[^3]

2) The modern standpoint leaves the method of ordinary scientific cognition introduced by Cartesius untouched on the one hand, and carries on the sciences of the empirical and finite that sprang from it in just the same way, – but on the other side it repudiates that method and, since it knows no other, repudiates _every_ method for the knowing of what is infinite in its import; for that reason it gives itself up to the wild arbitrariness of imaginings and assurances, to the self-conceit of morality and haughtiness of sensation, or to unbounded whim and ratiocination, which declares itself most strongly against philosophy and philosophemes. For philosophy permits neither mere assuring nor imagining nor the arbitrary thinking to and fro of ratiocination.

## {{ 78 }} § 78.

The _opposition_ between a self-subsistent immediacy of content or knowing and a mediation, equally self-subsistent and incompatible with it, is to be set aside first of all, being a mere _presupposition_ and arbitrary _assurance_. Likewise every other presupposition or prejudice must be given up on entering science, whether drawn from representation or from thinking; for science is where all such determinations are first to be investigated, and where what lies in them and in their oppositions is to be cognized.

::: remark
{{ 78 A }} As a negative science carried through all forms of cognition, _skepticism_ would offer itself as an introduction, one in which such presuppositions would be shown null. But it {{{ 20.118 }}} would be a path not only cheerless but, for that reason, also superfluous, since the dialectical is itself an essential moment of affirmative science, as will shortly be noted. Besides, skepticism would find the finite forms only empirically and unscientifically, and would have to take them up as given. To demand such a completed skepticism is the same as demanding that science be preceded by _the_ _doubting_ _of_ _everything_, that is, by the complete _absence_ _of_ _presuppositions_ in regard to everything. It is in fact accomplished in the decision to _want_ _to_ _think_ _purely_, by the freedom that abstracts from everything and seizes its pure abstraction, the simplicity of thinking.
:::

[^1]: Whether atheism or faith in God is found to be more or less widespread in experience depends on whether one is satisfied with the determination of _a_ God _in_ _general_, or requires a more determinate cognition of him. In the Christian world it will not be granted of the Chinese, Indian and other idols at least, any more than of the African fetishes, and even of the Greek gods themselves, that such idols are God; whoever believes in them does not, therefore, believe in God. If, on the other hand, one considers that such faith in idols nonetheless contains _in_ _itself_ faith in God _in_ _general_, as the individual contains the genus, then idolatry counts as a faith too, not merely in an idol but in God. The Athenians, conversely, dealt with poets and philosophers as atheists who took Zeus and the rest for mere clouds and the like and perhaps asserted only a _God_ _in_ _general_. – What matters is not what is contained _in_ _itself_ in an object, but how much of it has become _explicit_ for consciousness. If one lets the confusion of these determinations stand, every intuition of the human being, even the commonest sensible one, would be religion, since _in_ _itself_ every such intuition, every spiritual thing, does contain the principle that, developed and purified, rises to religion. One thing, though, is _to_ _be_ _capable_ of religion (which is what that _in-itself_ expresses, the capacity and possibility), another _to_ _have_ religion. – Travelers of recent times (the captains _Ross_ and _Parry_, for instance) have again come upon tribes (Eskimaux) to whom they denied all religion, down to even the trace of religion one might still find in African _sorcerers_ (Herodotus' _Goetes_). From a wholly different side, an Englishman who passed the opening months of the last Jubilee year in Rome reports in his travel account on the _Romans_ of today that the common people are bigoted, while all who can read and write are atheists. – The charge of atheism has, incidentally, grown rarer in recent times, probably chiefly because the import of religion, and what is demanded of it, has been reduced to a minimum (see § 73.).

I. 15. magis hoc (ens summe perfectum existere) credet, si attendat, nullius alterius rei ideam apud se inveniri, in qua eodem modo necessariam existentiam contineri animadvertat; – intelliget, illam ideam exhibere veram et immutabilem naturam, quaeque non potest non existere, cum necessaria existentia in ea contineatur. A turn of phrase that follows, and sounds like a mediation and proof, does no damage to this first foundation. – With Spinoza it is wholly the same: God's _essence_, the abstract representation, includes existence. Spinoza's first definition is that of Causa sui: it is such, cujus essentia involvit existentiam; sive id, cujus natura non potest concipi, nisi existens; – the inseparability of the concept from being is the basic determination and presupposition. But which concept is it that this inseparability from being belongs to? not that of _finite_ things, since these are precisely things whose existence is _contingent_ and created. – That Spinoza follows the 11th proposition, that God necessarily exists, with a proof, and likewise the 20th, that God's existence and essence are one and the same, is a superfluous formalism of proof. To say that God is the (indeed only) substance, that substance is Causa sui, _hence_ God exists necessarily – amounts to saying merely that God is that in which concept and being are inseparable.
