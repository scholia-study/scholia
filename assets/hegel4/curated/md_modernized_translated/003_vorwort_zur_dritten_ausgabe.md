## {{{ 20.27 }}} Foreword to the Third Edition

In this third edition manifold improvements have been introduced here and there; in particular, care has been taken to assist the clarity and determinateness of the exposition. Yet for the compendious purpose of the textbook the style had to be kept condensed, formal and abstract; it retains its vocation of receiving the necessary elucidations only through oral delivery.

Since the second edition, several assessments of my philosophizing have appeared, which for the most part have shown little vocation for such business; such frivolous rejoinders to works that have been thought through for many years and worked out with all the seriousness of the subject matter and of the scientific demand offer nothing gratifying in the sight of the ugly passions of conceit, arrogance, envy, scorn, and so forth, that thrust themselves forward from them, still less anything instructive. Cicero says: Tuscul\. Quaest. 1\. II\. »Est philosophia paucis contenta judicibus, _multitudinem_ consulto ipsa fugiens, eique ipsi _et_ _invisa_ _et_ _suspecta_; ut, si quis universam velit vituperare, _secundo_ _id_ _populo_ facere possit.« It is all the more popular to rail against philosophy, the less insight and thoroughness there is in doing so; petty, repugnant passion is comprehensible in the echo it meets in others, and ignorance joins it with equal intelligibility. Other subjects strike the senses, or are present to representation as overall intuitions; one feels that some knowledge of them, however slight, is needed if one is to have a say about them; they also recall common sense more readily, since they stand in a familiar, firm presence. But the lack of all this unleashes itself without scruple against philosophy, or rather against some fantastical empty image that ignorance imagines and talks itself into about it; it has nothing before it by which it could orient itself, and so drifts about entirely in the indeterminate, the empty, and thereby in the {{{ 20.28 }}} senseless. – Elsewhere I have undertaken the unpleasant and unfruitful business of shedding light on some such phenomena, woven out of passions and ignorance, in their uncovered nakedness.

Recently it might have seemed as if, from the ground of theology and even of religiosity, a more serious inquiry into God, divine things and reason, over a wider range, was to be stirred up scientifically. But right from the start the movement let no such hope arise; for the occasion proceeded from _personalities_, and neither the pretension of accusing piety nor the attacked pretension of free reason rose to the _matter_, still less to the awareness that, in order to discuss the matter, the ground of philosophy must be entered. That attack of the personal, on the basis of very special externalities of religion, showed itself with the monstrous presumption of wanting to pass judgment, on its own plenary authority, on the Christianity of individuals, and thereby to stamp upon them the seal of worldly and eternal rejection. _Dante_ took it upon himself, by virtue of the inspiration of divine poetry, to wield the keys of Peter, and to condemn to hellish damnation many of his – though already deceased – contemporaries by name, even popes and emperors. A more recent philosophy has been burdened with the defaming charge that within it the human individual makes itself God; but compared with that charge of a false consequence, it is an altogether different, genuine presumption to act as judge of the world, to rule on the Christianity of individuals and so to pronounce over them the innermost rejection. This plenary authority has as its shibboleth _the_ _name_ _of_ _the_ _Lord_ _Christ_, together with the _assurance_ that it is in the hearts of these judges that the Lord has his dwelling. Christ says: (Matt. 7, 20) »You are to recognize them from their fruits,« but the monstrous insolence of rejecting and damning is not a good fruit. He goes on: »Not everyone who calls me _Lord_, _Lord_ will enter the kingdom of heaven; on that day many will address me: _Lord_, _Lord_, did we not prophesy in your name? did we not cast out devils _in_ _your_ _name_? did we not perform many deeds _in_ _your_ _name_? then I shall confess to them: I have _not_ _yet_ _known_ you, _all_ _of_ _you_ _get_ _away_ _from_ _me_, you _evildoers_!« Those who assure us of exclusive possession of Christianity, and demand from others this belief in them, have not got so far as to drive out devils; rather many of them, like the {{{ 20.29 }}} believers in the Seeress of Prevorst, take pride in being on good terms with a rabble of ghosts and in having reverence for them, instead of chasing away and banishing these lies of an anti-Christian, servile superstition. Just as little do they show themselves capable of speaking wisdom, and utterly incapable of doing great deeds of cognition and science, which would be their vocation and duty; scholarship is not yet science. While they occupy themselves at length with the mass of indifferent outer things of faith, they stay, as regards the import and content of faith itself, all the more barren at the name of the Lord Christ, and deliberately, with abuse, spurn the working-out of the doctrine on which the faith of the Christian church rests; for spiritual, and above all thinking and scientific, expansion would disturb, even forbid and extinguish, the self-conceit of subjective insistence upon an assurance that is spiritless, unfruitful in the good and rich only in bad fruits: the assurance that Christianity is theirs, and theirs alone. – Scripture, in full and determinate awareness, so distinguishes this spiritual expansion from mere faith that only through the expansion does faith become _truth_. »Whoever in general _believes_ in me, says Christ: (John 7, 38), from that person's body will flow _streams_ _of_ _living_ _water_.« This is directly clarified and determined in verse 39: it is not faith as such in Christ's temporal, sensuous, present personality that brings this about, nor is such faith yet truth as such; the following verse 39 gives faith this determination, that Christ said it of the _spirit_ which was to be _received_ by those who _believed_ in him, because the Holy Spirit _was_ _not_ _yet_ _there_, since Jesus was _not_ _yet_ _glorified_; – by Christ's still unglorified shape is meant the personality that was then sensuously present in time, or that is afterward represented as such, which comes to the same content, and this personality is the immediate object of faith. While present among them, Christ revealed to his disciples, by his own spoken word, his eternal nature and his calling to reconcile God with himself and human beings with God, as well as the ordering of salvation and the teaching on morals, and the faith the disciples had in him takes all this up into itself. Even so, this faith, in which nothing was missing of the firmest certainty, is pronounced merely a beginning and a conditioning basis, something still incomplete; those who believed in this manner do not yet possess the spirit but are first to _receive_ it – it, the truth itself, which comes only after that believing and leads into all truth. Those, however, remain halted at such certainty, the condition {{{ 20.30 }}}; but certainty, itself only subjective, brings forth only the subjective fruit, formally, of _assurance_, and then in it of arrogance, of vilification and damnation. In defiance of scripture they cling only to certainty, and so stand against the spirit, which is the expansion of cognition and, only thereby, the truth.

This barrenness of scientific and, in general, spiritual import is something this piety shares with what it makes immediately the object of its accusation and condemnation. Religion has been drained of all content as much by the enlightenment of the understanding, with its formal, abstract, contentless thinking, as by that piety, with its reduction of faith to the shibboleth of Lord, Lord. In this neither has any advantage over the other; and as they meet in conflict, there is no material present in which they could touch and attain a common ground and the possibility of bringing it to inquiry and further to cognition and truth. The enlightened theology, for its part, has held fast in its formalism, namely of invoking _freedom_ of conscience, _freedom_ of thinking, _freedom_ of teaching, even reason and science. Such freedom is indeed the category of spirit's _infinite_ _right_, and is the other, _particular_ _condition_ of truth next to the first, faith. But _what_ rational determinations and laws the truthful and free conscience _contains_, what _content_ and doctrine free believing and thinking have, this material point they have refrained from touching, and have stopped at that formalism of the negative and at the freedom to fill freedom out at will and opinion, so that the content itself is, in general, indifferent. These could not approach a content for this reason also: that the Christian community must be united by the bond of a body of doctrine, a confession of faith, and still ought to be, whereas the universalities and abstractions of the stale, not living, rationalist water of the understanding do not admit the specific character of a Christian content and body of doctrine determinate in itself and developed. Whereas the others, insisting on the name Lord! Lord! openly and freely disdain the fulfillment of faith into spirit, import and truth.

Thus, much dust of arrogance, of spite and of personality, as of empty universalities, has indeed been stirred up, but it is struck with unfruitfulness; it could not contain the matter, could not lead to import and cognition. – Philosophy has been able to be content to be left out of the game; it finds itself outside the terrain of those {{{ 20.31 }}} presumptions, of personalities as of abstract universalities, and, drawn onto such ground, could only have expected what is unpleasant and unprofitable.

Since the deep and rich import has fallen away from the greatest and unconditioned interest of human nature, and religiosity, pious and reflective alike, has come to find the highest satisfaction without content, philosophy has become a contingent, subjective need. Those unconditioned interests have been arranged, in both kinds of religiosity, and by nothing other than ratiocination, in such a way that there is no longer any need of philosophy to satisfy those interests; indeed it is held, and rightly so, to be disruptive of that newly created contentment and such a narrowed satisfaction. Philosophy is thereby wholly surrendered to the free need of the subject; no kind of compulsion toward it is exerted upon that need; rather, where it is present, this need has to remain steadfast against suspicions and dissuasions; it exists only as an inner necessity stronger than the subject, by which the subject's spirit is then driven restlessly, »that it may overcome,« and procure for the urge of reason a worthy enjoyment. Thus, without the stimulus of any authority, not even the religious, and rather declared a superfluity and a dangerous or at least doubtful luxury, preoccupation with this science rests all the more freely on the interest in the matter and in truth alone. If, as Aristotle says, theory is the _most_ _blessed_ and, among goods, the _best_, then those who share in this enjoyment know what they possess in it: the fulfillment of what their spiritual nature requires; they are able to refrain from putting demands to others on the matter, and to let them be with their needs and with the satisfactions found for them. Mention has been made above of the uncalled intruding upon the business of philosophy; just as it makes itself the louder the less it is suited to take part in it, so the more thorough, deeper participation is lonelier with itself and quieter outwardly; vanity and superficiality are quickly done and drive themselves to speedy interjection; seriousness, however, concerning a matter that is great in itself and satisfied only through the long and difficult labor of completed development, sinks itself for long in quiet preoccupation into it.

That the second edition of this encyclopaedic guide was soon exhausted, a guide that, given its above-stated {{{ 20.32 }}} destination, makes the study of philosophy anything but easy, has let me see that, besides the loudness of superficiality and vanity, a quieter, more rewarding participation has taken place, which I now also wish for this new edition.

_Berlin_, 19 _September_ 1830.
