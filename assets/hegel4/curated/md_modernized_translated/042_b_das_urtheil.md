## b. The Judgment

## {{ 166 }} § 166.

The _judgment_ is the concept in its particularity, a distinguishing _relation_ among its moments, which stand posited as for themselves, and as identical at once with themselves, not with one another.

::: remark
{{ 166 A }} In the judgment one commonly thinks first of the _self-subsistence_ of the extremes, subject and predicate: the subject is a thing or determination for itself, the predicate likewise a universal determination lying outside this subject, say in my head, – which I then bring together with the subject, and so judge. Yet once the copula, _is_, asserts the predicate of the subject, that external, subjective _subsuming_ is sublated again, and the judgment is taken as a determination of the _object_ itself. – The _etymological_ sense of the word _Urteil_ in our language goes deeper: it expresses the concept's unity as what is first, and its distinguishing as the _original_ division, which is what the judgment is in truth.

In the abstract judgment the proposition is: the _singular_ is the _universal_. These are the determinations that _subject_ and _predicate_ initially bear to one another, the moments of the concept being taken in their immediate determinateness, their first abstraction. (The propositions that the _particular_ is the _universal_, and that the _singular_ is the {{{ 20.183 }}} _particular_, belong to the judgment's further determination.) It counts as a remarkable failure of observation that one finds in the logics no statement of the fact that in _every_ judgment this proposition is uttered: _the_ _singular_ _is_ _the_ _universal_, or, put more determinately, _the_ _subject_ _is_ _the_ _predicate_ (for instance, God is absolute spirit.) To be sure, singularity and universality, subject and predicate, are also distinct determinations, but the entirely general _fact_ remains none the less that every judgment states them as identical.

The copula, _is_, derives from the nature of the concept, which is _identical_ with itself in its externalization; singular and universal, being _its_ moments, are determinatenesses that cannot be isolated. The earlier determinatenesses of reflection _also_ stand in relation to one another within their relations, yet their connection is merely _having_, and not _being_, the _identity_ _posited_ _as_ _such_ or _universality_. Hence the judgment is first the true _particularity_ of the concept, being its determinateness or distinguishing, which none the less remains _universality_.
:::

## {{ 167 }} § 167.

The judgment is commonly taken in a _subjective_ sense, as an _operation_ and form occurring only in _self-conscious_ thinking. In the logical, however, this distinction is not yet present, and the judgment is to be taken quite generally: _all_ _things_ _are_ _a_ _judgment_, – that is, they are _singulars_ which contain within themselves a _universality_ or inner nature; or a _universal_ that has been _singularized_; universality and singularity are distinguished in them, and yet identical at the same time.

::: remark
{{ 167 A }} The objective way in which a judgment is expressed contradicts that merely subjective supposed sense, as though _I_ were to _ascribe_ a predicate to a subject: the rose _is_ red, the gold _is_ metal and so forth; it is not I who first attach anything to them. – Judgments are to be told apart from _propositions_ {{{ 20.184 }}}; propositions contain a determination of their subjects that does not stand to them in the relation of universality, – a state, a single action and the like; Caesar was born at Rome in a given year, spent ten years making war in Gaul, went across the Rubicon and so forth: these are propositions, not judgments. It is, further, wholly empty to say that such propositions as _I_ _slept_ _well_ _last_ _night_, – or: _Present_ _arms!_, _can_ be cast in the form of a judgment. A statement like: a wagon is passing by, – could count as a judgment, and a subjective one, only where it was in doubt whether what moves past is a wagon, or whether it is the object that moves and not rather the standpoint from which we observe it; the interest then lies in finding the determination for [my] representation, which is not yet duly determined.
:::

## {{ 168 }} § 168.

The standpoint of the judgment is _finitude_; and on this standpoint the _finitude_ of things consists in this, that they are a judgment: existence and universal nature (their body and their soul) are admittedly joined, since otherwise things would be nothing, but these moments of theirs are not only already distinct but also separable in general.

## {{ 169 }} § 169.

In the abstract judgment: _the_ _singular_ _is_ _the_ _universal_, the subject, as negatively self-relating, is the immediately _concrete_, whereas the predicate is the _abstract_, the indeterminate, the _universal_. Since the two are connected by _is_, though, the predicate must in its universality also contain the subject's determinateness; this is _particularity_, and {{{ 20.185 }}} it is the _posited_ _identity_ of subject and predicate; and being thus indifferent to this formal difference, it is the _content_.

::: remark
{{ 169 A }} The subject has its explicit determinateness and content only in the predicate; for itself it is therefore a mere representation or an empty name. In the judgments: _God_ is the most real of all and so forth, or the _absolute_ is identical with itself and so forth – _God_ and the _absolute_ are a mere name; what the subject _is_ is said only in the predicate. What else it might be as a concrete is no concern of _this_ judgment (compare § 31).
:::

## {{ 170 }} § 170.

As to the closer determinateness of subject and predicate, the _former_, being the negative relation to itself (§ 163; § 166, Remark), is the fixed substrate in which the predicate subsists and is ideal (it _inheres_ in the subject); and since the subject is concrete as such and _immediately_ so, what the predicate determinately contains figures as just _one_ among the _many_ determinatenesses the subject has, wherefore the subject is richer and wider than the predicate.

The _predicate_, conversely, being the universal, subsists on its own and is indifferent to whether this subject is or not; it reaches beyond the subject and _subsumes_ it, being in its turn the more extensive. The _determinate_ _content_ of the predicate (preceding §) alone constitutes the identity of the two.

## {{ 171 }} § 171.

Subject, predicate and the determinate content, or identity, are at first posited in the judgment, within their very relation, as _different_, as falling apart. _In_ _themselves_, that is, by the concept, they are nonetheless _identical_: the subject's concrete totality consists in not being some indeterminate manifoldness, but solely _singularity_, the particular and the universal in a single identity, and this very unity is the predicate (§ 170.) – In the copula, further, the _identity_ of subject and predicate is indeed _posited_, but at first only as an abstract _is_. By virtue of this _identity_ the subject has to be _posited_ in {{{ 20.186 }}} the predicate's determination as well; the predicate then takes on the subject's determination too, and the copula is _fulfilled_. Through the copula so filled with content the judgment is _further_ _determined_ into the _syllogism_. In the judgment itself, its further determination is the determining of the initially abstract, _sensuous_ _universality_ into _allness_, _genus_ and _species_, and into the fully developed _universality_ _of_ _the_ _concept_.

::: remark
{{ 171 A }} Only cognition of the further determination of the judgment gives both a _coherence_ and a _sense_ to what is usually listed as the _kinds_ of judgment. Apart from the fact that the customary enumeration looks wholly contingent, it is something superficial, even unkempt and wild, in the way it states the differences; how positive, categorical and assertoric judgment are distinguished is in part simply plucked out of the air, in part remains indeterminate. The various judgments are to be regarded as following of necessity from one another and as _one_ _further_ _determining_ _of_ _the_ _concept_, since the judgment itself is nothing but the concept in its _determinate_ form.

In relation to the two spheres that came before, of _being_ and _essence_, the _determinate_ _concepts_ are, as judgments, reproductions of them, though now posited within the simple relation proper to the concept.
:::
