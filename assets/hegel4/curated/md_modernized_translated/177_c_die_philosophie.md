## C. Philosophy

## {{ 572 }} § 572.

To this extent this science is the unity of art and religion: the manner of intuiting of the former, external in form, with its subjective producing and splintering of the substantial content into many self-subsistent shapes, is, in the _totality_ of the latter, with its diverging that unfolds in representation and its mediating of what has unfolded, not merely held together into one whole but also united into the simple spiritual _intuition_ {{{ 20.555 }}} and then raised therein to _self-conscious_ _thinking_. This knowing is thereby the _concept_ of art and religion, cognized in thinking, in which what is different in the content is cognized as necessary, and this necessary as free.

## {{ 573 }} § 573.

Philosophy accordingly takes shape as a cognizing of how necessary the _content_ of the absolute representation is, and how necessary the two _forms_ are: _on_ _the_ _one_ _side_ immediate intuition with its _poetry_ and presupposing representation, that is objective and external _revelation_; _on_ _the_ _other_ first subjective withdrawal into oneself, then the subjective movement toward the presupposition and the identifying of _faith_ with it. This cognizing is thus the _recognizing_ of this content and its form, and the _liberation_ from the one-sidedness of the forms and their elevation into the absolute form, which determines itself to content and remains identical with it, and in this is the cognizing of that necessity that is in and for itself. This movement that philosophy is finds itself already accomplished when philosophy at the close grasps its own concept, that is, only _looks_ _back_ on its knowing.

::: remark
{{ 573 A }} This might seem the place to treat the _relation_ _of_ _philosophy_ _to_ _religion_ in a determinate discussion. What alone matters is the difference between the forms of speculative thinking and the forms of representation and of the reflecting understanding. It is, however, the entire course of philosophy, that of the logic above all, which has not merely brought this difference to light but also passed judgment on it, or rather has let its nature unfold and be set right in these very categories. The true {{{ 20.556 }}} conviction at issue, that the content of philosophy and that of religion are one, leaving aside the further content of external nature and finite spirit, which lies outside the circuit of religion, can be won only out of this cognition of the forms. But religion is the truth _for_ _all_ _human_ _beings_; what faith rests on is the _witness_ _of_ _the_ _spirit_, and this spirit, in bearing witness, is the spirit in the human being. This witness, which is substantial in itself, grasps itself at first, once it is driven to explicate itself, in the culture that otherwise belongs to its worldly consciousness and understanding; thereby the truth falls into the determinations and relations of finitude in general. This does not prevent spirit from holding fast its content, which as religious is essentially speculative, even in the use of sensuous representations and of the finite categories of thinking, against these, from doing violence to them, and from being _inconsistent_ toward them. Through this inconsistency it corrects what is deficient in them; hence nothing comes more easily to the understanding than to show up contradictions in the presentation of faith, and so to prepare triumphs for its principle, formal _identity_. If spirit gives way to that finite reflection, the one that has called itself reason and philosophy (– rationalism), it makes the religious content finite and in fact turns it to nothing. Religion then has its perfect right to guard itself against such reason and philosophy and to declare itself hostile to them. It is another matter, however, when it sets itself against comprehending reason and against philosophy in general, and determinately also against such a philosophy whose content is speculative and thereby religious. Such an opposing rests on the lack of insight into the nature of the stated difference and into the worth of the spiritual forms in general, and especially of the forms of thinking, and most determinately on the lack of insight into the difference of the content from those forms, which can be the same in {{{ 20.557 }}} both. On the ground of form, philosophy has drawn reproaches and accusations from the religious side, and conversely, on account of its speculative content, from a so-called philosophy and likewise from a piety empty of content; to the former it would contain _too_ _little_ of God, to _the_ _latter_ too much.

Seldom heard any more is the accusation of _atheism_, so often brought against philosophy in the past, that it has _too_ _little_ of God; all the more widespread is the accusation of pantheism, that it has _too_ _much_ of him, so widespread that this counts less as an accusation than as an established matter, one needing no proof at all, a sheer _fact_; piety in particular, which in its pious loftiness in any case believes itself relieved of proving, abandons itself, in accord with the empty philosophy of the understanding to which it wishes to be so opposed, yet on whose culture it in fact wholly rests, to the assurance, as if to the mere mention of a known matter, that philosophy is the doctrine of all-one, or pantheism. One must say that it did more honor to piety and to theology themselves to accuse a philosophical system, for instance Spinozism, of atheism than of pantheism, although that accusation looks harsher and more invidious at first sight (compare § 71 Remark). The accusation of atheism does after all presuppose a determinate representation of a God _full_ _of_ _content_, and arises, then, because representation misses in the philosophical concepts the peculiar forms by which it is bound. Philosophy, for its part, is well able to recognize, in the categories of the religious way of representing, its own forms, and thereby its own content in the religious content, and to let this content have justice; the reverse does not hold, because the religious way of representing never turns the critique of thought upon itself and does not comprehend itself, and in its immediacy {{{ 20.558 }}} is therefore exclusive. The accusation of pantheism, raised against philosophy in place of atheism, belongs chiefly to the more recent culture, to the new piety and new theology, for which philosophy has _too_ _much_ _God_, so much that, by its assurance, he is supposed to be even everything, and everything to be God. For in making religion merely a subjective feeling and denying the cognition of God's nature, this new theology keeps for itself nothing further than a God _in_ _general_, without objective determinations. Since the concrete, fulfilled concept of God holds no interest of its own for it, it looks upon such a concept merely as an interest that _others_ once had, and therefore treats whatever belongs to the doctrine of God's concrete nature as something merely _historical_. In all religions the indeterminate God is to be found; every kind of piety (§ 72), the Indian toward apes, cows and so forth, or toward the Dalai Lama, the Egyptian toward the ox and so on, is always veneration of an object that, despite its absurd determinations, also contains the abstract of the genus, _the_ _God_ _in_ _general_. If such a God suffices that view for finding God in everything called religion, then it must at least find him acknowledged in philosophy as well, and can hardly go on accusing philosophy of atheism. Hence the toning down of the reproach of atheism into that of pantheism rests solely on the superficiality of the representation, to which this mildness has thinned God out and emptied him. Since that representation now holds fast to its abstract universality, _outside_ of which all determinateness falls, determinateness is further only the ungodly, the worldly existence of things, which thereby remains in _firm_ _undisturbed_ _substantiality_. On such a presupposition, the _universality_ _that_ _is_ _in_ _and_ _for_ _itself_ which philosophy asserts of God, and in which external things have no truth to their being, leaves matters as they were: it stays the case that, all the same, _the_ _worldly_ _things_ keep _their_ _being_, and that they are what {{{ 20.559 }}} constitutes the determinate element in the divine universality. So they turn that universality into what they call the _pantheistic_ one: that _everything_, that is the empirical things without distinction, the more highly esteemed as well as the common, _is_ and possesses substantiality, and that God is this very being of the worldly things. – Nothing but their own thoughtlessness, and a falsification of concepts that proceeds from it, produces the representation of pantheism and the assurance of it.

But if those who pass off some philosophy as pantheism are neither able nor willing to see this, for it is precisely insight into concepts that they do not want, they would have to ascertain, before all else, merely the _fact_ that _some_ _philosopher_ or _some_ _human_ _being_ has in actuality ascribed to _all_ things a reality, a substantiality, that is in and for itself, and [has] regarded them as God, and that such a representation has entered the head of anyone besides themselves. This fact I will still illuminate in this exoteric consideration; which cannot be done otherwise than by laying the facta themselves before the eyes. If we want to take the so-called pantheism in its poetic, most sublime, or if one likes, crassest shape, one has, as is well known, to look around for it among the _Oriental_ poets, and the broadest presentations are found among the _Indian_. Out of the wealth that has been opened to us on this, I select, from the _Bhagavad-Gita_, which lies before us in the most authentic form, and from among its tirades carried out and repeated to excess, some of the most telling passages. In the tenth lesson (in Schlegel, p. 162) _Krishna_ says of himself:

I am the breath that dwells in the body of the living; I am the beginning, the middle of the living, likewise their end. – I am among the stars the radiant sun, among the lunar signs the moon. Among the holy books the book of hymns, among the senses the sense, the understanding of the living, and so forth. Among the Rudras I am {{{ 20.560 }}} Shiva, Meru among the peaks of the mountains, among the mountains Himalaya and so forth, among the animals the lion and so forth, among the letters I am A., among the seasons I am spring and so forth. I am the seed of all things, there is none that is without me, and so forth.

Even in these entirely sensuous depictions _Krishna_ (and one must not suppose that apart from _Krishna_ there is here still another God or a god; as he said before that he is Shiva, and Indra too, so it is said of him afterwards (11th lesson, p. 15) that Brahma also is in him) passes himself off only as the _most excellent_ of everything, but not as _everything_; everywhere the difference is made between external, inessential existences and an _essential_ one among them, which he is. Even when it is said at the beginning of the passage that he is the beginning, the middle and the end of the living, this totality is distinguished from the living themselves as single existences. Hence even such a depiction, extending divinity widely in its existence, cannot yet be called _pantheism_; one would have to say rather that the infinitely manifold empirical world, the _everything_, has been reduced to a more limited multitude of essential existences, to a _polytheism_. But it already lies in what has been cited that even these substantialities of what exists externally do not retain the self-subsistence required to be called gods; even Shiva, Indra and so forth dissolve in the one Krishna.

This reduction goes on more explicitly in the following depiction (7th lesson, p. 7 ff.); _Krishna_ speaks: The entire world has its _origin_ in me, and its _dissolution_. Nothing more excellent than I exists. The universe hangs upon me as rows of pearls upon a thread. Among the waters I am the taste, in sun and moon the gleam, in all the holy books the mystical name and so forth, in all the living the life and so on, the understanding of the understanding, the strength of the strong, and so forth. He then adds that through _maya_ (Schlegel: Magia), which too is nothing self-subsistent but only his own, through the peculiar qualities, the world, deceived, does not cognize him, _the_ _higher_, _the_ _unchangeable_, that this maya is hard to break through; those who {{{ 20.561 }}} however have a share in him have overcome it, and so forth. – The representation then condenses into the simple utterance: after many rebirths, says Krishna, the one endowed with science comes forward to me; Vasudeva (that is, Krishna) is the _all_, and a great-minded one who holds this conviction is hard to find. Other people turn to other gods; to them I give a reward in keeping with their faith, but what such little-discerning ones receive is limited. The fools take me for _visible_, – _me_ _the_ _invisible_, _imperishable_, _and_ _so_ _forth_. _This_ _all_, as which Krishna declares himself, is just as little the _everything_ as the Eleatic _one_ and the Spinozistic _substance_ are. Rather, this everything, the infinitely many-sided sensuous plurality of the finite, is determined in all these representations as the _accidental_: something that has no being in and for itself and has its truth in the substance, the one, which alone, distinct from that accidental, is to be the divine and God. Indian religion moves on in any case to the representation of _Brahma_, thought's pure unity within itself, wherein the empirical everything of the world vanishes, together with those nearest substantialities that are called gods. For this reason _Colebrooke_ and many others have classed Indian religion, in its essentials, as _monotheism_. The little that has been cited already shows that this classification is not incorrect. But so lacking in concreteness within itself, so to speak so powerless, is this unity of God, of a spiritual God at that, that Indian religion proves a monstrous confusion, being equally the maddest polytheism. But when the wretched Indian worships the ape or whatever else, his idolatry is still not that wretched representation of pantheism, according to which _everything_ is God and God _everything_. Incidentally, Indian monotheism itself illustrates how little mere monotheism achieves where the idea of God lacks deep _determination_ in itself. For to the extent that this unity is abstract in itself and hence empty, it is that very unity which brings it about that the concrete as such stands _outside_ it, whether as a multitude of gods or as empirical, worldly singularities, as {{{ 20.562 }}} self-subsistent. Even that pantheism could, going by its shallow representation, still be called a monotheism; for if on this representation God is identical with the world, then, since there is only one world, this pantheism too would have just one God. The empty numerical unity may be predicated of the world, but this abstract determination holds no further particular interest, – rather this numerical unity is precisely this: to be, in its _content_, the infinite plurality and manifoldness of finitudes. But it is that deception with the empty unity which alone makes possible and brings about the bad representation of a pantheism. Only the representation of the world as _one_ _thing_, _the_ _all_, floating in the indeterminate blue, could be regarded as connectable with God; only on that basis could one come to suppose that what was meant was that God is the world; for had the world been understood as what it is, as everything, the endless multitude of empirical existences, no one, surely, would have thought it even possible that a pantheism existed which declared such a content to be God.

If one wants, to return once more to the factual, to see the consciousness of the one, not in the Indian splitting, on the one hand into the determinationless unity of abstract thinking, on the other into the wearying carrying-through in the particular that itself becomes litany-like, but in its most beautiful purity and sublimity, one must look around among the Muslims. Where, for instance, in the excellent _Jalaluddin_ _Rumi_ the unity of the soul with the One is stressed in particular, this unity too as love, that spiritual unity constitutes an _elevation_ over what is finite and common, a transfiguring of natural and spiritual alike, wherein the external, transitory side of the immediately natural, as of the empirical, worldly spiritual, is precisely excluded and absorbed.[^1]

{{{ 20.563 }}} I refrain from multiplying the examples of the religious and poetic representations that people are accustomed to call pantheistic. Of the philosophies to which this very name has been given, for instance {{{ 20.564 }}} the Eleatic or Spinozistic, it has already been remarked earlier (§ 50 Remark) that they so little identify God with the world and make him finite that in these philosophies this _everything_ rather has no truth, {{{ 20.565 }}} and that, to put it more correctly, they should be labelled _monotheisms_ and, in regard to the representation of the world, _acosmisms_. Most precisely they would be determined as the systems that grasp the absolute only as _substance_. As for the Oriental, especially Muslim ways of representing, it can be added that in them the absolute shows itself as the _utterly_ _universal_ _genus_, indwelling in the species, the existences, yet with the result that to these no actual reality belongs. What all these ways of representing and systems lack is the advance to determining substance as _subject_ and as _spirit_.

These ways of representing and systems start from the single need, common to all philosophies and all religions, of forming a representation of God and then of his _relation_ to the world. Philosophy cognizes more closely that how God stands to the world is determined by what God's nature is determined to be. The reflecting understanding starts by discarding the ways of representing and the systems of heart, of fantasy and of speculation that express the connection of God and the world; and so that God may be had purely in faith or consciousness, God is sundered, as the essence, from appearance, as the infinite from the finite. Once this sundering is done, however, there also enters the conviction of the _relation_ obtaining between appearance and essence, between the finite and the infinite, and so forth, and with it the question, now a reflecting one, about the nature of this {{{ 20.566 }}} relation. The whole difficulty of the matter lies in the form of reflection upon it. This relation is what those who want nothing to do with God's nature call the _incomprehensible_. At the close of philosophy it is no longer the place, nor in an exoteric consideration at all, to waste a word on what _comprehending_ means. But because the grasping of science at large, and every accusation brought against it, hangs together with the grasping of this relation, a further reminder is in order: unity in general is philosophy's concern, yet not abstract unity, mere identity and the empty absolute, but _concrete_ unity (the concept), and in its whole course this alone, – every stage of the advance gives this concrete _unity_ a _distinctive_ _determination_, and the last and deepest of unity's determinations is that of absolute spirit. Those who want to judge philosophy and to express themselves about it could be asked to engage with these _determinations_ of _unity_ and to take pains to know them, or at least to know this much, that there is _a_ _great_ _plurality_ of these determinations and a great diversity among them. They show, however, so little knowledge of this, and still less effort at it, that, as soon as they hear of _unity_, – and the _relation_ at once contains _unity_, – they stop at the entirely abstract, _indeterminate_ _unity_, and abstract from that in which alone all interest falls, namely the manner of determinateness of unity. So the only thing they can say of philosophy is that its principle and result is dry identity, and that it is the identity system. Holding to this conceptless thought of identity, they have grasped of the concrete unity, the concept and the import of {{{ 20.567 }}} philosophy, nothing at all, but rather its opposite. They proceed in this field as the physicists do in the physical one, who likewise know well that they have before them manifold sensuous properties and materials, – or usually _only_ materials (for the properties too are transformed for them into materials), and that these materials, too, are _related_ to one another. The question now is what kind of relation it is, and the peculiarity and the whole difference of all natural, inorganic and living things rests solely on the _different_ _determinateness_ _of_ _this_ _unity_. Instead of cognizing this unity in its different determinatenesses, however, ordinary physics (including chemistry) grasps only the one, the most external, the worst, namely _composition_, applies only it in the whole series of natural formations, and thereby makes it impossible for itself to grasp any one of them. – That stale pantheism issues so directly from that stale identity; those who turn this product of their own into an accusation against philosophy gather, from considering the _relation_ of God to the world, that in this category, _relation_, the one is also merely the _one_ _moment_, and indeed the moment of indeterminateness, _identity_; now they remain at this halfway grasp and claim, falsely in point of fact, that philosophy maintains an identity between God and the world; and because both, God as much as the world, have firm substantiality for them, what they work out is that in the philosophical idea God is _composed_ of God and the world; that is then the picture they form of pantheism and pin upon philosophy. Those who in their thinking and grasping of thoughts do not get beyond such categories, and from them, which they carry into philosophy where nothing of the kind is present, attach the scab to it in order to be able to scratch it, avoid all the difficulties that emerge in grasping the relation of God to the world, immediately and very easily, by admitting that this relation contains for them a contradiction of which they understand nothing; {{{ 20.568 }}} so they have to let it rest with the entirely _indeterminate_ _representation_ of such a relation and likewise of its closer ways, for instance omnipresence, providence and so forth. _Faith_ in this sense means nothing other than not proceeding to a determinate representation, not wanting to engage further with the content. That human beings and estates of uneducated understanding are satisfied with indeterminate representations is in keeping; but when cultivated understanding and interest in reflective consideration want to content themselves with indeterminate representations in what is recognized as the higher and highest interest, it is hard to tell whether spirit is in fact in _earnest_ about the content. But if those who cling to the stated bare understanding were in _earnest_, for instance, with the assertion of the _omnipresence_ of God, in the sense of making the faith in it present to themselves in a determinate representation, into what difficulty would the faith they have in the _true_ _reality_ of sensuous things entangle them? They would hardly want to let God dwell, as Epicurus did, in the interstices of things, that is, in the _pores_ of the physicists, which pores are the negative that is supposed to be _next_ _to_ the materially real. This _next_ _to_ already gives them a pantheism of spatiality of their own, an everything determined as space's outside-one-anotherness. Yet were they to credit God with efficacy on filled space and in it, on the world and in the world, within his relation to it, divine actuality would be splintered infinitely into infinite materiality, and the bad representation they call pantheism or doctrine of all-one would be theirs, in fact merely as the necessary consequence of their own bad representations of God and the world. To impose on philosophy anything like the much-discussed unity or identity is, however, so great a carelessness about justice and truth that it could be made comprehensible only by the difficulty of getting thoughts and concepts, that is, not the abstract unity but the many-shaped ways of its determinateness, into one's head. When assertions about fact are put forward, {{{ 20.569 }}} and the facta are thoughts and concepts, it is indispensable to grasp them. But even the fulfilling of this demand has been made superfluous by the fact that it has long since become a _settled_ prejudice that philosophy is pantheism, a system of identity, doctrine of all-one, so that anyone who did not know this fact would be treated either as merely ignorant of a known matter, or as seeking evasions for some purpose or other. – On account of this chorus, then, I have believed I must explain myself at greater length and exoterically about the outer and inner untruth of this alleged fact; for about the external grasping of concepts as mere facta, whereby the concepts are precisely turned into their opposite, one can at first also speak only exoterically. The esoteric consideration, however, of God and of identity, as of cognizing and of concepts, is philosophy itself.

## {{ 574 }} § 574.

Understood so, the concept of philosophy is _the_ _idea_ _that_ _thinks_ _itself_, the knowing truth (§ 236): the logical, in the significance of a universality that has _proved_ _itself_ in concrete content, its actuality. Science has thereby returned to its beginning; the logical, as the _spiritual_, is accordingly its _result_, in that out of the judging that presupposes, wherein the concept was merely _in_ _itself_ and the beginning something immediate, and hence out of the _appearance_ it had in this judging, the logical has raised itself into its pure principle, which is at once its element.

## {{ 575 }} § 575.

This appearing is what first grounds the further development. The _syllogism_ taking the _logical_ for its ground, its starting point, and _nature_ for its middle, which closes _spirit_ together with {{{ 20.570 }}} the logical, constitutes the first appearance. The logical becomes nature, and nature becomes spirit. Standing between spirit and its essence, nature does not, to be sure, drive them apart into extremes of finite abstraction, nor detach itself from them as something self-subsistent that, being an other, would join only others together; the syllogism is _in_ _the_ _idea_, after all, and nature is determined essentially as no more than a point of passage and a negative moment, being _in_ _itself_ the idea; yet the concept's mediation wears the external form of _passing_ _over_, and science that of a course of necessity, with the result that the concept's freedom, its closing together with itself, is posited in only the one extreme.

## {{ 576 }} § 576.

This appearance is sublated in the _second_ _syllogism_ in so far as this is already the standpoint of spirit itself, which is the mediating element of the process, _presupposes_ nature and closes it together with the _logical_. It is the syllogism of spiritual _reflection_ in the idea; science appears as a subjective _cognizing_, whose purpose is freedom and which is itself the way of producing it.

## {{ 577 }} § 577.

The idea of philosophy is the third syllogism; _self-knowing_ _reason_, the absolutely universal, forms its _middle_, dividing itself into _spirit_ and _nature_, so that the former, as the process of the idea's _subjective_ activity, becomes the presupposition, and the latter, as the process {{{ 20.571 }}} of the idea that is _in_ _itself_ and objective, the universal extreme. By the idea's _self-judging_ into the two appearances (§§ 575, 576), it fixes them as _its_ manifestations, those of self-knowing reason, and in it this is united: that it is the nature of the matter, the concept, which moves on and develops, that this movement is just as much the activity of cognizing, and that the eternal idea, being in and for itself, eternally activates, begets and enjoys itself as absolute spirit.

Who will recognize in this poetry, soaring above the external and sensuous, the prosaic representation that is made of so-called pantheism, and that rather degrades the divine into the external and sensuous? The rich communications that Mr. _Tholuck_ gives, in his writing: _Collection_ _of_ _Blossoms_ _from_ _Oriental_ _Mysticism_, of the poems of Jalaluddin and others, are made precisely from the standpoint of which we are speaking here. In the introduction Mr. Th. shows how deeply his heart has grasped mysticism: there he also determines more closely the character of the Oriental, and that of the Western and Christian in contrast to it. Despite their difference they have the common determination of being mysticism. According to him (p. 33), the connection of mysticism with so-called pantheism embraces the inner liveliness of heart and spirit, which consists essentially in annihilating that external _all_ that is wont to be ascribed to pantheism. For the rest Mr. Th. contents himself with the usual unclear representation of pantheism; a more thorough discussion of it held, to begin with, no interest for the author's standpoint of feeling; one sees him himself, however, seized by a mysticism, to be called entirely pantheistic in the ordinary expression, of marvelous inspiration. Where, however, he engages in philosophizing (p. 12 f.), he does not get beyond the ordinary standpoint of the metaphysics of the understanding and its uncritical categories.
