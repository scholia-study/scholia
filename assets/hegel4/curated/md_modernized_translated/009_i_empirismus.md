## I. Empiricism

## {{ 37 }} § 37.

The need, partly of a _concrete_ content against the abstract theories of the understanding, which cannot advance by itself from its universalities to particularization and determination, partly of a _firm_ _hold_ against the possibility of being able to _prove_ _everything_ on the field and by the method of finite determinations, led first of all to _empiricism_, which, instead of seeking the true in thought itself, goes to fetch it from _experience_, from outer and inner presence.

## {{ 38 }} § 38.

_Empiricism_ has this source in common, on the one side, with metaphysics itself, which likewise has representations, that is, the content derived first from experience, as warrant for the authentication of its definitions – of the presuppositions as well as of the more determinate content. On the other side, experience is something other than individual perception, and empiricism elevates the content that pertains to perception, feeling and intuition into the _form_ of _universal_ _representations_, _propositions_ and _laws_ and so forth. This happens, however, only in the sense that these universal determinations (for instance force) are to have no further meaning and validity for themselves than the one taken from perception, and no connection other than one demonstrable in appearance is to have justification. Empirical cognition has its firm {{{ 20.76 }}} hold on the _subjective_ side in that consciousness in perception has its _own_ _immediate_ _presence_ and _certainty_.

::: remark
{{ 38 A }} A great principle lies in empiricism: whatever is true must exist in actuality and be present to perception. This principle stands against the _ought_, on which reflection prides itself while looking down on actuality and the present from a _beyond_ that is supposed to have its seat and existence only in the subjective understanding. Like empiricism, philosophy too cognizes (§ 6) only what _is_; it knows nothing of what only _ought_ to be and so _is_ _not_ _there_. – On the subjective side one must likewise recognize the important principle of _freedom_ that lies in empiricism, namely that human beings are to see _for_ _themselves_ what they are to let count in their knowledge, to know _themselves_ _present_ in it. – The _consistent_ carrying-through of empiricism, insofar as it restricts itself in content to the finite, however denies the supersensible in general, or at least cognition and determinateness of it, and allows thinking only abstraction and formal universality and identity. – The basic delusion in scientific empiricism is always this: that it uses the metaphysical categories of matter, force, and moreover of one, many, universality, also of the infinite and so forth, further _infers_ onward along the thread of such categories, thereby presupposing and applying the forms of inference, and in all this does not know that it itself contains and practices metaphysics, and uses those categories and their connections in a wholly uncritical and unconscious manner.
:::

## {{ 39 }} § 39.

Concerning this principle, the correct reflection was made first of all that in what is called _experience_, and is to be distinguished from mere individual perception of individual facts, _two_ _elements_ are found – the one the _material_, isolated on its own account and infinitely _manifold_, – the other {{{ 20.77 }}} the _form_ – the determinations of _universality_ and _necessity_. Empirical observation certainly shows many, perhaps countless, like perceptions; but _universality_ is something wholly different from a great multitude. Likewise empirical observation certainly affords perceptions of _successive_ alterations or of _side-by-side_ objects, but not a connection _of_ _necessity_. Since perception is now supposed to remain the basis of what is to count as truth, universality and necessity appear as something _unjustified_, as a subjective contingency, a mere habit, whose content can be constituted one way or another.

::: remark
{{ 39 A }} A significant corollary is that, on this empirical approach, the determinations and laws of right and of ethics, together with the content of religion, come to look contingent, and their objectivity and inner truth are forfeited.

The _Humean_ skepticism, from which the above reflection chiefly proceeds, is moreover to be carefully distinguished from _Greek_ _skepticism_. The Humean takes the _truth_ of the empirical, of feeling, of intuition as its basis, and from there contests the universal determinations and laws, on the ground that they have no justification through sense-perception. Ancient skepticism was so far from making feeling and intuition the principle of truth that it turned rather first of all against the sensuous. (On modern skepticism in comparison with the ancient, see Schelling's and Hegel's Critical Journal of Philosophy, 1802, vol. 1, no. 1.)
:::
