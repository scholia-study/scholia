## {{{ 20.5 }}} Preface to the Second Edition

The kind reader will find in this new edition several parts reworked and developed into more precise determinations; in doing so I have endeavoured to soften and reduce the formal aspect of the presentation, and also, by means of more extensive exoteric remarks, to bring abstract concepts closer to ordinary comprehension and to the more concrete representations of them. The compressed brevity that an outline requires, in matters abstruse enough to begin with, nonetheless leaves this second edition the same destination that the first had, namely to serve as a book to lecture from, which has to receive its necessary elucidation through oral delivery. The title of an _Encyclopaedia_ was, to be sure, initially meant to leave room for a lesser strictness of scientific method and for an external juxtaposition; but the nature of the matter brings with it that logical connection had to remain the foundation.

There would be only too many occasions and incitements that seemed to make it necessary for me to explain myself about the external standing of my philosophizing in relation to spiritual and spiritless pursuits of the culture of the age; which can be done only in an exoteric manner, as in a preface; for these pursuits, although they give themselves a relation to philosophy, do not engage with it scientifically, and so not with it at all, but conduct their chatter from the outside and out there. It is distasteful and even precarious to enter upon such ground foreign to science, for such explaining and discussing does not further that comprehension on which alone genuine cognition can depend. But to discuss a few appearances may be useful or necessary.

What I have worked toward in my philosophical efforts, and continue to work toward, is the scientific cognition of truth. It is the hardest path, but the only one that can have interest and worth for spirit {{{ 20.6 }}}, once spirit has set out on the path of thought, has not lapsed on it into vanity, but has preserved the will and the courage for truth; it soon finds that method alone is able to tame thought, to lead it to the matter and to keep it there. Such a carrying forward proves itself to be nothing other than the restoration of that absolute import beyond which thought at first strove and set itself, but a restoration in the most proper, freest element of spirit.

Not very long ago an unselfconscious, seemingly happy state came to its end, in which philosophy walked alongside the sciences and culture; a moderate enlightenment of the understanding contented the need for insight and religion alike, a natural right was compatible with state and politics, and the name of natural philosophy was borne by empirical physics. The peace was superficial enough, however, and that insight in particular stood in inner contradiction with religion, as did this natural right with the state, in fact. Then the separation ensued, the contradiction developed; but in philosophy spirit has celebrated its reconciliation with itself, with the result that this science has to do solely with that contradiction itself and with its whitewash, in contradiction. It is one of the bad prejudices to suppose that it stands in opposition to a thoughtful empirical knowledge, the rational actuality of right, and an unselfconscious religion and piety; these shapes are acknowledged by philosophy, even justified; the thinking sense rather immerses itself in their import, learns from them and corroborates itself in them as in the great intuitions of nature, history and art; the speculative idea itself is this solid content once it is thought. The collision with philosophy occurs only insofar as this ground steps out of its own character, and its content is to be grasped in categories and made dependent on them, without leading these to the concept and completing them into the idea.

The important negative result in which the understanding of general scientific culture finds itself, that no mediation with truth is possible by way of the finite concept, tends, namely, to have the opposite consequence from the one lying immediately in it. That conviction has namely done away with interest in the investigation of the categories, {{{ 20.7 }}} and with attention and caution in their application, instead of bringing about the removal of finite relations from cognition; their use has become, as in a state of despair, all the more undisguised, unconscious and uncritical. From the misunderstanding that the insufficiency of the finite categories for truth brings with it the impossibility of objective cognition, there is inferred the entitlement to speak and to pronounce from feeling and subjective opinion, and in place of proving there come assurances and narrations of what is found in consciousness as facts, which is deemed purer the less critical it is. The highest needs of spirit are supposed to rest on a category as barren as _immediacy_, left uninvestigated, and to be decided by it. One can find, especially where religious objects are treated, that philosophizing is expressly set aside, as if all evil were hereby banished and security against error and deception attained, and then the investigation of truth is carried out from presuppositions made from somewhere or other and by ratiocination, that is, by employing the ordinary determinations of thought, essence and appearance, ground and consequence, cause and effect and so on, and by the usual drawing of inferences in keeping with these and the other relations of finitude. »They are rid of the evil one, the evil has remained,« and the evil has become nine times as bad as it was, because people entrust themselves to it without any suspicion and criticism; and as if that evil which is kept at a distance, philosophy, were anything other than the investigation of truth, but with consciousness about the nature and worth of the relations of thought that connect and determine all content.

The worst fate that philosophy itself has to undergo at those hands is when they busy themselves with it, and partly grasp it, partly judge it. It is the _fact_ of physical or spiritual vitality, in particular religious vitality too, that is distorted by that reflection, which is incapable of grasping it. This grasping has, however, the sense in itself of first raising the fact to something known, and the difficulty lies in this transition from the matter to cognition, which is effected by reflective thinking. This difficulty is no longer present in science itself. For the fact of philosophy is cognition already prepared, and grasping would hence be only a _reflective_ _thinking_ in the sense of a {{{ 20.8 }}} _following_ thinking; only judging would require reflective thinking in the ordinary meaning. But that uncritical understanding proves just as unfaithful in the bare grasping of the idea determinately stated; it has so little suspicion or doubt about the fixed presuppositions it contains that it cannot so much as restate the mere fact of the philosophical idea. This understanding strangely unites in itself the twofold: that what strikes it in the idea is the complete deviation and even the express contradiction to its use of the categories, and that at the same time it never suspects that another way of thinking is present and practised than its own, and that it must therefore comport itself here as thinking otherwise than usual. In such a way it happens that the idea of speculative philosophy is at once held fast in its abstract definition, in the belief that a definition must show itself clear and settled by itself and finds its regulator and touchstone only in presupposed representations, at least in ignorance that the sense, like the necessary proof, of the definition lies solely in its development and in its issuing from this as result. Since now, more closely, the idea in general is the _concrete_, _spiritual_ unity, while the understanding consists in grasping the determinations of the concept only in their _abstraction_ and so in their one-sidedness and finitude, that unity gets turned into an abstract, spiritless identity, in which, it is thus alleged, difference is not present, but _everything_ is _one_, among other things good and evil too are all the same. For speculative philosophy the name _system_ _of_ _identity_, _philosophy_ _of_ _identity_ has therefore already become a received name. If someone were to make his profession of faith, I believe in God the Father, creator of heaven and earth, one would be amazed if somebody else concluded from this first article alone that the believer trusts in a God who made heaven, _therefore_ takes the earth to be uncreated and matter eternal. That the former has declared in his creed a belief in God as maker of heaven is a correct fact, and nonetheless the fact as the other has taken it is utterly false; to such a degree that this example has to be considered unbelievable and trivial. And yet the case with the grasping of the philosophical idea is this violent halving, so that, in order that one cannot misunderstand what the identity, which is supposedly the principle of speculative philosophy, is like, the express instruction and, as the case may be, refutation follows, say that the subject {{{ 20.9 }}} is _different_ from the object, and the finite from the infinite, and so on, as though the concrete spiritual unity lacked all determination in itself and did not itself _contain_ _difference_ _in_ _itself_, as if any human being did not know that the subject is different from the object, the infinite from the finite, or philosophy, sunk in its school-wisdom, had to be reminded that outside the school there is a wisdom to which that diversity is something familiar.

While philosophy is thus more specifically vilified in respect of the diversity that it is supposed not to know, to the effect that in it the distinction of good and evil too falls away, equity and magnanimity are gladly practised by conceding »that philosophers in their expositions do not always develop (– so then perhaps also for this reason not, because these consequences do not belong to them –) the pernicious consequences that are _bound_ up with their proposition.«[^1] Philosophy must {{{ 20.10 }}} spurn this mercy which one is willing to bestow on it, for it has no more need of it for its moral justification than it can be short of insight into the real consequences of its principles, and it is no less ready with the express inferences. {{{ 20.11 }}} I will cast a brief light on that alleged consequence, by which the diversity of good and evil is supposed to be made a mere shine, less to defend philosophy than to give an instance of how hollow such a way of grasping it is. For this purpose let us take up merely Spinozism, that philosophy in which God receives the determination of _substance_ alone, not of subject and spirit. The distinction in question touches the _determination_ of unity; this alone matters, yet of this _determination_, although it is a fact, those know nothing who are wont to call this philosophy a system of identity, and may even use the expression that according to it _everything_ is _one_ _and_ _the_ _same_, good and evil too _equal_, – all of which are the worst ways of unity, of which there can be no talk in speculative philosophy, but which only a still barbarous thinking can make use of in ideas. As regards the claim that in that philosophy _in_ _itself_ or _properly_ the diversity of good and evil does not hold, we must ask what, then, the word _properly_ means. If it means the nature of God, it surely will not be demanded that evil be located in it; the substantial unity there is the good itself; evil is nothing but disunion; in that unity there is hence nothing less than a sameness of good and evil, the latter rather being excluded. Thereby there is in God as such just as little the distinction of good and evil; for this distinction is only in what is disunited, in one in which evil itself is. Further, in Spinozism there also occurs the distinction, _man_ _different_ _from_ _God_. The system may be theoretically unsatisfying in this respect; for man and the finite in general, even if it is afterwards reduced to a mode, _is_ _found_ in the consideration only _alongside_ the substance. Here, then, in man, where the distinction exists, it is that it exists also essentially as the distinction {{{ 20.12 }}} of good and evil, and here alone is it that it is _properly_, for here alone is its proper determination. If one has in view in Spinozism only the substance, there is indeed in it no distinction of good and evil, but because evil, like the finite and the _world_ in general (see § 50, Remark, p. 60), _is_ _not_ _at_ _all_ on this standpoint. But if one has in view the standpoint on which in this system man and the relation of man to substance also occur, and where only evil in its distinction from good can have its place, one must have consulted the parts of the Ethics that deal with the latter, with the affects, human servitude and human freedom, in order to be able to tell of the moral consequences of the system. Without doubt one will be convinced of the high purity of this morality, whose principle is the pure love of God, as well as that this purity of morality follows from the system. In his time _Lessing_ remarked that people treat Spinoza like a dead dog; it cannot be said that in more recent times Spinozism, and then speculative philosophy in general, is dealt with better, when one sees that those who report and judge about it do not even trouble themselves to get the facts right, and to state and relate them accurately. This would be the minimum of justice, and such at any rate it could demand.

Philosophy's history is the history of how the _thoughts_ about the absolute, its object, were discovered. Socrates, for instance, one can say, discovered the determination of _purpose_, which Plato, and in particular Aristotle, developed and cognized in determinate form. _Brucker's_ history of philosophy is uncritical to such a degree, not only in the external side of what is historical but in the reporting of thoughts, that of the older Greek philosophers one finds twenty, thirty and more propositions listed as their philosophemes, not one of which is theirs. These are inferences that Brucker draws in [the manner] of the bad metaphysics of his age and foists on those philosophers as their assertions. Inferences are of two kinds, partly mere elaborations of a principle down into further detail, partly however a regress to deeper principles; the historical consists precisely in stating to which individuals such a {{{ 20.13 }}} further deepening of thought and the unveiling of it belongs. But that procedure is improper not simply because those philosophers themselves drew no such consequences from what their principles are supposed to contain, and so merely failed to state them expressly, but rather because such inferring flatly imputes to them an allowing to hold and a use of thought-relations of finitude that are directly contrary to the sense of the philosophers who were of speculative spirit and that rather only contaminate and falsify the philosophical idea. If, in the case of ancient philosophies, of which only a few propositions are reported to us, such falsification has the excuse of the supposedly correct inference, that excuse lapses where a philosophy has itself partly grasped its idea in determinate thoughts, partly expressly investigated and determined the worth of the categories, if nonetheless the idea is grasped in mutilated form, only One moment is taken out of the exposition and (like identity) passed off for the totality, and if the categories are brought in quite unselfconsciously in the nearest handy manner in which they run through everyday consciousness, in their one-sidedness and untruth. The cultivated cognition of the relations of thought is the first condition for grasping a philosophical fact correctly. But the crudity of thought is by the principle of immediate knowing not only justified but made into law; the cognition of thoughts and with it the cultivation of subjective thinking is as little an immediate knowing as any science or art and skill.

Religion is the mode of consciousness in which truth exists for all human beings, for human beings of every culture; scientific cognition of truth, however, is a particular mode of its consciousness, whose labor not all but only few undertake. _The_ _import_ _is_ _the_ _same_, but just as, in Homer, some things have two names, one used among the gods and the other in the speech of the creatures of a day, so for that import there are two languages, one of feeling, of representation and of understanding thinking that nests in finite categories and one-sided abstractions, the other of the concrete concept. If one wants to discuss and judge philosophy from the standpoint of religion, more is required than merely to have a familiarity with the language of {{{ 20.14 }}} the consciousness of a day. The foundation of scientific cognition is the inner import, the indwelling idea and its lively activity stirring in spirit, no less than religion is a worked-through heart, a spirit awakened to reflection, a cultivated import. In the most recent time religion has contracted ever more the cultivated expansion of its content and withdrawn into the intensive of piety or feeling, and often indeed of a feeling that manifests a very meagre and bare import. So long as it has a credo, a doctrine, a dogmatics, it has that with which philosophy occupies itself and in which, as such, it can unite with religion. This, however, is again not to be taken according to the separating bad understanding in which modern religiosity is entangled, and according to which it represents the two as mutually exclusive, or as so separable in general that they come together only afterwards, from outside. Rather it also lies in what has gone before that religion can well be without philosophy, but philosophy not without religion, which it rather includes within itself. True religion, the religion of spirit, must have such a thing, a content; spirit is essentially consciousness, hence of the content made object; as feeling it is the non-objective content itself (it only _quals_, to use an expression of Jacob Böhme's) and only the lowest stage of consciousness, indeed in the form of the soul shared with the animal. _Thinking_ first makes the soul, with which the animal too is endowed, into spirit, and philosophy is only a consciousness about that content, spirit and its truth, also in the shape and manner of that essential character of spirit by which it is set off from the animal and becomes capable of religion. The contracted religiosity, punctualizing itself on the heart, must make the heart's contrition and crushing an essential moment of its rebirth; but it ought at the same time to remember that it has to do with the heart of a spirit, that spirit is appointed as the power of the heart and can be this power only insofar as it is itself reborn. Spirit's rebirth out of natural ignorance and equally out of natural error takes place through instruction and through the faith, brought about by the testimony of spirit, in _objective_ {{{ 20.15 }}} _truth_, in the content. This rebirth of spirit is among other things also immediately the rebirth of the heart from the vanity of one-sided understanding, which prides itself on knowing things like these: that the finite is distinct from the infinite, that philosophy has to be either polytheism or, among keen-thinking spirits, pantheism, and so on – the rebirth from such wretched insights, upon which pious humility toward philosophy as toward theological cognition struts. If religiosity persists in its intensity, devoid of expansion and therefore of spirit, it knows, to be sure, only of the opposition of this its narrow and narrowing form to the spiritual expansion of religious doctrine as such, as of philosophical doctrine.[^2] {{{ 20.16 }}} But not only does thinking spirit not confine itself to satisfaction in the purer, unselfconscious religiosity, but that standpoint is in itself a result issued from reflection and ratiocination; it is with the help of superficial understanding that it has procured for itself this lofty liberation from as good as all doctrine, and in that it uses the thinking by which it is infected to zeal against philosophy, it is thus that it maintains itself by force on the thin contentless point of an abstract state of feeling. – I cannot refrain from quoting in excerpt the exhortation of Mr. _Fr._ _von_ _Bader_ on such a configuration of piety, from the Fermenta Cognitionis, fifth issue, preface, page IX and following.

So long, he says, as to religion, to its doctrines, there has not again been procured by science a respect founded on free inquiry and hence genuine conviction, – so long will you, pious and non-pious, with all your commandments and prohibitions, with all your talk and doing – not remedy the evil, and so long will also this religion, unrespected, not be loved, because one can love heartily and {{{ 20.17 }}} correctly only what one sees sincerely respected and recognizes without doubt as respectable, just as religion too can be served only by such an amor generosus. – In other words: you who want the practice of religion to thrive again, take care that we regain a rational theory of it, and do not entirely yield the field to your opponents (the atheists) with that _irrational_ and _blasphemous_ assertion: that such a theory of religion is wholly not to be thought of, as an impossible thing, that religion is a mere matter of the heart, in which one may well, indeed must, divest oneself of the head.[^3] –

As to the poverty of content, it may still be remarked that it can be spoken of only as the appearance in the external condition of religion at a particular time. Such a time could be lamented, if it is so needful merely to produce the bare faith in God, which was so much a concern of the noble _Jacobi_, and further only to awaken a concentrated Christianness of sensation; the higher principles, however, are not to be mistaken, which manifest themselves even in this (see Introduction to the Logic, § 64, Remark). But before science lies the rich content that centuries and millennia of cognitive activity have brought forth before it, and it lies before it not as something historical that only _others_ possessed, and for us a thing of the past, only an occupation for the knowledge of memory and for the acuteness of criticizing the narratives, not for the cognition of spirit and the interest of truth. The most sublime, deepest and innermost has been brought to light, in religions, philosophies and works of art, in purer and less pure, clearer and murkier, often very repellent shape. It is to be esteemed a special merit that Mr. _Franz_ _von_ _Bader_ continues to bring such forms not only to recollection, but with deeply speculative spirit expressly to bring their import to {{{ 20.18 }}} scientific honors, in that he expounds and corroborates the philosophical idea from them. _Jacob_ _Böhme's_ depth in particular affords occasion and forms for this. To this mighty spirit the name philosophus teutonicus has rightly been given; he has partly expanded the import of religion for itself into the universal idea, conceived in it the highest problems of reason, and sought to grasp in it spirit and nature in their more determinate spheres and configurations, in that he took as foundation that after the image of God, indeed of no other than the _triune_, the spirit of man and all things are created, and are only this life, to be redintegrated to it out of the loss of their archetype; partly, conversely, he has forced the forms of natural things (sulfur, saltpetre and the like, the harsh, the bitter and the like) into service as spiritual forms and forms of thought. The gnosis of Mr. von Bader, which attaches itself to such configurations, is a peculiar way of kindling and furthering philosophical interest; it sets itself powerfully both against contentment with the content-empty bareness of enlightenment-mongering and against the piety that wants to remain merely intensive. Mr. von Bader proves in all his writings that he is far from taking this gnosis as the exclusive mode of cognition. It has its inconveniences in itself, its metaphysics does not push on to the consideration of the categories themselves and to the methodical development of the content; it suffers from the unsuitability of the concept to such wild or ingenious forms and configurations; as it suffers in general from having the absolute content as _presupposition_, and explaining, ratiocinating and refuting from that.[^4]

{{{ 20.19 }}} In purer and murkier configurations of truth we have, one may say, _enough_ _and_ _to_ _spare_, – in the religions and mythologies, in the gnostic and mystifying philosophies of older and newer times; one can take delight in making the _discovery_ of the idea in these configurations and in drawing satisfaction from seeing that philosophical truth is no merely solitary thing, its efficacy having been at work in them at least as a ferment. But when the conceit of immaturity, as was the case with an imitator of Mr. von B., gets to warming up such products of fermentation, it easily raises {{{ 20.20 }}} in its sloth and incapacity for scientific thinking such gnosis into the exclusive mode of cognizing; for it is less laborious to roam in such constructs and to attach assertoric philosophemes to them, than to undertake the development of the concept and to subject one's thinking, like one's heart, to its logical necessity. It also comes naturally to conceit to ascribe to itself as a discovery what it has learned from others, and it believes this the more easily when it combats or belittles them; or is rather therefore irritated against them because it has drawn its insights from them.

As in the appearances of the age to which we have in this preface paid regard the urgency of thinking, although distorted, announces itself, so it is in and for itself a need for the thought cultivated up to the height of spirit and for its time, and therefore alone worthy of our science, that what was earlier revealed as mystery, but in the purer and still more in the murkier configurations of its revelation remains something mysterious to formal thought, be revealed for thinking itself, which in the absolute right of its freedom maintains the obstinacy of reconciling itself with the solid content only insofar as this has known how to give itself the shape at once most worthy of itself, that of the concept, of necessity, which binds everything, content as well as thoughts, and precisely therein makes free. If the old is to be renewed, that is, an old configuration, for the import itself is eternally young, then the configuration of the idea such as _Plato_ and, far more deeply, _Aristotle_ gave it is infinitely more worthy of recollection, also because the unveiling of it through appropriation to our cultivation of thought is immediately not only an understanding of it but an advance of science itself. But to understand such forms of the idea likewise does not lie so on the surface as to grasp gnostic and kabbalistic phantasmagorias, and it goes still less of itself to develop those further than to point out or indicate in these echoes of the idea.

The true has rightly been called index sui et falsi, whereas from the false the true is not known; in the same way the concept understands itself and the shape devoid of concept, whereas that shape, starting from its inner truth, does not understand the concept. Science understands feeling and faith, but it can be judged only from the concept, on which it rests, and since it is the concept's self-development, a {{{ 20.21 }}} judging of it from the concept is not so much a judging of it as a progressing along with it. Such judging I must wish also for this attempt, as I can esteem and heed only such.

_Berlin_, 25 May 1827.

[^1]: Words of Mr. _Tholuk_ in the Anthology of Blossoms from Oriental Mysticism, p. 13. Even the deeply feeling Tholuk allows himself to be led there to follow the common highway of the grasping of philosophy. The understanding, he says, can infer in only the following two ways: either there is an all-conditioning primal ground, then the ultimate ground of my own self lies in it as well, and my being and free action are mere deception; or I am really a being different from the primal ground, whose acting is not conditioned and effected by the primal ground, then the primal ground is no absolute, all-conditioning being, hence there would be no infinite God, but a multitude of gods and so forth. All the more deeply and keenly thinking philosophers are supposed to profess the first proposition (I do not see just why the first one-sidedness should be deeper and keener than the second); the consequences, which as mentioned above they nonetheless do not always develop, are said to be »that the ethical standard of man too is not absolutely true, but _properly_ (underlined by the author himself) good and evil are the same and different only in semblance.« It would always be better to say nothing at all about philosophy, so long as one, for all one's depth of feeling, remains so caught up in the one-sidedness of the understanding as to know only of the _Either_ _Or_ of a primal ground in which individual being and its freedom are only a deception, and of the absolute self-subsistence of individuals, and to have learned nothing of the _Neither_ _Nor_ of these two one-sidednesses of the dilemma that Mr. Th. calls dangerous. To be sure, on p. 14 he speaks of such spirits, and these, he says, are the genuine philosophers, who adopt the second proposition (this is surely the same as what was earlier called the first proposition) and sublate the opposition of _unconditioned_ and conditioned being through the _indifferent_ _primal_ _being_, in which all relative oppositions interpenetrate. But did Mr. Th., in speaking thus, not notice that the indifferent primal being, wherein the opposition is to interpenetrate, is quite identical with that unconditioned being the sublation of whose one-sidedness was intended, and that he thus pronounces in one breath the sublating of that one-sided in something which is precisely this very one-sided, hence, instead of sublating, the letting-stand of the one-sidedness. If one wants to say what _spirits_ do, one must be able to grasp the fact with spirit; otherwise the fact has meanwhile become false. – Moreover I remark superfluously that what is said here and further on about Mr. Tholuk's representation of philosophy cannot and is not meant to bear, as it were, on him _individually_; the same is read in a hundred books, among others especially in the prefaces of theologians. I have cited the presentation of Mr. Th. partly because it happened to be nearest to me, partly because the deep feeling that seems to place his writings on the quite other side from understanding-theology stands nearest to profundity; for the fundamental determination of the latter, _reconciliation_, which is not the unconditioned primal being and the like abstraction, is the import itself that is the speculative idea and that it expresses in thinking – an import that that deep sense ought least of all to fail to recognize in the idea. But it happens to Mr. Tholuk there, as everywhere else in his writings, that he lets himself go in the current talk of _pantheism_, about which I have spoken at greater length in one of the last Remarks of the Encyclopaedia. Here I remark only the peculiar clumsiness and perversion into which Mr. Th. falls. In placing the primal ground on the one side of his supposedly philosophical dilemma, and afterwards designating that side on pp. 33 and 38 as pantheistic, he characterizes the other as that of the Socinians, Pelagians and popular philosophers in such a way that on it »an infinite God does not exist, only a _great_ _number_ of gods, that is, the sum of _all_ those beings which differ from the so-called primal ground and possess a _being_ and acting of their own, together with that same so-called primal ground.« In fact it is not merely a great number of gods that there are on this side, but _everything_ (here everything finite is taken to have a _being_ of its own) _is_ _gods_; on this side Mr. Th. thus in fact has his _all-godism_, his _pantheism_ expressly, not on the first, to whose God he expressly makes the _One_ primal ground, where there is thus only _monotheism_.

[^2]: To return once more to Mr. _Tholuk_, who may be regarded as the inspired representative of the pietistic tendency, the lack of a _doctrine_ in his writing »_On_ _the_ _Doctrine_ _of_ _Sin_,« second edition (which has just come before my eyes) is conspicuous. His treatment of the doctrine of the Trinity in his writing, _The_ _Speculative_ _Doctrine_ _of_ _the_ _Trinity_ _of_ _the_ _Later_ _Orient_, for whose diligently drawn-out historical notices I am sincerely grateful to him, had struck me; he calls this doctrine a _scholastic_ doctrine; at any rate it is much older than what is called scholastic; he considers it solely from the external side of a supposedly merely historical origin from speculation on biblical passages and under the sway of Platonic and Aristotelian philosophy (p. 41). But in the writing on sin he deals with this dogma, one is tempted to say, cavalierement, declaring it capable only of being a _framework_ in which the doctrine of faith (which one?) can be arranged (p. 220), indeed one must also apply to this dogma the expression (p. 219) that it appears to those standing on the shore (perhaps in the sand of spirit?) as a Fata Morgana. But the doctrine of the Trinity is »never« »a foundation« (so Mr. Th. speaks of the tripod in the same place, p. 221) »on which _faith_ _can_ _be_ _founded_.« Has this doctrine, as the most holy, not been from time immemorial, or at least since when? the chief content of faith itself as credo, and this credo the foundation of subjective faith? Without this dogma, how could the doctrine of reconciliation, which Mr. Th. in the cited writing strives so energetically to bring home to feeling, have a sense that is more than moral or, if one will, pagan; how could it have a Christian sense? Of other, more specific dogmas too nothing is found in this writing; Mr. Th. leads his readers, for instance, only ever up to the suffering and death of Christ, but never as far as his resurrection and exaltation at the Father's right hand, nor as far as the pouring out of the Holy Spirit. A chief determination in the doctrine of reconciliation is the _punishment_ _of_ _sin_; this is, in Mr. Th., on page 119 and following, the burdensome self-consciousness and the unblessedness connected with it, in which all are who live _apart_ from God, the sole source of blessedness as of holiness; so that sin, consciousness of guilt and unblessedness cannot be _thought_ without one another (here, then, thinking too comes in, as on page 120 the determinations too are shown to flow from the _nature_ of God). This determination of the punishment of sin is just what has come to be named the _natural_ punishment for sin, and (like the indifference toward the doctrine of the Trinity) it is the result and doctrine of reason and enlightenment, which Mr. Th. otherwise decries so loudly. – Some time ago a bill concerning the sect of the _Unitarians_ failed in the House of Lords of the English Parliament; on this occasion an English paper gave a notice about the large number of Unitarians in Europe and in America, and then adds; »on the European continent Protestantism and Unitarianism are at present mostly synonymous.« Let theologians decide whether Mr. _Tholuk's_ dogmatics differs from the usual theology of the Enlightenment in more than one or at most two points, and, when these are looked at more closely, whether not even in these.

[^3]: Mr. _Tholuk_ repeatedly quotes passages out of _Anselm's_ treatise Cur Deus homo, and on page 127 praises »the deep humility of this great thinker«; why does he not also consider and cite the passage from the same treatise (cited at § 77 of the Encyclopaedia, p. 93): Negligentiae mihi videtur si – non studemus quod credimus, _intelligere_. – If, to be sure, the credo has shrunk to barely a few articles, little material remains to be cognized, and little can come of cognition.

[^4]: It must be welcome to me to see the assent of Mr. von _Bader_ to my propositions, both from the content of several recent writings of his and in the express mentions of many of them; about most of what he contests, or easily all of it, it would not be hard to come to an understanding with him, namely to show that it does not in fact deviate from his views. I want to touch on only one reproach, which occurs in the »Remarks on Some Antireligious Philosophemes of Our Time, 1824,« page 5, compare page 56 and following: there mention is made of a philosopheme which »has issued from the school of the philosophy of nature and sets up a false concept of matter, in that it asserts of the perishable essence of this world, which harbours corruption within it, that it has proceeded and proceeds _immediately_ and eternally from God, as God's eternal going-out (externalization), which eternally _conditions_ his eternal going-back-in (as spirit).« As regards the first part of this representation, the _issuing_ _forth_ (this is in general a category I make no use of, being merely a figurative expression and not a category) of matter from God, I can see it only as a proposition already contained in the determination of God as creator of the world; but as regards the other part, that the eternal going-out _conditions_ God's return as spirit, Mr. von Bader puts _the_ _conditioning_ in this place, a category partly improper here in and for itself and as little used by me for this relation; I recall what I remarked above about the uncritical exchange of determinations of thought. But to discuss the _immediate_ or _mediated_ issuing forth of matter would lead only to wholly formal determinations. What Mr. von B. himself states on page 54 and following about the concept of matter I do not see as deviating from my determinations concerning it; just as I do not understand what remedy for the absolute task of apprehending the world's creation as concept lies in what Mr. von B. states on page 58, that matter is »not the product of the unity directly, but that of its _principles_ (plenipotentiaries, Elohim), _which_ _it_ _for_ this purpose called forth.« If the sense is this (for according to the grammatical position it is not wholly clear), that matter is the product of the principles, or this, that matter called forth these Elohim for itself and let itself be produced by them, then either those Elohim or else this whole circle together must be set in a relation to God which is not clarified by the interpolation of Elohim.
