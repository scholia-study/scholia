## {{{ 20.237 }}} Concept of Nature

## {{ 247 }} § 247.

Nature has turned out to be the idea in the form of _otherness_. Since the _idea_ is thus the negative of itself, or is _external_ _to_ _itself_, nature is not external merely in relation to this idea (and to the idea's subjective existence, spirit); rather, _externality_ constitutes the determination in which nature is nature.

## {{ 248 }} § 248.

In this externality the determinations of the concept have the shine of an _indifferent_ _subsistence_ and of _isolation_ from one another; the concept is therefore as something inner. Nature accordingly shows in its existence no freedom, but _necessity_ and _contingency_.

::: remark
{{ 248 A }} Nature, then, in its determinate concrete existence, whereby it is precisely nature, is not to be deified, nor are sun, moon, animals, plants and so forth to be regarded and cited as works of God in preference to human deeds and events. – Nature is divine _in_ _itself_, in the idea, but as it _is_, its being does not correspond to its concept; it is rather the _unresolved_ _contradiction_. Its peculiarity is _positedness_, the negative, just as the ancients grasped _matter_ in general as the non-ens. Thus nature has also been spoken of as the idea's _fall_ from itself, since the idea, in this shape of externality, is in a disparity of itself with itself. – Only to consciousness, which is itself at first external and therefore immediate, that is, to _sensuous_ consciousness, does nature appear as the first, the immediate, the existent. – Yet because even in such an element of externality it is the presentation _of_ _the_ _idea_, one may and should admire in it the wisdom of God. But if _Vanini_ said that a straw suffices to cognize the being of God, then every representation of spirit, the poorest of its imaginings, the play of its most contingent whims, every word, is a more excellent ground of cognition {{{ 20.238 }}} for God's being than any single natural object. In nature the play of forms has not only its unbound, unbridled contingency, but every shape taken by itself lacks the concept of itself. The highest to which nature drives in its existence is _life_, but as merely natural idea this is surrendered to the unreason of externality, and the individual living being is, in every moment of its concrete existence, entangled with a singularity other than itself; whereas in every spiritual expression the moment of a free, universal relation to itself is contained. – It is a like misunderstanding when what is spiritual in general is esteemed less than natural things, when _human_ _works_ _of_ _art_ are ranked below natural things because the material for the former has to be taken from outside and because they are not alive. As if the spiritual form did not contain a higher livingness and were not worthier of spirit than the natural form; as if form in general were not higher than matter, and as if in everything ethical even that which can be called matter did not belong wholly to spirit alone; as if in nature too the higher, the living, did not take its matter from outside. – Nature, it is further given out as its advantage, remains true to eternal laws amid all the contingency of its existences; but surely the realm of self-consciousness does so too, as is already acknowledged in the faith that a providence guides human events; – or are the determinations of this providence in the field of human events supposed to be merely contingent and unreasonable? – Yet if spiritual contingency, _arbitrary_ _will_, advances as far as _evil_, this is itself still infinitely higher than the lawful course of the stars or the innocence of the plant; for what goes astray in this way is still spirit.
:::

## {{ 249 }} § 249.

Nature is to be considered as a _system_ _of_ _stages_, of which one proceeds necessarily from the other and is the proximate truth of that from which it results, yet not in such a way that the one would be _naturally_ {{{ 20.239 }}} <i>en</i>gendered from the other, but in the inner idea that makes up the ground of nature. _Metamorphosis_ belongs only to the concept as such, since only its alteration is development. In nature, however, the concept is partly only inner, and partly concretely existent only as a living individual; _existing_ metamorphosis is therefore confined to this alone.

::: remark
{{ 249 A }} It has been a clumsy representation, in older and also in more recent philosophy of nature, to regard the further formation and the passing of a natural form and sphere into a higher one as an externally actual production, which, in order to make it _clearer_, has nevertheless been shifted back into the _obscurity_ of the past. Externality is precisely what is peculiar to nature, letting the differences fall apart and appear as indifferent existences; the dialectical concept that leads the _stages_ onward is their inner. Thinking consideration must renounce such nebulous representations, at bottom sensuous, as in particular the so-called _emerging_ of plants and animals, for instance, from water, and then the _emerging_ of the more developed animal organizations from the lower, and so on.
:::

## {{ 250 }} § 250.

The _contradiction_ of the idea, in being external to itself as nature, is more precisely the contradiction between, on one side, the _necessity_ of its formations, engendered through the concept, and their rational determination within the organic totality, – and, on the other side, their indifferent contingency and indeterminable irregularity. Contingency and determinability from outside have their right in the sphere of nature. This contingency is greatest in the realm of the concrete formations, which, as natural things, are at the same time only _immediately_ concrete. For the _immediately_ concrete is a multitude of properties that are outside one another and more or less indifferent to one another, and to which for that very reason the simple subjectivity that is for itself is likewise {{{ 20.240 }}} indifferent, leaving them to external, hence contingent determination. It is the _impotence_ of nature to maintain the determinations of the concept only abstractly, and to expose the carrying out of the particular to external determinability.

::: remark
{{ 250 A }} The infinite wealth and manifoldness of forms, and, most unreasonably of all, the contingency that intrudes into the external arrangement of natural formations, have been praised as the lofty freedom of nature, also as the divinity _of_ it or at least the divinity _in_ it. It belongs to the sensuous way of representing to hold contingency, arbitrariness and disorder to be freedom and rationality. – That impotence of nature sets limits to philosophy, and it is the most improper demand to make of the concept that it should comprehend such contingencies, – and, as it has been called, construct and deduce them; the task is even seemingly made easier the more trifling and isolated the formation is[^1]. Traces of the determination by the concept will indeed be followed up into the most particular things, but these cannot be exhausted by it. The traces of this derivation and inner connection will often surprise the observer, but will seem especially surprising, or rather incredible, to one accustomed to see only the contingent in the history of nature as in that of human beings. One has to be wary, however, lest such a trace be taken for the totality of the formations' determination, which marks the transition to the analogies mentioned.

In nature's impotence to hold fast to the concept in its carrying out lies the difficulty, and in many spheres the impossibility, of finding from empirical observation firm differences for classes and {{{ 20.241 }}} orders. Everywhere nature blurs the essential boundaries by intermediate and defective formations, which always furnish instances against every firm distinction, even within determinate genera (for instance that of the human being) by way of malformed births, which on one side must be counted in this genus but which on the other side lack determinations that would have to be regarded as the genus's essential peculiarity. – In order to be able to regard such formations as deficient, defective, deformed, a firm type is presupposed, but one that could not be drawn from experience, for experience itself supplies those so-called malformed births, deformities, intermediate things and so forth: it would rather presuppose the self-subsistence and dignity of the determination by the concept.
:::

## {{ 251 }} § 251.

Nature is in itself a living whole; the movement through its sequence of stages is more precisely this: that the idea _posits_ itself as what it is _in_ _itself_; or, what is the same, that it goes _into_ _itself_ out of its immediacy and externality, which is _death_, in order first of all to be a _living_ being, but then also to sublate this determinateness in which it is only life, and to bring itself forth into the concrete existence of spirit, which is the truth and the ultimate purpose of nature and the true actuality of the idea.

[^1]: Mr. _Krug_ once, in a sense quite naïve in this and at the same time in another respect, called on the philosophy of nature to perform the trick of deducing _just_ his quill pen. – He might have been given hope of this achievement and the respective glorification of _his_ quill pen, if some day science were to advance so far and be so settled about everything more important in heaven and on earth, in present and past, that there would be nothing more important left to comprehend.
