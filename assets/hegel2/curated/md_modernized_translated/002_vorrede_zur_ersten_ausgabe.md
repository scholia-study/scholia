## {{{ 21.5 }}} Preface to the First Edition

The complete transformation which philosophical thinking has undergone among us over the last twenty-five years or so, the higher standpoint that the self-consciousness of spirit has attained over itself in this period, has so far had little influence on the shape of _logic_.

What bore the name of metaphysics before this period has been eradicated, so [to] speak, stem and stump, and has dropped out of the roster of the sciences. In what quarter do the tones of the erstwhile ontology, of rational psychology, of cosmology, or indeed of the erstwhile natural theology, still get leave, or still have leave, to make themselves audible? Investigations into the soul's immateriality, for instance, or into mechanical causes and final ones: what interest could they still be expected to arouse? the remaining proofs of the existence of God, too, are adduced only historically, or for the purposes of edification and the elevation of the heart. It is a fact that interest has been lost partly in the content, partly in the form of the former metaphysics, partly in both at once. Remarkable as it is when a people finds, say, the science of its own constitutional law, when it finds its convictions, its ethical habits and virtues, become useless to it, at least as remarkable is it when a people loses its metaphysics, when the spirit that occupies itself with its own pure essence has no actual existence in that people any more.

The exoteric doctrine of the Kantian philosophy – that the _understanding_ _may_ _not_ _fly_ _beyond_ _experience_, since otherwise the faculty of cognition becomes _theoretical_ _reason_, which of itself gives birth to nothing but _chimeras_ – has justified from the side of science the renunciation of speculative thinking. This popular doctrine was met halfway by the clamor of modern pedagogy, by the distress of the times which fixes its gaze on immediate need, the clamor, namely, that just as for cognition experience is what comes first, so for competence in public and in private life theoretical insight is even harmful, while practice and practical training generally are the essential thing, the only thing that furthers us. – Since science {{{ 21.6 }}} and common human understanding thus worked into each other's hands to bring about the downfall of metaphysics, there seemed to be staged the odd spectacle of _a_ _cultivated_ _people_ _without_ _metaphysics_; – like a temple otherwise adorned in every variety but without a holy of holies. – Theology, which in earlier times was the keeper of the speculative mysteries and of a metaphysics that was, to be sure, dependent upon it, had surrendered this science in exchange for feelings, for what is practically popular, and for learned history. Corresponding to which change is the fact that elsewhere those _solitaries_, who were sacrificed by their people and set apart from the world so that the contemplation of the eternal, and a life serving that contemplation alone, should be present, not for the sake of any use but for the sake of the blessing, – disappeared; a disappearance which, in another connection, may be regarded as essentially the same phenomenon as the one just mentioned. – So that, once these darknesses had been driven off, the colourless occupation of the inwardly turned spirit with itself, existence seemed to have been transformed into the cheerful world of flowers, among which, as everyone knows, there is no _black_ one.

Things have not gone quite so badly for _logic_ as for metaphysics. That through it one _learns_ _to think_, which formerly counted as its use and thereby as its purpose – as though one first had to learn to digest and to move about through the study of anatomy and physiology –, this prejudice has long since faded away, and the spirit of practicality no doubt intended for logic no better a fate than for its sister. Notwithstanding this, and probably for the sake of some formal usefulness, logic was still left a rank among the sciences, indeed it was even retained as a subject of public instruction. This better lot, however, concerns only its outer fortune; for its shape and content have remained the same as they were handed down through a long tradition, though in that transmission they had been thinned out and starved more and more; the new spirit that has dawned upon science no less than upon actuality has not yet made itself felt in logic. But it is once and for all in vain, when the substantial form of spirit has reshaped itself, to want to keep the forms of an earlier culture; they are withered leaves, pushed off by the new buds that are already being generated at their roots.

The _ignoring_ of the general change is by now, in the scientific sphere too, beginning to run out. Unnoticed, even the opponents have grown familiar with the other ways of representing things and made them their own, and though they persist in acting coy toward their source and their principles and conduct themselves contradictorily toward them, {{{ 21.7 }}} they have had to put up with the consequences all the same and have not been able to fend off their influence; for their ever more insignificant negative stance they know no other way of gaining a positive importance and a content than by joining in the talk of the new ways of representing things.

From the other side, the time of ferment with which a new creation begins seems to be over. In its first appearance such a creation is apt to behave with fanatical hostility toward the far-flung systematizing of the earlier principle, apt in part also to be fearful, losing itself in the extension of the particular, and in part to shy away from the labor that scientific elaboration requires and, in the need for such elaboration, to reach first for an empty formalism. What is demanded now, with so much the more urgency, is that the material be worked up and elaborated. An age, no less than an individual, passes through a phase of formation in which everything turns chiefly on acquiring the principle and holding fast to it in its still undeveloped intensity. What is required at the higher level, however, is that the principle should turn into science.

Whatever may already have been done in other respects for the substance and for the form of science, logical science, which makes up metaphysics proper or pure speculative philosophy, has hitherto still found itself greatly neglected. What I understand more precisely by this science and by its standpoint I have indicated provisionally in the _Introduction_. Equitable judges will, I hope, take into account that this science had once again to be begun over from scratch, that the subject matter is of the kind it is, and that no preliminary studies lay ready which the recasting here undertaken might have drawn upon, even though the labor of many years was unable to lend this attempt any higher degree of completeness. – Seen from the vantage point that is essential, what is at stake throughout is a new concept of scientific treatment. Given that philosophy is to be science, it cannot, as I have recalled[^1] in another place, take over for this purpose a method on loan from some science ranked beneath it, mathematics for example; just as little may it settle for categorical assurances of an inner intuition, or avail itself of ratiocination proceeding from grounds supplied by external reflection. Rather, it can only be _the_ _nature_ _of the_ _content_ that _moves_ itself in {{{ 21.8 }}} scientific cognition, since at the same time it is this _own_ _reflection_ of the content _which_ first posits and _generates_ _its_ _determination_ itself.

[^1]: Phenomenology of Spirit, Pref. to the first ed. – The proper working out of this is the cognition of the method, and it has its place in the logic itself.

The _understanding_ _determines_, and it keeps its determinations fixed; _reason_ is negative and _dialectical_, for it melts the determinations of the understanding away into nothing; and it is _positive_, for it brings forth the _universal_ and grasps what is particular as contained within it. Just as the understanding is taken as something separate from reason in general, so too dialectical reason is apt to be taken as something separate from positive reason. But in its truth reason is _spirit_, which is higher than both, being reason that understands, or understanding that reasons. It is the negative, that which makes up the quality of dialectical reason as much as of the understanding; – it negates the simple, and so posits the determinate difference of the understanding, and it just as much dissolves that difference, and so is dialectical. It does not, however, hold to the nothing of this result, but is therein just as much positive, and has thereby restored the first simple, but as a universal that is concrete within itself; a given particular is not subsumed under this universal, rather in that determining and in the dissolution of it the particular has already determined itself along with it. This movement of spirit, which in its very simplicity confers determinateness upon itself and, within that determinateness, confers upon itself its own self-sameness, and which accordingly constitutes the immanent unfolding of the concept, is cognition's absolute method and at once the soul immanent in the content itself. – Only on this self-constructing path, I maintain, is philosophy capable of being objective, demonstrated science. – In this manner I have attempted to present _consciousness_ in the _Phenomenology_ _of_ _Spirit_. Consciousness is spirit as knowledge that is concrete and indeed caught up in externality; but the onward movement of this subject matter rests solely, like the development of all natural and spiritual life, on the nature of the _pure_ _essentialities_ that make up the content of logic. Consciousness, as spirit in its appearing, which on its way frees itself from its immediacy and its external concretion, becomes pure knowledge, which gives itself those pure essentialities themselves, as they are in and for themselves, as its subject matter. They are the pure thoughts, spirit thinking its own essence. Their self-movement is their spiritual life, and it is that through which science constitutes itself and of which science is the exposition.

The relation of the science that I call the _Phenomenology_ _of_ _Spirit_ to logic is herewith indicated. – Turning to the outward connection: {{{ 21.9 }}} the first part _of the_ _System_ _of_ _Science_,[^2] the part containing the phenomenology, was designated to have a second part come after it, one that was to hold the logic together with philosophy's two real sciences, that of nature and that of spirit, and that would thereby have brought the system of science to its close. But the necessary extent that the logic had to acquire in its own right has led me to let it come out on its own; within an expanded plan it therefore makes up the first sequel to the Phenomenology of Spirit. Later I shall let the working out of the two named real sciences of philosophy follow. – This first volume of the logic, however, contains as its first book _the_ _doctrine_ _of_ _being_; the second book, _the_ _doctrine_ _of_ _essence_, as the second section of the first volume; the second volume, on the other hand, will contain the _subjective_ _logic_, or the _doctrine_ _of the_ _concept_.

[^2]: (Bamberg and Würzburg, at Göbhard's, 1807). This title will no longer be attached to the second edition, which is to appear next Easter. – In place of the intention mentioned in what follows, of a second part that was to contain all the other philosophical sciences, I have since brought out the Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences, last year in its third edition (Note to the second edition).

Nuremberg, 22 March 1812.
