## Remark

The expression our language has for the question after quality, one that at first seems peculiar, namely _what_ _kind_ _of_ thing something may be, singles out the moment here under consideration in its reflection-into-itself. Idealistic in its origin, this expression asks not what this thing A may be _for_ _an_ _other_ thing B, not what this human being may be for another human being; – but rather what _kind_ _of_ _thing_ _this_ is, what _kind_ _of_ _human_ _being_? so that being-for-one is herewith taken back into the thing, into the human being itself, and so that what _is_, together with that _for_ _which_ it is, makes up a single selfsame thing, – an identity, and it is as such an identity that ideality too must be viewed.

Ideality belongs in the first instance to the sublated determinations, as distinguished from that _wherein_ they are sublated, which by contrast may be taken as the real. Taken so, however, the ideal turns out once more to be one moment and the real the other; ideality, though, consists in this, that both determinations are alike only _for_ _one_ and hold good only for _one_, which _one_ ideality is on that account undifferentiatedly reality. In this sense self-consciousness, spirit, God, the ideal, are infinite relating purely to self, – I is for I, both are the same, I is named twice, yet of the two, taken thus, each is only for-one, ideal; spirit is only for spirit, {{{ 21.148 }}} God only for God, and God is nothing but this unity, God as spirit. – Consciousness, though, is where self-consciousness steps into the difference between _itself_ and an _other_, or between its ideality, wherein it is representing, and its reality, inasmuch as its representation carries a determinate content that retains the side of being known as the negative not yet sublated, as existence. To call thought, spirit, God _merely_ something ideal, however, presupposes the standpoint on which finite existence counts as the real and on which the ideal, or being-for-one, carries only a one-sided sense.

In a preceding Remark the principle of idealism was stated, and it was said that with a philosophy what then matters more closely is how far the principle has been carried through. Concerning the manner of this carrying-through a further observation may still be offered with reference to the category at which we now stand. Such carrying-through depends first of all upon this, – whether, alongside being-for-itself, finite existence does not still persist self-subsistently, and besides that upon whether within the infinite itself the moment _for-one_, a comporting of the ideal towards itself as ideal, is already posited. Eleatic being, or Spinoza's substance, is accordingly nothing but abstraction's negating of all determinateness, no ideality being posited within it; – in _Spinoza_, as will be remarked further on, infinity amounts merely to the absolute _affirmation_ of a thing, hence merely to the unity that does not move, and substance for that reason never even arrives at the determination of being-for-itself, much less at that of subject and spirit. More explicated within itself is the idealism of the noble _Malebranche_; it holds the following fundamental thoughts: since God encloses within himself every eternal truth, every idea, and the perfections belonging to all things, so that these are _his_ alone, it is only in him that we see them; by an action having nothing sensuous about it God awakens our sensations of the objects in us, whereby we fancy that from the object we obtain not merely its idea, representing its essence, but the sensation of that object's existence besides (De la recherche de la Verité, Eclairc. sur la nature des idées etc.). As with the eternal truths and ideas (essentialities) of things, so too their existence, in God, is ideal, not an actual existence; although they are our objects, they are only _for_ _one_. Present here is that moment of explicated and concrete idealism which Spinozism lacks, in that absolute ideality is determined as knowledge. Pure and profound though this idealism is, those relations still hold in part much that is indeterminate for thought, while in part their content is straightaway wholly concrete (sin and redemption and so forth enter into them at once); the logical determination of infinity, which would have to be their foundation, is not worked out on its own account, and so that lofty and replete idealism is indeed the product of a purely speculative spirit, yet not yet of a purely speculative thinking that alone truly grounds.

{{{ 21.149 }}} The <i>Leibniz</i>ian idealism lies more within the limit of the abstract concept. – The _Leibnizian_ _representing_ essence, the _monad_, is essentially something ideal. Representing is a being-for-itself in which the determinatenesses are not limits and hence not an existence, but only moments. Representing is admittedly a more concrete determination as well, yet here it carries no further meaning than that of ideality; for with Leibniz even what is wholly without consciousness is something representing, something perceiving. Within this system, then, otherness stands sublated; neither spirit and body nor the monads at large are others for one another, none limits another, none exerts an effect upon another; every relation whatever that has an existence for its ground drops away. Manifoldness is only an ideal and inner one, the monad within it stays related only to its own self, the alterations unfold inside it and are no relatings of it to others. Whatever, according to the real determination, is taken as an existent relating of the monads to one another, is an independent, merely _simultaneous_ becoming, shut up within the being-for-itself of each. – That there are _several_ _monads_, that with this they are also determined as others, is no concern of the monads themselves; this is the reflection of a third party falling outside them; they are not _in_ _their_ _own_ _selves_ _others_ _over_ _against_ _one_ _another_; being-for-itself is held pure, without the _alongside_ of an existence. – Yet just here lies at the same time what is unfinished about this system. Such representing beings the monads are only _in_ _themselves_, or _in_ _God_, the monad of monads, or _else_ _in_ _the_ _system_. Otherness is likewise at hand; let it fall where it will, into representing itself, or however that third party be determined which regards the monads as others, as many. Excluded, and that only for a moment, is the plurality of their existence, the monads being posited as such solely through the abstraction that they should be non-others. If it is a third party that posits their otherness, then it is also a third party that sublates their otherness; but this whole _movement_, _which_ _makes_ _them_ _ideal_, falls outside them. While it may be recalled, however, that this movement of thought itself falls, after all, only within a representing monad, it is at the same time to be recalled that precisely _the_ _content_ of such thinking is _external_ _to_ _itself_ _within_ _its_ _own_ _self_. The passage {{{ 21.150 }}} is made from the unity of absolute ideality (the monad of monads) immediately, without comprehension (– by way of the representation of creating), to the category of the abstract (relationless) _plurality_ of existence, and from this back, just as abstractly, to that unity. Ideality, representing in general, stays something formal, as does likewise representing heightened into consciousness. In the conceit of Leibniz cited above about the magnetic needle, which, were it possessed of consciousness, would regard its northward pointing as a determination of its own freedom, consciousness gets thought merely as a one-sided form that is indifferent towards its determination and content; just so, ideality in the monads is a form that stays external to plurality. Immanent in them ideality is supposed to be, representing is supposed to be their nature; but their comporting is, first, their harmony, which does not fall within their existence and is on that account pre-established; second, this _existence_ of theirs is grasped neither as being-for-other nor any further as ideality, but is determined merely as abstract plurality; the ideality of plurality and its further determination into harmony do not become immanent in and proper to this plurality itself.

Other idealism, such as for example the Kantian and Fichte's, does not get beyond the _ought_ or the _infinite_ _progress_, and stays within the dualism of existence and of being-for-itself. Within these systems the thing-in-itself, or else the infinite check, does indeed step immediately into the I and becomes only something _for_ _the_ _latter_; but it proceeds from a free otherness that perennializes itself as negative being-in-itself. Hence the I is indeed determined as the ideal, as being-for-itself, as infinite relating to self; but _being-for-one_ is not consummated into the vanishing of that beyond, or of the direction towards the beyond.
