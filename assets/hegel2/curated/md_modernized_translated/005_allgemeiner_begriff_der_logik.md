## General Concept of Logic

In no science is the need felt more strongly to begin from the subject matter itself, without any preceding reflections, than in the science of logic. In every other science the object it treats and the scientific method are distinguished from each other; and its content, too, does not make an absolute beginning but depends on other concepts and hangs together on all sides with other material. These sciences are therefore granted leave to speak of their ground and its connections, and likewise of method, only lemmatically, to apply without further ado the forms of definition and the like that are presupposed as known and accepted, and to make use of the ordinary manner of argumentation in fixing their general concepts and fundamental determinations.

Logic, by contrast, cannot presuppose any of these forms of reflection, or rules and laws of thinking, for they constitute a part of its own content and first have to be grounded within it. But not only the statement of scientific method, the _concept_ itself of _science_ as such belongs to its content, and indeed constitutes its final result; what it is, therefore, it cannot say in advance, but rather its entire treatise brings forth this knowledge of itself only as its last word and as its completion. Likewise its object, _thinking_ or more precisely _conceptual_ _thinking_, is essentially dealt with within it; the concept of thinking generates itself in the course of it, and thus cannot be sent on ahead. Nothing that this introduction sends on ahead, then, is meant to establish the concept of logic or to vindicate beforehand and scientifically its content and its method; the intent is rather, by way of a handful of clarifications and reflections offered in a ratiocinative and historical mode, to bring nearer to representation that vantage point out of which this science has to be viewed.

{{{ 21.28 }}} To accept logic as the science of thinking in general carries with it the understanding that such thinking amounts to _the_ _mere_ _form_ of a cognition, that logic leaves all _content_ out of account, that the so-called second _constituent_ belonging to a cognition, the _matter_, has to be supplied from some other quarter, and hence that logic — a science from which that matter is utterly independent — can do no more than state the formal conditions of true cognition, while real truth itself it neither contains nor even furnishes the _road_ to, since the very thing that makes truth truth, namely the content, falls outside it.

To begin with, however, there is already something inept in the claim that logic leaves every _content_ aside, that its teaching covers only the rules of thinking, incapable of going into what gets thought or of heeding how the latter is constituted. For thinking, along with the rules of thinking, is precisely what is meant to be its object, and in that object logic already possesses, without mediation, a content all its own; there too it possesses that second constituent of cognition, a matter whose constitution is very much its concern.

But secondly, the representations on which the concept of logic has rested up to now have in part already perished, and in part it is time that they should vanish altogether, that the standpoint of this science should be grasped at a higher level, and that it should gain a completely altered shape.

Ordinary consciousness takes for granted, once and for all, a divorce between the _content_ of cognition and its _form_, between _truth_ and _certainty_, and it is on this divorce that logic's concept has rested until now. It is presupposed, _first_, that the material of cognition is present in and for itself as a finished world outside thinking, that thinking for itself is empty, that it comes to that matter externally as a form, fills itself with it, only thereby gains a content, and so becomes a real cognizing.

These two components then stand toward each other, – (for they are supposed to have the relation of components, and cognition is compounded out of them mechanically or at most chemically –) in the following order of rank: that the object is something complete and finished for itself, something that can entirely dispense with thinking for its actuality, whereas thinking is something deficient which has first to complete itself on some material and, as a pliable indeterminate form, has to make itself adequate to its matter. Truth is the agreement of thinking with the object, and in order to bring this agreement about, – for it is not present in and for itself, – thinking is supposed to yield and accommodate itself to the object.

Third, inasmuch as the diversity of matter and form, of object and thinking, is not left in that misty indeterminacy but is taken {{{ 21.29 }}} more determinately, each is a sphere severed from the other. In its receiving and forming of the material, therefore, thinking does not get beyond itself; its receiving and its accommodating itself to the material remain a modification of itself, and it does not thereby become its own other; moreover, self-conscious determining belongs to it alone in any case; and so, even in its relating to the object, it does not come out of itself over to the object, which remains a thing-in-itself, simply a beyond of thinking.

What such opinions about how subject and object stand to one another put into words are the determinations making up the nature of our everyday consciousness, consciousness as it appears; yet once these preconceptions are transferred to reason, as though the very same relation held good there, as though that relation possessed truth in and for itself, they turn into the errors whose refutation — carried through every region of the spiritual and the natural universe — is philosophy itself, or rather, since they block the doorway into philosophy, errors to be discarded before one enters.

In this respect the older metaphysics had a higher concept of thinking than has become current in more recent times. For it laid down as its basis that what is cognized through thinking of and in things is alone what is genuinely true in them; hence not things in their immediacy, but things first raised into the form of thinking, as things thought. On this metaphysics' reckoning, then, neither thinking nor its determinations stand foreign to objects; they are what those objects essentially are, so that _things_ and the _thinking_ of them (– a kinship our language, too, puts into words –) tally in and for themselves, thinking in its immanent determinations and the genuine nature of things making up one single content.

But the _reflecting_ understanding took possession of philosophy. One must know exactly what this expression is meant to say, an expression otherwise much used as a catchword; by it is to be understood in general the abstracting and hence separating understanding, which persists in its separations. Set against reason, it conducts itself as _plain_ _common-sense_ and pushes its own opinion: that sensuous reality is the ground on which truth stands, that thoughts are _merely_ thoughts, meaning that substance and reality accrue to them only from sensuous perception, and that reason, so long as it stays in and for itself, spins out nothing but chimeras. In this renunciation by reason of itself the concept of truth is lost; reason is restricted to cognizing only subjective truth, only appearance, {{{ 21.30 }}} only something to which the nature of the subject matter itself does not correspond; _knowledge_ has sunk back into _opinion_.

This turn, however, which cognition takes, and which appears as a loss and a step backwards, has at its basis something deeper, on which the elevation of reason into the higher spirit of modern philosophy rests altogether. One must look for the ground of that now widely shared representation in an insight, namely into how the determinations of the understanding stand in _necessary_ _conflict_ with themselves. – Reflection, as named a moment ago, consists in passing _beyond_ the concrete immediate and in _determining_ and _separating_ it. But it must _just_ _as_ _much_ _go_ _beyond_ these its _separating_ determinations, and in the first instance _relate_ them. At the standpoint of this relating, their conflict comes to the fore. This relating of reflection belongs in itself to reason; the elevation above those determinations, which arrives at the insight into their conflict, is the great negative step toward the true concept of reason. But insight not carried through falls into the misunderstanding that it is reason which lands in contradiction with itself; it fails to recognize that the contradiction is precisely the raising of reason above the limitations of the understanding and the dissolving of them. Instead of taking the last step upward from here, cognition has fled back from the unsatisfactoriness of the determinations of the understanding to sensuous concrete existence, supposing that in it it has what is firm and self-consistent. But since on the other side this cognition knows itself as cognition only of what appears, its unsatisfactoriness is conceded, yet at the same time it is presupposed that although things-in-themselves are not correctly cognized, still within the sphere of appearance there is correct cognizing; as though it were, as it were, only the _kind_ _of_ _objects_ that differed here, and the one kind, namely things-in-themselves, did not fall within cognition, while the other kind, namely appearances, did. It is as if correct insight were attributed to a man, with the addition that he is nevertheless capable of having insight into nothing true, but only into what is untrue. The latter supposition is preposterous, and precisely as preposterous is a true cognition that would fail to cognize its object such as the object is in itself.

The upshot already mentioned of the _critique_ _of_ _the_ _forms_ _of_ _the_ _understanding_ was that no _application_ _to_ _things_ _in_ _themselves_ belongs to these forms. – Which can mean nothing else than that the forms are, on their own account, something untrue. Yet inasmuch as they are allowed to go on holding good for subjective reason and for experience, {{{ 21.31 }}} nothing about the forms themselves has been altered by the critique, which leaves them shaped for the subject exactly as they had once been shaped for the object. Should they not suffice for the thing-in-itself, however, then the understanding they are said to belong to has all the less reason to tolerate them or make do with them. Determinations of the _thing_ _in_ _itself_ they cannot be; determinations of the _understanding_ they can be still less, given that the understanding ought at any rate to be granted the dignity of a thing-in-itself. The same conflict afflicts the determinations of the finite and the infinite, be it that one applies them to time and space, to the world, or that they count as determinations lying within spirit; black and white yield a grey just as surely when brought together on a wall as when brought together while still on the palette; and if our representation of the world falls apart once the determinations of the infinite and the finite get transferred onto it, then _spirit_ itself, holding the two of them within it, is all the more something that contradicts itself, something that falls apart. – It is not the constitution of the material or of the object, to which they would be applied or in which they would be found, that can make a difference; for the object has the contradiction in it only through and according to those determinations.

That critique has therefore only removed the forms of objective thinking from the thing, but left them in the subject just as it found them. For in doing so it did not consider these forms in and for themselves, according to their own peculiar content, but simply took them over lemmatically from subjective logic; so that there was no question of a derivation of them in themselves, or even of a derivation of them as subjective-logical forms, still less of a dialectical consideration of them.

Transcendental idealism, carried through more consistently, recognized the nullity of the spectre of the _thing-in-itself_ still left over by the critical philosophy, that abstract shadow cut off from all content, and had the purpose of destroying it completely. This philosophy also made the beginning of letting reason exhibit its determinations out of itself. But the subjective posture of this attempt did not let it come to completion. Subsequently this posture, and with it that beginning too and the development of pure science, was abandoned.

What is commonly understood by logic, however, is considered entirely without regard to metaphysical significance. This science, in the {{{ 21.32 }}} condition in which it still finds itself, has of course no content of the sort that counts as reality and as a genuine subject matter in ordinary consciousness. But it is not for this reason a formal science, lacking truth rich in content. In that material which is missed in it, a lack to which its unsatisfactoriness is usually ascribed, the domain of truth is in any case not to be sought. Rather, the emptiness of the logical forms lies solely in the manner of considering and treating them. Inasmuch as they fall apart as fixed determinations and are not held together in organic unity, they are dead forms and do not have dwelling within them the spirit that is their living concrete unity. But with that they lack solid content, – a matter which would be substance in its own self. The content that is missed in the logical forms is nothing other than a firm foundation and concretion of these abstract determinations; and such a substantial essence is customarily sought for them on the outside. But logical reason itself is the substantial or the real, which holds all abstract determinations together within itself and is their solid, absolutely concrete unity. One need not, then, go hunting far afield for whatever tends to get called a matter; should logic be reckoned devoid of substance, the blame lies not with logic's object but purely with the way that object is taken up.

This reflection leads more closely to the statement of the standpoint from which logic is to be considered, in what respect that standpoint differs from the way this science has been treated hitherto, and how it is the sole genuine standpoint on which logic is henceforth to be placed for good.

In the _Phenomenology_ _of_ _Spirit_ I presented consciousness in its forward movement from the first immediate opposition of itself and the object up to absolute knowledge. Every form assumed by the _relation_ _of_ _consciousness_ _to_ _the_ _object_ lies along this road, and what issues from it is the _concept_ _of_ _science_. This concept therefore requires no justification here (apart from the fact that it emerges within logic itself), because it has received one there; and it is capable of no other justification than just this bringing forth of it by consciousness, for which all its own shapes dissolve into this concept as into their truth. – The most that any ratiocinative grounding or clarification of the concept of science can achieve is to set that concept before {{{ 21.33 }}} representation and to yield some historical familiarity with it; whereas whatever definition one gives of science, or of logic more narrowly, finds its _proof_ nowhere but in the necessity with which science comes forth. A definition with which some science makes an absolute beginning can contain nothing other than the determinate, correct expression of what, _by_ _general_ _admission_ _and_ _acknowledgment_, one _represents_ to oneself as the object and purpose of the science. That one represents precisely this to oneself under it is a historical assurance, in regard to which one can only appeal to this or that recognized view, or, properly speaking, can only plead that one might let this and that pass as recognized. There is no end to it: one person adduces from here, another from there, a case and instance according to which something more and other still is to be understood by this or that expression, so that a closer or a more general determination has to be taken up into its definition and the science arranged accordingly. – Furthermore, it depends here on argumentation what all is to be drawn in or excluded and up to what limit and extent; but argumentation itself lies open to the most manifold and most varied opinions, on which in the end arbitrary choice alone can settle a fixed determination. In this procedure of beginning the science with its definition, there is no talk of the need that the _necessity_ of its _object_, and thereby of itself, should be exhibited.

In the present treatise, accordingly, the concept of pure science together with its deduction counts as presupposed, to the extent that the Phenomenology of Spirit amounts to precisely that deduction and nothing besides. Every mode of consciousness has its _truth_ in absolute knowledge, since — as the course consciousness ran made plain — nowhere but in absolute knowledge does the divorce of the _object_ from _certainty_ _of_ _self_ come wholly undone, truth turning equal to that certainty and that certainty equal to truth.

Pure science thus presupposes liberation from the opposition of consciousness. It contains _the_ _thought_, _insofar_ _as_ _it_ _is_ _just_ _as_ _much_ _the_ _subject_ _matter_ _in_ _its_ _own_ _self_, or _the_ _subject_ _matter_ _in_ _its_ _own_ _self_, insofar as it _is_ _just_ _as_ _much_ _pure_ _thought_. As _science_, truth is pure self-developing self-consciousness and has the shape of the self, in that _what_ _is_ _in_ _and_ _for_ _itself_ _is_ _the_ _known_ _concept_, but _the_ _concept_ _as_ _such_ is _what_ _is_ _in_ _and_ _for_ _itself_. {{{ 21.34 }}} This objective thinking, then, is the _content_ of pure science. So little, therefore, is it formal, so little does it want for the matter that an actual and true cognition demands, that its content is on the contrary the absolutely true and nothing else, or, should one still care to employ the word matter, the genuine matter, – matter of a kind, though, to which form is nothing external, seeing that such matter is itself pure thought and therewith absolute form. Logic is on this showing to be grasped as pure reason's system, as the realm of pure thought. _This_ _realm_ is _truth_ _such_ _as_ _it_ _is_ _in_ _and_ _for_ _itself_, _stripped_ _of_ _all_ _covering_. One may accordingly put it this way: _the_ _portrayal_ _of_ _God_ is what this content is, _God_ _as_ _he_ _is_ _in_ _his_ _eternal_ _essence_ _prior_ _to_ _the_ _creation_ _of_ _nature_ _and_ _of_ _any_ _finite_ _spirit_.

Praise goes to _Anaxagoras_ as the first to have voiced the thought that the world's principle is _nous_, _thought_, and that as thought the essence of the world is to be determined. With this he laid the foundation for an intellectual view of the universe, whose pure shape must be _logic_. What is at issue in logic is not a thinking _about_ something that would lie at its base for itself outside thinking, not forms that would yield mere _marks_ of truth; rather, the necessary forms and the proper determinations of thinking are the content and the highest truth itself.

In order at least to take this up into representation, the opinion must be set aside that truth has to be something palpable. Such palpability is carried, for example, even into the Platonic ideas that are in God's thinking, as though they were, so to speak, existing things, only in another world or region, outside of which the world of actuality is to be found, possessing a substantiality distinct from those ideas and real only through this distinctness. Nothing else is the Platonic idea than the universal, or more exactly the object's concept; a something has actuality in its concept alone; where it diverges from its concept it stops being actual and counts as a nullity; and palpability, together with sensuous being-outside-itself, falls on that null side. – From the other side, however, one can appeal to ordinary logic's own representations; for it is assumed, for example, that definitions do not contain determinations that fall only within the cognizing subject, but the determinations of the object, which constitute its most essential, its ownmost nature. Or again, where one infers other determinations from determinations already given, the supposition is that whatever has been inferred is nothing extraneous, nothing foreign to the {{{ 21.35 }}} object, but rather accrues to the object itself, being answering to such thinking. – It underlies the use of the forms of concept, judgment, syllogism, definition, division and so forth quite generally that these are not merely forms of self-conscious thinking but also of objective understanding. – _Thinking_ is an expression that attributes the determination contained in it preeminently to consciousness. Yet whenever the claim is made that _understanding_, _that_ _reason_ _dwells_ _in_ _the_ _world_ _of_ _objects_, that _universal_ _laws_ govern the life and the alterations of spirit and of nature, then it stands conceded that objective worth and concrete existence attach no less to the determinations of thinking.

The critical philosophy had, it is true, already converted _metaphysics_ into _logic_, yet it — and the later idealism along with it — out of dread before the object, assigned the logical determinations a significance essentially subjective, as was recalled a moment ago; by that very move the determinations stay encumbered with the object they had fled, and a thing-in-itself, an infinite impetus, was left clinging to them as something beyond. Science, however, has to be able to presuppose that release from consciousness's opposition, and such release lifts the determinations of thinking clear of that timid, unfinished standpoint and calls for them to be considered in and for themselves, free of any such restriction and deference, as the logical, the purely rational.

Kant elsewhere counts logic, namely the aggregate of determinations and propositions that in the ordinary sense goes by the name of logic, fortunate in that so early a completion has fallen to its lot in advance of other sciences; since _Aristotle_, he says, it has taken no step backward, but no step forward either, the latter because to all appearances it seems to be closed and complete. – If logic has undergone no change since Aristotle, – as indeed the changes, when one looks at the more recent compendia of logic, frequently consist in nothing more than omissions, – then what should rather be inferred from this is that it stands all the more in need of a total reworking; for two thousand years of continued labour by spirit must have procured for it a higher consciousness of its thinking and of its pure essentiality within itself. Set the shapes into which the spirit of the practical and of the religious world, and the spirit of science, have lifted themselves in every kind of real and ideal consciousness beside the shape that logic — spirit's consciousness of its own pure essence — currently occupies, and the disparity shows itself too {{{ 21.36 }}} large for even the most superficial look to miss at once that this last-named consciousness falls wholly short of those earlier elevations and is unworthy of them.

In fact the need for a transformation of logic has long been felt. In the form and the content in which it shows itself in the textbooks, it has, one may say, fallen into contempt. It is still dragged along more from a feeling that a logic of some kind cannot be done without, and from a still persisting habituation to the tradition of its importance, than from any conviction that that ordinary content and the occupation with those empty forms have value and use.

The expansions given to it for a time by psychological, pedagogical and even physiological material have since been fairly generally acknowledged to be disfigurements. In and for itself a large part of these psychological, pedagogical, physiological observations, laws and rules must appear very stale and trivial, whether they stand in logic or wherever else. Above all, such rules as, for example, that one should think through and examine what one reads in books or hears spoken; that if one does not see well one should come to the aid of one's eyes with spectacles, – rules which the textbooks of so-called applied logic gave, and indeed in all seriousness divided into paragraphs, so that one might attain truth, – must strike everyone as superfluous, except at most the writer or the teacher who is at a loss how to pad out by something or other the otherwise too short and lifeless content of logic.

Why such content should be so devoid of spirit has already been accounted for above. Fixed and unbudging is how its determinations hold good, and they are set into no relation with one another but an external one. Since with judgments and syllogisms the operations are chiefly traced back to, and founded upon, the quantitative side of the determinations, the whole affair comes to rest upon a difference that is external, upon comparison and nothing more, and turns into a wholly analytic procedure, a conceptless computing. The derivation of the so-called rules and laws, above all of inference, is not much better than fingering little sticks of unequal length in order to sort and join them according to their size, – than the children's pastime of picking out the matching pieces from pictures cut up in various ways. – Hence this thinking {{{ 21.37 }}} has not unjustly been equated with reckoning, and reckoning in turn with this thinking. In arithmetic numbers are taken as the conceptless, which apart from its equality or inequality, that is, apart from its wholly external ratio, has no significance, which is a thought neither in its own self nor in its relation. When it is worked out in a mechanical way that three-quarters multiplied by two-thirds makes one-half, this operation contains roughly as much and as little thought as the calculation whether this or that kind of syllogism can occur in a given figure.

If this dead skeleton of logic is to be brought to life by spirit as substance and content, its _method_ must be the one through which alone it is capable of being pure science. In the condition in which it finds itself, hardly an inkling of scientific method is to be discerned. It has roughly the form of an empirical science. Empirical sciences have found, for what they are supposed to be, their own peculiar method of defining and classifying their material as well as this can be done. Pure mathematics likewise has a method of its own, one fitted to its abstract objects and to the quantitative determination that is the sole respect under which it regards them. What is essential on the score of this method, and generally on the subordinate rank of the scientific character attainable in mathematics, I stated in the preface to the Phenomenology of Spirit; the method will nevertheless come up for closer consideration inside logic itself. Spinoza, Wolff and others let themselves be seduced into applying it to philosophy as well, and into making the external course of conceptless quantity into the course of the concept, which is in and for itself contradictory. Hitherto philosophy had not yet found its method; it looked with envy at the systematic edifice of mathematics and borrowed its method from it, as was said, or made do with the method of sciences that are only mixtures of given material, empirical propositions and thoughts, – or helped itself out by the crude discarding of all method. But setting out what alone can serve as the true method of philosophical science belongs to logic's own treatise; for method is nothing but consciousness of the form belonging to the inner self-movement of logic's content. In the _Phenomenology_ _of_ _Spirit_ I have set out an example of this method on a more concrete object, on _consciousness_[^1]. There we have shapes of consciousness, each of which in its realization {{{ 21.38 }}} at the same time dissolves itself, has its own negation for its result, – and has thereby passed over into a higher shape. The sole thing needed _in_ _order_ _to_ _secure_ _scientific_ _advance_, and the thing whose quite _simple_ insight one must essentially labour to reach, – is the recognition of that logical proposition according to which the negative is positive in equal measure, or according to which whatever contradicts itself resolves not into a zero, into abstract nothing, but essentially into nothing more than the negation of its _particular_ content, so that a negation of this kind is not negation of everything but _the_ _negation_ _of_ _the_ _determinate_ _subject_ _matter_ that is resolving itself, and so is determinate negation; whence the result essentially harbours within it that from which it results; – which is strictly speaking a tautology, since otherwise the result would be an immediate and no result at all. Because the negation that results is _determinate_ negation, a _content_ belongs to it. What it amounts to is a new concept, though one higher and richer than its predecessor; enriched, namely, by that predecessor's negation or opposite, it holds the earlier concept within itself and something over and above it besides, being the unity of the earlier concept with its own opposite. – In this way the system of concepts as such has to form itself, – and to complete itself in an unstoppable, pure course that takes in nothing from outside.

Far be it from me to imagine that the method I have pursued in this system of logic, – or rather the method this system pursues in its own self, – admits of no further perfecting, no thorough working-out in detail; and yet I know withal that no other method is the true one. This is already evident of itself from the fact that the method is nothing distinct from its object and content; – for it is the content within itself, _the_ _dialectic_, _which_ _it_ _has_ _in_ _its_ _own_ _self_, that moves it forward. It is clear that no expositions can count as scientific which do not go the course of this method and are not in keeping with its simple rhythm, for it is the course of the subject matter itself.

Conformably with this method I would recall that whatever divisions and headings for books, sections and chapters are stated in the work, together with any explanations attached to them, have been made to serve a preliminary survey, and that their worth is properly speaking only _historical_. Neither the content nor the body of the science includes them; they are assemblages put together by external reflection, a reflection that has already traversed the execution as a whole {{{ 21.39 }}} and so knows the sequence of its moments beforehand and announces that sequence before the subject matter itself has brought it on.

In the other sciences such advance determinations and divisions are likewise, taken by themselves, nothing other than such external indications; but within those sciences too they are not raised above this character. Even in logic, for example, one finds it said, »logic has two main parts, the doctrine of elements and methodology,« and then under the doctrine of elements there stands, without further ado, the _heading_: laws of thinking; – then _first_ _chapter_: on concepts. _First_ _section_: on the clarity of concepts, and so forth. – Determinations and divisions of this sort, arrived at with no deduction and no justification at all, are what makes up the systematic framework of such sciences and the whole of their coherence. A logic of that kind takes its vocation to lie in declaring that concepts and truths have to be _derived_ from principles; yet in what it names method, deriving is not so much as distantly contemplated. The order consists, say, in putting like things together, in sending the simpler ahead of the composite, and in other external considerations. But as regards an inner, necessary connectedness, it stops at the register of the divisional determinations, and the transition is effected only by its now saying: _second_ _chapter_; – or: _we_ _come_ _now_ to judgments, and the like.

The headings and divisions occurring in this system, too, are meant to have by themselves no other significance than that of a table of contents. Apart from that, however, the _necessity_ of the connectedness and the _immanent_ _emergence_ of the differences must be found in the treatment of the subject matter itself, for it falls within the concept's own further determining.

That through which the concept leads itself further is the _negative_ indicated above, which the concept has within its own self; this constitutes the genuinely dialectical. _Dialectic_, which has been regarded as a separate part of logic and, with respect to its purpose and standpoint, one may say, entirely misconstrued, thereby acquires {{{ 21.40 }}} an altogether different position. – Even _Platonic_ dialectic, in the Parmenides itself and elsewhere still more directly, has in part only the intention of dissolving and refuting restricted assertions through themselves, and in part simply the nothing for its result. Ordinarily dialectic is looked upon as an external and negative doing that does not belong to the subject matter itself, that has its ground in mere vanity as a subjective craving to set what is firm and true into wavering and to dissolve it, or that at least leads to nothing but the vanity of the object treated dialectically.

Kant placed dialectic higher, – and this aspect belongs among the greatest of his merits, – in that he took from it the semblance of arbitrariness which it has according to the ordinary representation, and exhibited it as _a_ _necessary_ _doing_ _of_ _reason_. So long as it passed for nothing but the art of conjuring up deceptions and producing illusions, it was simply presupposed that it plays a false game and that its whole force rests solely on concealing the fraud; that its results are merely got by trickery and are a subjective shine. Kant's dialectical expositions in the antinomies of pure reason certainly deserve no great praise when they are examined more closely, as will be done at greater length in the course of this work; but the general idea that he laid down as basis and made prevail is the _objectivity_ of _shine_ and the _necessity_ of the _contradiction_ that belongs to the _nature_ of the determinations of thinking: in the first instance, to be sure, in the manner in which these determinations are applied by reason to _things_ _in_ _themselves_; but precisely what they are in reason and in regard to what is in itself, that is their nature. This result, _grasped_ _in_ _its_ _positive_ _side_, is nothing other than the inner _negativity_ of those determinations, their self-moving soul, the principle of all natural and spiritual liveliness as such. Halt, though, at the merely abstract-negative side of the dialectical, and what comes out is nothing but the familiar claim that reason cannot cognize the infinite; – an odd outcome, given that the infinite is what is rational, to declare reason unable to cognize the rational.

In this dialectical element, as it is taken here, and hence in the grasping of the opposed in its unity, or of the positive in the negative, consists _the_ {{{ 21.41 }}} _speculative_. It is the most important aspect, but for the still unpractised, unfree power of thinking the most difficult. If such a power is still engaged in tearing itself loose from sensuously concrete representing and from argumentation, it has first to practise itself in abstract thinking, to hold concepts fast in their _determinateness_ and to learn to cognize from them. An exposition of logic for this purpose would have to keep, in its method, to the division spoken of above and, with respect to the closer content, to the determinations that emerge for the individual concepts, without entering into the dialectical. In outward shape it would come to resemble the ordinary presentation of this science, though in content it would also differ from it, and it would still serve to practise abstract if not speculative thinking, a purpose which the logic made popular by psychological and anthropological ingredients cannot even fulfil. To spirit it would offer the image of a whole ordered methodically, even though what is the soul of that edifice – the method, which has its life in the dialectical – would not itself show up in it.

Touching the _formation_ _and_ _the_ _relation_ _of_ _the_ _individual_ _to_ _logic_, let me note in closing that grammar and this science alike present themselves under two distinct aspects, with two distinct values. To whoever first steps up to logic, and to the sciences at large, it is one thing; to whoever returns to logic out of them, another. Whoever begins to get acquainted with grammar finds in its forms and laws dry abstractions, contingent rules, in general an isolated multitude of determinations that display only the value and the significance of what lies in their immediate sense; cognition at first cognizes in them nothing but them. Only to one who commands a language and knows other languages alongside it for comparison can a people's spirit and formation become perceptible within the grammar of that people's tongue; those very rules and forms carry from then on a value that is filled out and alive. Through the grammar he can cognize the expression of spirit as such, logic. Just so, one who steps up to science meets in logic, to begin with, an insulated system of abstractions that, shut up within itself, extends over none of the other branches of knowledge and science. Measured rather against the riches of one's representation of the world, against the content of the other sciences with its air of reality, and set beside absolute science's pledge to lay bare the _essence_ of those riches, the _inner_ _nature_ of spirit and of world, the _truth_, this {{{ 21.42 }}} science, in the abstractness of its shape, in the colourless and chilly plainness of its pure determinations, looks far more likely to deliver anything whatever than that pledge, and to stand substanceless in the face of such riches. The first acquaintance with logic restricts its significance to itself; its content counts as merely an isolated occupation with the determinations of thinking, _alongside_ which the other scientific occupations have a material and substance of their own, on which the logical exercises at most a formal influence, and indeed one that comes about more of itself, and for which the scientific shape and the study of it can, at a pinch, well enough be done without. The other sciences have on the whole thrown off the regular method of being a sequence of definitions, axioms, theorems and their proofs and so forth; so-called natural logic makes itself felt in them on its own account and gets along without any special cognition directed at thinking itself. Above all, however, the material and content of these sciences takes itself to be entirely independent of the logical, and is besides more appealing to sense, feeling, representation and practical interest of every kind.

So logic must indeed first be learned as something that one understands and sees into well enough, but in which scope, depth and further significance are at first missed. Only out of a deeper knowledge of the other sciences does the logical rise up for subjective spirit as something not merely abstractly universal but as the universal that embraces the wealth of the particular within itself; – just as the same moral maxim in the mouth of a youth who understands it quite correctly does not possess the significance and the scope that it has in the spirit of a man experienced in life, for whom it expresses the whole force of the substance contained in it. Thus the logical first receives the appreciation of its worth when it has become the result of the experience of the sciences; out of that it presents itself to spirit as universal truth, not as a _particular_ piece of knowledge _alongside_ other material and realities, but as the essence of all this other content.

Granted that at the outset of study the logical is not there for spirit with this conscious force, spirit receives through the logical no less the inward force by which it is guided into truth of every sort. A realm of shades is what the system of logic is, a world of simple essentialities set free from every sensuous concretion. The study of this science, the sojourn and the labour in this realm of shadows, is the absolute cultivation and discipline of consciousness. In it consciousness carries on a {{{ 21.43 }}} business remote from sensuous intuitions and purposes, from feelings, from the merely meant world of representation. Considered from its negative side, this business consists in keeping at a distance the contingency of argumentative thinking and the arbitrariness of letting these or the opposite grounds occur to one and pass as valid.

Above all, however, thought thereby gains self-subsistence and independence. It becomes at home in the abstract and in advancing through concepts without sensuous substrates, becomes an unconscious power for taking up the remaining manifoldness of bodies of knowledge and sciences into the rational form, for grasping and holding fast what is essential in them, for stripping off the external and in this way drawing the logical out of them, – or, what is the same, for filling the abstract foundation of the logical acquired beforehand through study with the substance of all truth, and for giving it the value of a universal that no longer stands as a particular alongside other particulars but reaches across all of this and is its essence, the absolutely true.
