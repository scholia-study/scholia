## {{{ 11.293 }}} Remark

Ground has been expressed in a principle, like the other determinations of reflection: _everything_ _has_ _its_ _sufficient_ _ground_. – Taken generally this says no more than that whatever _is_ must be regarded not as an _immediate_ _which_ _is_ but as something _posited_; one is not to halt at immediate existence, or at determinateness generally, but to go back out of it into its ground, in which reflection it is as sublated and in its being-in-and-for-itself. What the principle of ground pronounces, then, is the essentiality of reflection-into-self as against mere being. – To add that the ground be _sufficient_ is in truth quite superfluous, since it goes without saying; whatever the ground did not suffice for would have no ground, and yet everything is supposed to have one. _Leibniz_, however, who had the principle of sufficient ground particularly at heart and even made of it the fundamental proposition of his entire philosophy, bound up with it a deeper sense and a weightier concept than is commonly bound up with it when one halts at the immediate expression alone; though even in this sense the principle already deserves to count as important, in that being as such, in its immediacy, is thereby declared untrue and essentially something posited, whereas ground is declared the genuine immediate. What Leibniz chiefly set the _sufficiency_ of ground against was causality in its strict sense, the mechanical mode of efficacy. Since this latter is an external activity, one restricted as to its content to a single determinateness, the determinations posited by it meet _externally_ and _contingently_ in a _connection_; the partial determinations are comprehended out of their causes, but the _reference_ among them, which makes up what is essential in a concrete existence, is not contained in the causes of mechanism. That reference, the whole as essential unity, resides in the _concept_ alone, in _purpose_. For such a unity mechanical causes do not suffice, since purpose, the unity of the determinations, does not lie at their ground. By a sufficient ground Leibniz accordingly understood one that would suffice for this unity as well, one comprehending within itself not the bare causes but the _final_ _causes_. This determination of ground, however, does not yet belong in this place; the _teleological_ ground is a property of the _concept_ and of the mediation effected through it, and that mediation is reason.
