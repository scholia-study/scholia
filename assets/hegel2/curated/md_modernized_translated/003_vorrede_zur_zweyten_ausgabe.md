## {{{ 21.10 }}} Preface to the Second Edition

I have entered upon this new reworking of the Science of Logic, of which the first volume herewith appears, well aware both of the difficulty of the subject matter in itself and then of its exposition, and of the imperfection that attaches to the treatment of it in the first edition; however much I have laboured, after many further years of occupation with this science, to remedy that imperfection, I still feel I have cause enough to claim the reader's indulgence. One warrant for such a claim may in the first place be founded on the circumstance that for the content there was to be found in the earlier metaphysics and logic chiefly nothing but external material. Universally and frequently as these were pursued – the latter on down to our own days – such labour touched the speculative side very little; on the whole the same material has rather been repeated over and over, by turns thinned out to a trivial superficiality, by turns the old ballast fetched up anew in still greater bulk and dragged along, so that through such efforts, often wholly mechanical, no gain could accrue to the philosophical import. To set forth the realm of thought philosophically – that is, in its own immanent activity, or, what says the same, in its necessary unfolding – had for that reason to be a fresh undertaking, and one begun over from the start; but that acquired material, the familiar forms of thought, is to be regarded as a most important model, indeed as a necessary condition, a presupposition to be gratefully acknowledged, even if it yields only here and there a meagre thread, or the lifeless bones of a skeleton, and these even tossed together in disorder.

The forms of thought are in the first instance set out and deposited in human _language_, and in our days it cannot be recalled often enough that what distinguishes the human being from the animal is thinking. Into everything that becomes for him something inward, a representation as such, into everything that he makes his own, language has forced its way, and whatever he turns into language and utters in it contains a category, whether wrapped up, mixed in, or worked out; so natural to him is the logical, or rather the logical is his own peculiar _nature_ itself. But if nature in general, as the physical, is set over against the spiritual, {{{ 21.11 }}} then one would have to say that the logical is rather the supernatural, which forces its way into all the natural comportment of the human being, into his sensation, intuition, desire, need, drive, and thereby makes it into something human at all, even if only formally, into representations and purposes. A language has this in its favour, that it commands a wealth of logical expressions – distinctive ones, set apart – for the determinations of thought themselves; of the prepositions and articles, many already belong to relations that rest on thinking; the Chinese language is said in its development not to have got that far at all, or only poorly; but these particles appear in a wholly serviceable role, only a little more detached than augments, inflectional signs and the like. Far more important is it that in a language the determinations of thought are brought out as nouns and verbs and so stamped with objective form; the German language has many advantages here over the other modern languages; some of its words even have the further peculiarity of possessing not merely different but opposed meanings, so that in this one cannot fail to recognize a speculative spirit of the language itself; it can afford thinking a joy to come upon such words and to find, in a naive way and already lexically, the union of opposites – which is a result of speculation, though for the understanding it is nonsense – present as one word with opposite meanings. Philosophy therefore has no need at all of a special terminology; a few words are indeed to be taken over from foreign languages, though these have already acquired their citizenship in it through use, and an affected purism would be least in place just where everything most decisively turns on the matter itself. – The onward march of cultivation as a whole, and of the sciences especially, even the empirical and sensuous among them, inasmuch as they in general move within the most ordinary categories (e.g. that of a whole and the parts, of a thing and its properties, and the like), gradually brings higher relations of thought to light as well, or at least raises them to greater universality and thereby to closer attention. If, for instance, in physics the thought-determination of _force_ has become dominant, in more recent times the category of _polarity_ – which incidentally is pressed far too much à tort et à travers into everything, even into light – plays the most significant role, the determination of a difference in which the differentiated are _inseparably_ bound together; – that in this way one has moved on from the form of abstraction, of identity, by which a determinateness such as force acquires a self-subsistence, and that the form of determining, of difference, which at the same time remains as something inseparable within identity, has been brought out and has become a current representation, is of infinite importance. The study of nature carries with it, through the reality in which its {{{ 21.12 }}} objects hold themselves fast, this compulsion: to hold fast the categories which within it can no longer be passed over, even if this happens with the grossest inconsistency towards others that are _also_ left in force, and not to permit the passage – which happens more easily in the spiritual domain – over to abstractions from the opposition and to generalities.

But since the logical objects, like their expressions, are thus something universally known to anyone with an education, then, as I have said elsewhere, what is _familiar_ is not for that reason _cognized_, and it can even provoke impatience to have to occupy oneself still with what is familiar – and what indeed could be more familiar than precisely the determinations of thought that we employ on all sides, that come out of our mouths in every proposition we utter. This preface is meant to indicate the general moments of the course of cognition that starts out from this familiar material, and of the relation of scientific thinking to this natural thinking; that much, taken together with what the earlier _Introduction_ contains, will suffice to give a general representation of the sense of logical cognition, of the kind one demands to have in advance of a science, prior to the science, which is the matter itself.

It is to be regarded in the first place as an infinite advance that the forms of thinking are set loose from the stuff in which they lie sunk in self-conscious intuiting and representing, as also in our desire and volition, or rather in a desire and volition that is itself representational (– and no human desire or volition occurs without representation –), that these universalities have been brought out for themselves and, as _Plato_ and then above all _Aristotle_ did, made into an object of consideration on their own account; this gives the beginning of the cognition of them. “Only after nearly everything necessary,” says Aristotle, “and what belongs to the comfort and intercourse of life was at hand, did men begin to trouble themselves about philosophical cognition.” “In Egypt,” he had remarked before, “the mathematical sciences were developed early, because there the priestly class was early placed in a position to have leisure.” – In fact the need to busy oneself with pure thoughts has as its precondition a far journey which the spirit of humanity must already have completed; it is, one might say, a need arising from a need of necessity already stilled, from the state of needlessness at which it must have arrived, the abstraction from the material of intuiting, imagining, etc., from the concrete interests of desire, of the drives, of the will, in which material the determinations of thought lie wrapped up. In the still spaces of a thinking that has come {{{ 21.13 }}} to itself and is only within itself, the interests that move the life of peoples and of individuals fall silent. “On so many sides,” says Aristotle in the same connection, “is the nature of the human being dependent; but this science, which is not sought for any use, is alone the one free in and for itself, and for that reason it seems not to be a human possession.” – Philosophy in general still has to do with concrete objects in its thoughts – God, nature, spirit – but logic occupies itself wholly and solely with these thoughts for themselves in their complete abstraction. This logic is therefore wont to fall first of all to the study of youth, which has not yet entered into the interests of concrete life, lives in leisure with respect to them, and has as yet only to occupy itself, for its own subjective purpose, with acquiring the means and the possibility of becoming active in the objects of those interests, and so still occupies itself with these objects theoretically. Among these _means_, in contrast to the view of Aristotle just cited, logical science is reckoned; the labour spent on it is a preliminary work, its place the school, upon which the seriousness of life and activity for genuine purposes is only then supposed to follow. In life it comes to the _use_ of the categories; they are demoted from the honour of being considered for their own sake to that of _serving_ in the spiritual business of living content, in the creating and exchanging of the representations that bear upon it, – partly as _abbreviations_ by virtue of their universality; – for what an infinite mass of singular details of outer existence and of activity does the representation: battle, war, people, or sea, animal, etc., gather together into itself; – how in the representation: God, or love, etc., is an infinite mass of representations, activities, states, etc., epitomized in the _simplicity_ of such representing! – partly for the closer determination and discovery of _objective_ _relations_, where however the import and the purpose, the correctness and truth of the thinking that mixes itself in, is made wholly dependent on what is at hand, and no content-determining efficacy is ascribed to the determinations of thought on their own account. Such use of the categories, which was earlier called natural logic, is unconscious, and if in scientific reflection they are assigned in spirit the relation of serving as means, then thinking as such is made into something subordinate to the other determinations of spirit. Of our sensations, drives, interests we do not readily say that they serve us; rather they count as self-subsistent forces and powers, so that we ourselves just are this – to feel thus, to desire and will this, to place our interest in this. But again we may rather become conscious that we stand in the service of our feelings, drives, passions, interests, and of habits anyway, than that we have them in our possession, {{{ 21.14 }}} still less that, given our intimate unity with them, they serve us as means. Determinations of mind and spirit of this sort soon show themselves to us as _particulars_ over against the _universality_ as which we become conscious of ourselves and in which we have our freedom, and we take ourselves to be rather caught up in these particularities, to be dominated by them. Accordingly we can far less suppose that the forms of thought which run through all our representations – be these merely theoretical, or contain a material belonging to sensation, to drive, to the will – serve us, that we have them in our possession and not they rather us; what is left over for _us_ as against them, how are _we_, how am _I_ to set myself up as the more universal _above_ them, when they are themselves the universal as such. When we put ourselves into a sensation, a purpose, an interest, and feel ourselves restricted and unfree therein, then the place into which we are able to withdraw out of it and back into freedom is this place of self-certainty, of pure abstraction, of thinking. Or again, when we mean to talk of _things_, it is their _nature_ or their _essence_ that we name their _concept_, and a concept exists only for thinking; but of the concepts of things we shall be far less inclined to say that we dominate them, or that the determinations of thought whose complex they are serve us; on the contrary, our thinking has to restrict itself in accordance with them, and our arbitrary choice or freedom is not to want to trim them to suit itself. Insofar, then, as subjective thinking is our very own, innermost doing, and the objective concept of things constitutes the matter itself, we cannot get outside that doing, cannot stand above it, and just as little can we get beyond the nature of things. We can however leave the latter determination aside; it coincides with the former insofar as it would yield a relation of our thoughts to the matter, but only something empty, because the matter would thereby be set up as a rule for our concepts, whereas the matter can be for us nothing other than our concepts of it. If the critical philosophy understands the relation of these _three_ terms in such a way that we place _the_ _thoughts_ as a middle between _us_ _and_ _the_ _things_, in the sense that this middle rather shuts _us_ off from the _things_ instead of joining us together with them, then against this view the simple remark is to be set that just these things, which are supposed to stand on the other extreme beyond us and beyond the thoughts that relate to them, are themselves thought-things, and, as wholly indeterminate, are only a single thought-thing (– the so-called thing-in-itself) belonging to empty abstraction itself.

But this may suffice for the standpoint from which the relation vanishes according to which the determinations of thought are taken merely as being for use and as means; more important is what further hangs together with this, according {{{ 21.15 }}} to which they are wont to be grasped as external forms. – The activity of thinking that works through all our representations, purposes, interests and actions is, as was said, busy unconsciously (natural logic); what our consciousness has before it is the content, the objects of the representations, that with which the interest is filled; on this relation the determinations of thought count as _forms_ that are only _on_ _the_ _import_, not the import itself. But if it stands as was indicated above, and as is otherwise generally conceded, that the _nature_, the peculiar _essence_, the truly _abiding_ and _substantial_ amid the manifoldness and contingency of appearing and of transient expression, is the _concept_ of the matter, _the_ _universal_ _in_ _it_ _itself_, just as every human individual, infinitely peculiar though it be, has in itself the prius of all its peculiarity in being _human_, and every single animal the prius of being _animal_, then there would be no saying what an individual of that sort is still supposed to be once this foundation is stripped from a being furnished with ever so many further predicates besides, granted that this foundation, like the others, can be called a predicate. The indispensable foundation, the concept, the universal that is thought itself – insofar as one can abstract from the representation attaching to the word: thought – cannot be regarded _merely_ as an indifferent form that would be _on_ a content. But these thoughts of all natural and spiritual things, the substantial _content_ itself, are still such a content as contains manifold determinacies and still has in it the difference of a soul and a body, of the concept and a relative reality; the deeper foundation is the soul for itself, the pure concept, which is the innermost of the objects, their simple pulse of life, and equally the pulse of the subjective thinking that thinks them. The task is to raise into consciousness this _logical_ nature, which ensouls spirit and stirs and works within it. Instinct-like doing differs from intelligent and free doing in general in that the latter takes place with consciousness; when the content of what drives is brought out of immediate unity with the subject into objectivity before it, the freedom of spirit begins – of that spirit which, caught in the instinctive operation of thinking, is splintered in the bonds of its categories into an infinitely manifold material. In this net firmer knots tie themselves here and there, which are the points of support and direction of its life and consciousness; they owe their firmness and power precisely to this, that once brought before consciousness they are concepts, existing in and for themselves, of its essentiality. For the nature of spirit the weightiest point is the relation, not merely of what it is _in_ _itself_ to what it _actually_ is, but {{{ 21.16 }}} of the former to that as which it _knows_ _itself_; this self-knowing is, just because spirit is essentially consciousness, a basic determination of its _actuality_. To purify these categories, which are effective only instinctively as drives, and which are brought into the consciousness of spirit at first singly and therefore variably and confusedly, thus affording it only a fragmentary and uncertain actuality, and thereby to raise spirit within them to freedom and truth – this, then, is the higher business of logic.

What we specified as the beginning of the science, whose high worth on its own account and at the same time as a condition of genuine cognition was acknowledged above – namely to treat the concepts and the moments of the concept in general, the determinations of thought, in the first instance as forms distinct from the material and merely attaching to it – this at once announces itself in its own self as a procedure inadequate to the truth that is specified as the object and purpose of logic. For taken in that way, as bare forms held apart from the content, they are posited as standing in a determinateness which brands them finite and leaves them unfit to hold a truth that is infinite in itself. However much the true may in other respects be again associated with limitation and finitude, this is the side of its negation, of its untruth and unactuality, precisely of its end, not of the affirmation which it is as the true. Against the barrenness of the merely formal categories the instinct of sound reason has at length felt itself strong enough to abandon the knowledge of them with contempt to the province of a school logic and school metaphysics – at the same time disdaining the worth that the mere consciousness of these threads has on its own account, and remaining unconscious that in the instinct-like doing of natural logic, and still more in the more reflective rejection of any acquaintance with and cognition of the determinations of thought, it is caught in the service of an unpurified and hence unfree thinking. The simple fundamental determination, or common determination of form, of the collection of such forms is _identity_, which is asserted in the logic of this collection as a law, as A = A, as the principle of contradiction. Sound reason has so far lost its reverence for the school – which is in possession of such laws of truth and in which they are still carried on in this way – that it laughs at it on that account and holds a man to be intolerable who according to such laws knows how to speak truly: the plant is a – plant, science is – science, _and_ _so_ _forth_ _infinitely_. Concerning the formulas too which give the rules of syllogizing, which is in fact a chief use of the understanding – so unjust is it to fail to recognize that [they] have their field in cognition, within which they must hold, and at the same time that they are essential material for the thinking of reason, {{{ 21.17 }}} – the equally just consciousness has established itself that they are indifferent means at least as much of error and of sophistry, and that however one may otherwise determine truth they are unusable for the higher, e.g. the religious truth; – that they concern in general only a correctness of cognitions, not the truth.

The incompleteness of this way of considering thinking, which leaves truth to one side, can be made good only by drawing into the thinking consideration not merely what is usually reckoned to the external form, but the content along with it. It soon shows itself of its own accord that what in the nearest and most ordinary reflection is separated from form as content is in fact not supposed to be formless, not without determination in itself; so it would be only the empty, something like the abstraction of the thing-in-itself, – that it rather has form within itself, indeed has animation and import through form alone, and that it is form itself which merely turns about into the shine of a content, and thereby also into the shine of something external to this shine. With this introduction of the content into the logical consideration, it is not the _things_ but the _matter_, the _concept_ of things, that becomes the object. Here, however, one may also be reminded that there _are_ a multitude of concepts, a multitude of matters. What restricts this multitude, however, has partly been said already: that the concept as thought in general, as universal, is the immeasurable abbreviation as against the singularity of things in the multitude in which they hover before indeterminate intuiting and representing; partly, though, _a_ concept is at once, first, _the_ concept in its own self, and of that there is but one, and it is the substantial foundation; in the second place, however, it is indeed a _determinate_ concept, and this determinateness in it is what appears as content, but the determinateness of the concept is a determination of form of this substantial unity, a moment of form as totality, of _the_ _concept_ _itself_, which underlies the determinate concepts. Such a concept is not an object of sensuous intuition or of representation; it is only object, product and content _of_ _thinking_, and is the matter existing in and for itself, the logos, the reason of what is, the truth of what bears the name of things; least of all is it the logos that is to be left outside logical science. It must therefore not be an arbitrary choice whether to draw it into the science or to leave it outside. If the determinations of thought which are merely external forms are genuinely considered in their own selves, there can emerge only their finitude and the untruth of their supposed being-for-itself, and, as their truth, the concept. Hence logical science, in treating the determinations of thought which in general run through our spirit instinctively and unconsciously and which, even when they enter into language, {{{ 21.18 }}} remain non-objective and unnoticed, will also be the reconstruction of those determinations that are brought out by reflection and fixed by it as subjective forms external to the material and the import.

The exposition of no subject matter would be capable in and for itself of being so strictly and wholly immanently plastic as that of the development of thinking in its necessity; none carries this demand with it to such a degree; its science would in this respect have to surpass even mathematics, for no subject matter has within itself this freedom and independence. Such a presentation would require – as this is present in its own way in the course of mathematical consequence – that at no stage of the development should a determination of thought or a reflection occur that does not emerge immediately at that stage and has not come over into it from the preceding ones. Such abstract perfection of exposition must of course in general be renounced; already because the science has to begin with the purely simple, and hence with what is most universal and emptiest, the presentation would permit only these quite simple expressions of the simple themselves, without any further addition of a single word; – what might in the nature of the case take place would be negating reflections that took pains to hold off and remove what representation or an unregulated thinking might otherwise mix in. Such incursions into the simple immanent course of the development are, however, contingent on their own account, and the effort to fend them off is thereby itself afflicted with this contingency; besides, it is futile to want to meet _all_ such notions, precisely because they lie outside the matter, and incompleteness would at least be what would here be demanded for systematic satisfaction. But the peculiar restlessness and distraction of our modern consciousness allows nothing else than that one should likewise take more or less account of the reflections and notions that lie near at hand. A plastic presentation then also demands a plastic sense for receiving and understanding; but such plastic youths and men, listeners who so calmly, with the self-denial of their _own_ reflections and notions – with which thinking for <i>oneself</i> is impatient to prove itself – follow nothing but the matter, as Plato depicts them, could not be set up in a modern dialogue; still less could such readers be counted on. On the contrary, opponents have shown themselves to me too often and too violently who were unwilling to make the simple reflection that their notions and objections contain categories which are presuppositions and which themselves first stand in need of critique before they are used. The unconsciousness about this goes incredibly far; it makes for the fundamental misunderstanding, the bad, that is to say uncultivated, behaviour of thinking _something_ _else_ in the case of a category under consideration and not this category itself. This unconsciousness is the less to be justified in that such an _other_ {{{ 21.19 }}} consists of other determinations of thought and concepts, whereas in a system of logic just these other categories must likewise have found their place and will there be subjected to consideration for themselves. This is most striking in the preponderant mass of objections and attacks upon the first concepts or propositions of logic, _being_ and _nothing_ and _becoming_, which last, itself a simple determination, indisputably holds within it – the simplest analysis shows as much – those two determinations as moments. Thoroughness appears to demand that one examine before anything else the beginning, as the ground upon which all the rest is raised, indeed that one go no further until it has proved itself firm, and on the contrary, if this is not the case, that everything still to follow be rejected. This thoroughness has at the same time the advantage of affording the greatest relief for the business of thinking; it has before it the whole development enclosed in this germ, and takes itself to be done with everything when it is done with this germ, which is the easiest thing to dispose of, since it is the simplest, simplicity itself; it is the small amount of labour required that essentially recommends this so self-satisfied thoroughness. This limitation to the simple leaves free play to the arbitrariness of a thinking that will not itself remain simple but brings its reflections to bear upon it. With the good right of occupying itself at first _only_ with the principle, and of not thereby entering upon _the_ _sequel_, this thoroughness in its own business does the very opposite, namely brings in the _sequel_, that is, other categories than the principle alone, other presuppositions and prejudices. Such presuppositions – that infinity is different from finitude, that content is something other than form, the inner something other than the outer, that mediation likewise is not immediacy, as though one did not know such things – are at the same time brought forward by way of instruction and are not so much proved as recounted and asserted. In such instructing as a manner of conduct there lies – one cannot call it anything else – a silliness; but in point of substance there lies in it partly the illegitimacy of merely presupposing such things and simply assuming them, partly and still more the ignorance that it is the need and the business of logical thinking to investigate precisely this, whether such a finite without infinity is anything true, likewise such abstract infinity, further a formless content and a contentless form, such an inner for itself that has no expression, an externality without inwardness, and so on – whether these are _something_ _true_, likewise _something_ _actual_. – But this cultivation and discipline of thinking, through which a plastic comportment of thinking would be brought about and the impatience of intruding reflection overcome, is procured only by going further, by the study and the production of the whole development.

{{{ 21.20 }}} At the mention of Platonic exposition, whoever labours to erect anew a self-subsistent edifice of philosophical science in modern times may be reminded of the story that Plato reworked his books on the state seven times. The recollection of this, a comparison insofar as it might seem to enclose one within itself, could only press one all the more towards the wish that a work belonging to the modern world, one having before it for elaboration a deeper principle, a harder subject matter and a material of richer compass, might have been granted the free leisure of working it through seven and seventy times. As it is, however, the author, looking at the work in the face of the magnitude of the task, has had to be satisfied with what it was able to become under the circumstances of an external necessity, of the unavoidable distraction caused by the magnitude and many-sidedness of the interests of the age, and even under the doubt whether the loud noise of the day and the deafening garrulity of a conceit vain enough to confine itself to that noise still leave room for participation in the dispassionate stillness of a cognition that only thinks.

Berlin, 7 November 1831.
