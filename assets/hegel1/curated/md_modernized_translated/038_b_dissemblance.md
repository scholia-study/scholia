## {{{ 565 }}} b. Dissemblance

What the moral worldview shows us is, for one part, consciousness _itself_ _consciously generating_ its object; we do not see it stumble upon this object as something foreign, nor does the object come to be for it unconsciously; instead it proceeds throughout on a ground from which it _posits the objective essence_, and thus it knows that essence as its own self, since it knows itself as the _active_ one that generates it. It thus looks as though consciousness here arrives at its rest and satisfaction, for these it can find only where it no longer needs to reach out beyond its object, because the object no longer passes out beyond consciousness. From the other side, however, consciousness itself rather posits the object _outside itself_, as its own beyond. Yet this being that is in and for itself is equally posited as something which is not free of self-consciousness but is there for the behoof of self-consciousness and through it.

In truth, then, the moral worldview amounts to no more than the working out of this underlying contradiction along its various sides; it is, to use a Kantian expression here, where it fits best, an _entire nest_ of thoughtless contradictions. Consciousness behaves in this development so as to fix one moment and from there to pass immediately {{{ 566 }}} over to the other, sublating the first; but now that it _has set up_ this second one, it _dissembles it too_ in turn, and makes the opposite into the essence instead. {{ 333 }} At the same time it is conscious of its own contradiction and of its _dissembling too_, since it moves over from one moment to the opposed one _immediately_ and precisely _in relation to that moment itself_; _because_ one moment possesses no reality in its eyes, it posits just that moment as _real_, or, what comes to the same, in order to maintain that _one moment_ has being in itself, it maintains that the _opposed_ one is what has being in itself. By this it admits that in fact it is serious about neither of them. This is to be examined more closely in the moments of this dizzying movement.

Let us for the moment leave standing the presupposition of an actual moral consciousness, since it is not made in immediate relation to anything that went before, and let us take up instead the first postulate, the harmony between morality and nature. That harmony is to have being _in itself_, not for the actual consciousness, and not as something present; the present is rather nothing but the contradiction between the two. Within the present, _morality_ is taken to be _on hand_, while actuality is given a position in which it fails to harmonize with morality. But the _actual_ moral consciousness is one that _acts_; the actuality of its morality consists precisely in this. In _acting_ itself, however, that position is immediately dissembled; for to act is nothing else than realizing {{{ 567 }}} the inner moral purpose, nothing else than bringing forth a _purpose-determined actuality_, which is to say the harmony of actuality itself with the moral purpose. The carrying out of the action is at the same time there for consciousness, being the _presence_ of that unity in which actuality and purpose are one; and since consciousness, in the action once carried out, gives itself actuality as this singular one, or intuits existence as having come back into it, and since enjoyment lies just here, the actuality of the moral purpose also contains within it that form of itself which bears the name of enjoyment and happiness. — Acting therefore fulfills immediately and in fact what had been set up as not taking place and was supposed to be only a postulate, only a beyond. Through the deed, then, consciousness declares that it is not serious about postulating, since the sense of acting is rather to make present what was supposed not to be present. And since it is for the sake of acting that the harmony gets postulated — what is to become _actual_ through acting must, after all, already be so _in itself_, or else no actuality would be _possible_, the linkage between acting and the postulate has this shape: for the sake of acting, that is, for the sake of an _actual_ harmony of purpose with actuality, that very harmony is posited as _not actual_, as _beyond_.

{{{ 568 }}} In _acting_, then, there is no seriousness whatever about the _inadequacy_ of purpose and actuality; the _acting_ itself, by contrast, does seem to be meant seriously. {{ 334 }} But in fact the actual action is only the action of the _singular_ consciousness, hence itself only something singular, and the work is contingent. But the purpose of reason, as the universal purpose embracing all things, is no less than the world entire; a final purpose reaching far past the content of this singular action, and therefore to be set beyond every piece of actual acting whatever. Because the universal best is what is to be carried out, nothing good gets done. In fact, however, what has now been set up, namely the _nullity_ of actual acting and the _reality_ that belongs to the _whole_ purpose alone, is on every side dissembled once again. Moral action is neither contingent nor restricted, since its essence is pure _duty_; pure duty makes up the _one and only whole_ purpose; and so the action, as that purpose actualized, is the accomplishment of the whole absolute purpose, however restricted its content may otherwise be. Or if once again actuality is taken as nature, which has laws of its _own_ and stands over against pure duty, so that duty is unable to realize its law in nature, then, since duty as such is the essence, what is really at stake is _not the accomplishment_ of pure duty, though pure duty is the whole {{{ 569 }}} purpose; for what such accomplishment would aim at is not pure duty but its opposite, _actuality_. But that actuality is not at stake is dissembled once again; for by the concept of moral acting, pure duty is essentially a consciousness that is _active_; acting is therefore indeed called for, absolute duty is to find expression throughout the whole of nature, and the moral law is to become a law of nature.

If, then, we let this _highest good_ count as the essence, then consciousness has no seriousness whatever about morality. For within this highest good, the law that nature has is no other than the law of morality. Moral acting itself thereby drops away, for acting takes place only on the presupposition of something negative that is to be sublated through the action. But if nature does conform to the ethical law, then that law would surely be violated by acting, by the sublating of what is. — On that assumption, then, what is conceded to be the essential state is one in which moral acting has no place at all and is entirely superfluous. The postulate of the harmony of morality and actuality, of a harmony posited through the concept of moral acting, whose business is to bring the two into agreement — therefore expresses itself from this side too as follows: because moral acting is the absolute purpose, the absolute purpose turns out to be that moral acting should not be on hand at all.

{{{ 570 }}} If we set these moments side by side, the moments through which consciousness has rolled onward in its moral representing, it becomes clear that consciousness sublates each of them again in its opposite. {{ 335 }} It starts out from the claim that _for it_ morality and actuality are not in harmony, yet it is not serious about this, for in the action the presence of this harmony is there _for it_. Nor, however, is it serious about this _acting_, since acting is something singular; for it has so lofty a purpose, _the highest good_. But this in turn is only a dissembling of the matter, for in it all acting and all morality would drop away. Or rather, it is not really serious about _moral_ acting; what is most desirable, what is absolute, is that the highest good be carried out and moral acting be superfluous.

From this result consciousness, in its contradictory movement, must roll further onward and must necessarily dissemble the _sublating_ of moral acting once again. Morality is the in-itself; if it is to take place, the world's final purpose cannot already be carried out, and the moral consciousness has instead to be _for itself_ and to come upon a _nature opposed_ to it. But in its own self it must be complete. This leads to the second postulate, that of the harmony between it and the nature immediately attached to it, namely sensibility. Its purpose the moral self-consciousness sets up as pure, as independent of inclinations and drives, such that this purpose {{{ 571 }}} has annihilated within itself the purposes of sensibility. — Yet consciousness dissembles once more this sublation of the sensible essence that it has just set up. Consciousness acts, it brings its purpose into actuality, and the self-conscious sensibility that is supposed to be sublated turns out to be precisely this middle term standing between pure consciousness and actuality — sensibility is the instrument by which the former actualizes itself, the organ, and it is what goes under the names of drive and inclination. There is therefore no seriousness about sublating inclinations and drives, since it is just these that are _self-consciousness actualizing itself_. But they are not to be _suppressed_ either; they are merely to stand _in conformity_ with reason. And stand in conformity with it they do, for moral _acting_ is nothing other than consciousness actualizing itself and thereby giving itself the shape of a _drive_; that is, it is immediately the present harmony between drive and morality. In fact, however, the drive is not merely this empty shape, which might have within it some spring other than itself and be driven by that. For sensibility is a nature that has its own laws and its own springs in its own self; morality can therefore not be serious about being the mainspring of the drives, the inclining angle of the inclinations. For since the drives have their own fixed determinateness and their peculiar content, it would rather be the consciousness with which they conformed that conformed to them; a conformity that the moral self-consciousness declines. {{ 336 }} The harmony {{{ 572 }}} of the two is thus only _in itself_ and _postulated_. — Moral acting had a moment ago set up the _present_ harmony between morality and sensibility, but that _is now_ dissembled; the harmony lies beyond consciousness in a foggy remoteness where nothing can any longer be told apart with precision or comprehended, since our attempt a moment ago to comprehend this unity did not succeed. — In this in-itself, however, consciousness surrenders itself entirely. Such an in-itself is its moral completion, where morality's struggle with sensibility has come to an end and sensibility conforms to morality in a way that cannot be grasped. — For that reason this completion is once more nothing but a dissembling of the matter; for in it, in fact, _morality_ itself would rather be the thing that surrendered, morality being nothing but the consciousness of the absolute purpose in its _purity_, and therefore in _opposition_ to every other purpose; and morality is the _activity_ of this pure purpose to just the degree that it knows itself to be raised above sensibility, knows sensibility's meddling, and knows its own opposition to sensibility and its struggle with it. — That it is not serious about moral completion, consciousness itself declares outright in the very fact that it dissembles completion out into _infinity_, that is, asserts it to be never completed.

Rather, then, only this intermediate state of non-completion is what is valid for it; a state that is nevertheless supposed to be at least a _progressing_ toward completion. Yet it cannot be this either, for {{{ 573 }}} progressing in morality would rather be an approach to morality's own downfall. The goal, namely, would be that nothing spoken of above, the sublating of morality and of consciousness itself; and to draw ever closer to nothing is to _diminish_. Moreover, _progressing_ as such would, exactly like _diminishing_, presuppose differences of _magnitude_ within morality; but of such differences there can be no talk in morality. In morality, as the consciousness for which the ethical purpose is _pure_ duty, no diversity whatever is to be thought of, least of all the superficial diversity of magnitude; there is only a single virtue, only a single pure duty, only a single morality.

Since, then, moral completion is not what is meant seriously, but rather the middle state, that is, as was just discussed, non-morality, we are brought back from another side to what the first postulate contains. For there is no seeing how happiness could be demanded on account of this moral consciousness's _worthiness_. Conscious as it is of its own non-completion, it cannot in fact demand happiness as a thing merited, as something it would be worthy of; it can only beg for happiness out of a free grace, that is, crave happiness _as such_, in and for itself, and expect it not on that absolute ground but by chance and arbitrary favor. {{ 337 }} — In just this, non-morality declares what it is — that what is at stake is not morality but {{{ 574 }}} happiness in and for itself, without any relation to morality.

This second side of the moral worldview sublates the further assertion made by the first side as well, the assertion that presupposed a disharmony between morality and happiness. — For the experience is claimed to be made that in this present things often go badly for the moral person, while for the immoral one they often go happily. Yet the intermediate state of uncompleted morality, which has emerged as the essential thing, plainly shows that this perception and would-be experience is only a dissembling of the matter. For if morality is uncompleted, that is, if in fact morality is _not_, what can the experience that things go badly for it amount to? — Since it has at the same time come out that what is at stake is happiness in and for itself, it shows itself that in the judgment that things go well for the immoral person no wrong was meant to be taking place here. Since morality as a whole is uncompleted, the labeling of an individual as immoral falls away _in itself_, and so has none but an arbitrary ground. By this alone the judgment of experience acquires its sense and content, namely that to some people happiness in and for itself ought not to fall; that is, the judgment is _envy_, which takes morality for its cloak. The ground, however, why others should receive their share of so-called good fortune is that good {{{ 575 }}} friendliness which _grants_ and _wishes_ them, and itself, this grace, that is, this piece of chance.

Morality in the moral consciousness, then, is uncompleted; this is what is now being set up; but its essence is to be only what is _completed and pure_; uncompleted morality is therefore impure, or it is immorality. Morality itself is thus in an essence other than the actual consciousness; that other essence is a holy moral lawgiver. — Morality as _uncompleted_ within consciousness, which is the ground of this postulating, signifies _in the first instance_ that morality, being posited within consciousness as _actual_, thereby enters into relation with an _other_, with an existence, so that otherness or difference attaches to morality itself, and from this a manifold multitude of moral commandments arises. At the same time, however, these _many_ duties count as inessential for the moral self-consciousness; for what is at stake is the _one_ pure duty alone, and _for it_ these duties, so far as they are _determinate_, hold no truth. Their truth, accordingly, can lie nowhere but in another, and through a holy lawgiver they are what they are not for consciousness, namely holy. — Yet this is itself once again only a dissembling of the matter. For to itself the moral self-consciousness is the absolute, and nothing counts as duty except what _it_ _knows_ to be duty. {{ 338 }} But it knows pure duty alone as duty; whatever is not holy for it is not holy in itself either, and what lacks holiness in itself can receive no consecration from {{{ 576 }}} the holy essence. Nor is the moral consciousness serious at all about letting something be made holy _through a consciousness other_ than itself; for to it only that is holy which is holy to it _through itself and within itself_. — Just as little, then, is it serious about this other essence being a holy one, for in that essence something was supposed to attain essentiality which for the moral consciousness, that is, in itself, has no essentiality.

If the holy essence was postulated on the ground that within it duty would hold good not as pure duty but rather as a plurality of _determinate_ duties, then this too must be dissembled again, and the other essence must count as holy only insofar as _pure duty_ alone is valid within it. And in fact pure duty is valid only in another essence, not in the moral consciousness. Even though pure morality seems to be the one thing valid within that consciousness, the matter must be placed otherwise, since the moral consciousness is at the same time natural consciousness. Within it, morality stands affected and conditioned by sensibility, and so is not in and for itself but a contingency of the free _will_; while in the moral consciousness as pure _will_ it is a contingency of _knowledge_; _in and for itself_, therefore, morality resides in another essence.

This essence, then, counts here as purely completed morality for the reason that in it morality bears no relation {{{ 577 }}} to nature and sensibility. The _reality_ of pure duty, however, consists in its _actualization_ within nature and sensibility. What the moral consciousness takes its own imperfection to consist in is that morality, within it, holds a _positive_ relation toward nature and sensibility, for it regards this as an essential moment of morality, that morality hold nothing but a _negative_ relation toward them. The pure moral essence, by contrast, elevated as it is above all _struggle_ with nature and sensibility, holds no _negative_ relation toward them. In fact, then, all that is left to it is the _positive_ relation toward them, that is, precisely what a moment ago counted as the uncompleted, as the immoral. But _pure morality_ cut off entirely from actuality, so that it would be just as much without any positive relation toward actuality, would amount to an unconscious and unactual abstraction, and in such an abstraction the concept of morality, namely to be the thinking of pure duty together with a willing and a doing, would be sublated outright. This so purely moral essence is therefore once more a dissembling of the matter, and must be surrendered.

{{ 339 }} Within this purely moral essence, though, the moments of that contradiction in which this synthetic representing keeps driving itself about, along with the opposed _alsos_ that it lets follow one after another without ever bringing these thoughts of its own together, each opposite forever relieving the other, draw so {{{ 578 }}} close to one another that consciousness must here abandon its moral worldview and take flight back into itself.

Consciousness cognizes its morality as not completed on the ground that a sensibility and a nature standing opposed to morality affect it, and these in part cloud morality itself as such, in part let a whole crowd of duties spring up, which land consciousness in embarrassment whenever it comes to the concrete case of actual acting; for each case is the concretion of many moral relations, in the way that any object of perception is a thing possessing many properties; and since _determinate_ duty is a purpose, it carries a content, and its _content_ is a portion of the purpose, so that morality is not pure. — Morality thus has its _reality_ in another essence. But this reality means nothing other than that morality here is _in_ and _for itself_ — _for itself_, that is, that it is the morality of a _consciousness_; _in itself_, that is, that it has _existence_ and _actuality_. — Within that first, uncompleted consciousness morality is not carried through; there morality amounts to the _in-itself_ in the sense of a _thought-thing_, since it keeps company with nature and sensibility, with the actuality belonging to being and to consciousness, which constitutes its content, while nature and sensibility are what is morally null. — In the second, morality is on hand as _completed_, and not as an uncarried-out thought-thing. But this completion consists precisely in morality's having, in a _consciousness, actuality_, and likewise _free actuality_, {{{ 579 }}} existence as such, in its being not the empty but the filled and content-laden; — that is, morality's completion gets posited in this, that what a moment ago was determined as the morally null is on hand in it and on it. At the one time morality is supposed to have validity simply only as the unactual thought-thing of pure abstraction, yet just as much to have no validity in this manner; its truth is supposed to consist in standing opposed to actuality, entirely free and empty of actuality, and then once more in being actuality itself.

The syncretism of these contradictions, laid out in the moral worldview, collapses into itself, since the difference on which it rests, the difference between what must necessarily be thought and posited and what is nevertheless at the same time inessential, becomes a difference that no longer even lies in the words. What gets posited at the end as something diverse, whether as the null or as the real, is one and the same thing throughout, namely existence and actuality; and what is supposed to be absolutely nothing but the _beyond_ of actual being and consciousness, while equally being the null only within that being and as a beyond, is pure duty and the knowledge of pure duty as the essence. {{ 340 }} The consciousness that draws this difference which is none, that declares actuality to be at once the null and the real — and declares pure morality to be just as much the true {{{ 580 }}} essence as the essenceless, utters together the thoughts it had previously kept apart, and thereby states of itself that it is not serious about this determination and about setting the moments of the _self_ and of the _in-itself_ apart from one another; rather, what it declares to be what absolutely _has being_ outside consciousness it in fact holds enclosed inside the self belonging to self-consciousness, and what it declares to be the absolutely _thought_ or the absolute _in-itself_ it takes, for just that reason, to be something devoid of truth. — It comes to be for consciousness that this setting of the moments apart is a dissembling, and it would be _hypocrisy_ were it to hold onto that setting apart all the same. But as pure moral self-consciousness it flees back into itself with revulsion from this inequality between its _representing_ and what its _essence_ is, from this untruth which declares to be true what for it is valid as untrue. It is _pure conscience_, which spurns such a moral representation of the world; it is _within itself_ the simple spirit certain of itself, which acts conscientiously and immediately, without the mediation of those representations, and which has its truth in this immediacy. — But if this world of dissemblance is simply moral self-consciousness developing through its own moments, and so is its _reality_, then in going back into itself it will, as regards its essence, become nothing else; that going-back-into-itself is rather only the _attained consciousness_ that {{{ 581 }}} its truth is a merely pretended one. It _would_ still have to _pass off_ that truth as _its own_ truth, for it would have to utter and exhibit itself as an objective representation, yet it _would know_ that this is only a dissembling; it would thereby in fact be hypocrisy, and that _spurning_ of that dissembling would already be the first expression of hypocrisy.
