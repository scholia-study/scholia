## a. The Moral Worldview

Duty is what self-consciousness knows as the absolute essence; duty alone binds it, and this substance is nothing other than its own pure consciousness; for it, duty can never acquire the form of something alien. {{ 325 }} Sealed up within itself in this fashion, though, moral self-consciousness is not yet set down and examined as _consciousness_. Its object amounts to immediate knowledge, and having been saturated by the self in so pure a way, that object is no object. But being essentially mediation as well as negativity, it carries in its concept a relation to _otherness_; and so it is consciousness. This otherness, on the one hand, is for it a wholly _meaningless_ actuality, since duty makes up its one essential purpose and object. But because this consciousness is so perfectly sealed within itself, its stance toward that otherness is one of perfect freedom and {{{ 551 }}} indifference, and existence is therefore, on the other side, an existence that self-consciousness has entirely released, an existence relating to nothing but itself; the more freedom self-consciousness gains, the more the negative object of its consciousness gains as well. Through this it becomes a world complete in itself, rounded out into an individuality all its own, a self-subsistent totality of laws peculiar to it, together with a self-subsistent course and free realization of these, — a _nature_ in general, whose laws as much as its doings belong to it, as to an essence that troubles itself about moral self-consciousness as little as the latter troubles itself about nature.

From this determination onward a _moral worldview_ shapes itself, and it consists in the _relation_ in which _moral_ being-in-and-for-itself stands to _natural_ being-in-and-for-itself. Underlying this relation there is, on one side, the utter _indifference_ and the peculiar _self-subsistence_ that _nature_ and _moral_ purposes and activity have toward one another, and, on the other side, the consciousness that duty is the one and only essentiality while nature is entirely non-self-subsistent and without essentiality. What the moral worldview contains is the development of those moments that lie within this relation of presuppositions so utterly at odds.

To begin with, then, moral consciousness in general is presupposed; duty counts for it as the essence, for a consciousness that is _actual_ and _active_ and that fulfills duty in its actuality and its deed. For this {{{ 552 }}} moral consciousness, however, the freedom of nature is presupposed just as much, or it _experiences_ that nature takes no interest in giving it any consciousness that its own actuality is at one with nature's, and so leaves it to become _perhaps happy_, _perhaps_ also _not_. The immoral consciousness, on the contrary, may find its own realization by sheer chance, precisely where the moral consciousness finds nothing but an _occasion_ for acting and does not see the good fortune of execution and the enjoyment of accomplishment come to it through what it does. It therefore finds ground enough for lamenting a condition in which it and existence fail to match, and lamenting the injustice that restricts it to possessing its object merely as _pure duty_ while refusing it the sight of that object, and of _itself_, made actual.

{{ 326 }} Happiness is something moral consciousness cannot give up; it cannot drop this moment from its absolute purpose. A purpose pronounced as _pure duty_ has it essentially in it to contain this _singular_ self-consciousness; an absolute moment of morality was made up by _individual conviction_ and the knowledge of that conviction. This moment, taken at the _purpose_ that has become _objective_, at _fulfilled_ duty, is the _singular_ consciousness that intuits itself as realized, that is, _enjoyment_, which accordingly does not lie immediately within the concept of morality regarded as _disposition_, {{{ 553 }}} but only within the concept of its _realization_. By this very fact, however, enjoyment lies in morality as _disposition_ as well; for what disposition drives at is not to stay disposition set over against acting, but to _act_, that is, to make itself actual. Stated as the whole, with awareness of its moments, the purpose is accordingly this: that duty fulfilled should be a purely moral action and realized _individuality_ alike, and that _nature_, as the side of _singularity_ standing over against the abstract purpose, should be _one_ with that purpose. — Necessary as it is to experience the disharmony of both sides, since nature is free, duty is just as much the one essential thing, and nature over against duty is what has no self. That entire _purpose_, which the harmony makes up, has actuality itself inside it. It is likewise the _thought_ of _actuality_. The harmony between morality and nature — or, given that nature comes under consideration only to the degree that consciousness undergoes nature's unity with it — the harmony between morality and happiness gets _thought_ as necessarily _being_, or it stands _postulated_. For _to demand_ says that something not yet actual is thought as _being_; a necessity that belongs not to the _concept_ qua concept but to _being_. But this necessity is essentially, at the same time, a relation established through the concept. The _being_ that is demanded thus does not fall to the representing of a contingent consciousness; it lies rather in morality's own concept, whose true {{{ 554 }}} content is nothing but the _unity_ of _pure_ with _singular_ consciousness; and to the singular consciousness there belongs this, that such unity be _for it_ as an actuality, which, in the purpose's _content_, is happiness, while in its _form_ it is existence as such. — The existence thus demanded, or the unity of the two, is accordingly no wish, and, taken as a purpose, not one whose attainment might still be in doubt; it is, rather, reason's own demand, or reason's immediate certainty and presupposition.

That first experience, together with this postulate, does not stand alone; on the contrary, a whole circle of postulates opens out. {{ 327 }} For nature is not just this altogether free _external_ manner in which consciousness, as in a pure object, would have to bring its purpose to realization. Consciousness itself is, _in its own self_, essentially something _for which_ this other free actual being is; that is, it is itself contingent and natural. The nature that belongs to consciousness is _sensibility_, and sensibility, in the _shape_ of willing, as _drives_ and _inclinations_, possesses on its own account a _determinate_ essentiality of its own, or _singular purposes_, and therefore stands over against the pure will and the pure purpose of that will. Over against such opposition, though, the essence for pure consciousness lies rather in the relation of sensibility to it, in sensibility's absolute unity with it. Pure thinking on the one hand and the sensibility of consciousness on the other are _in themselves one consciousness_, and it is precisely pure thinking that this pure unity exists for {{{ 555 }}} and within; but for pure thinking taken as consciousness there stands the opposition between itself and the drives. Within this strife of reason and sensibility, what ranks as the essence for reason is that the strife dissolve itself and that the unity of the two come forth as _result_, a unity which is not that _original_ one, in which both are in a single individual, but one issuing from the _known_ opposition of the two. Morality becomes _actual_ only in such a unity, for that unity harbors the opposition by which the self is consciousness, or is for the first time actual and in fact a self, universal at the same time; or what finds expression in it is that _mediation_ which, as we see, morality essentially requires. — Sensibility, one of the two moments of the opposition, is simply the _otherness_ or the negative, while duty's pure thinking is the essence from which nothing may be given up; so the unity produced seems able to arise only through the sublation of sensibility. Yet since it is itself a moment of this becoming, the moment of _actuality_, one will have to be satisfied, so far as the unity goes, with saying that sensibility should be _conformable_ to morality. — This unity as well is a _postulated being_; it _is_ not _there_, since what _is there_ is consciousness, that is, the opposition between sensibility and pure consciousness. At the same time, though, it is not an in-itself in the manner of {{{ 556 }}} the first postulate, where free nature makes up one side, so that its harmony with moral consciousness falls outside that consciousness; nature here is instead the nature belonging to consciousness in its own self, and what is at issue here is morality as such, a harmony that belongs to the acting self; consciousness accordingly has to produce that harmony itself and to go on making progress within morality. Its _completion_, however, must be _pushed off into infinity;_ for were completion actually to arrive, moral consciousness would sublate itself. For _morality_ counts as moral _consciousness_ only as the negative essence, for whose pure duty sensibility has only a _negative_ meaning, is only _not conformable_. {{ 328 }} Within the harmony, on the other hand, what vanishes is _morality_ as _consciousness_, that is, its _actuality_, just as within moral _consciousness_, or within actuality, what vanishes is morality's _harmony_. Completion, therefore, is not something actually attainable; it can only be thought as an _absolute task_, that is, as a task that simply stays a task. At the same time, however, one has to think its content as content that simply must _be_ and does not stay a task; whether one represents consciousness in that goal as wholly sublated or not; how the matter really stands admits of no clear {{{ 557 }}} discrimination any longer in the dark distance of infinity, into which, for just that reason, the reaching of the goal has to be shoved. Strictly it will have to be said that the determinate representation ought neither to interest us nor to be sought after, because it leads into contradictions, — of a task that is to stay a task and yet be fulfilled, — of a morality that is to be consciousness no longer, actual no longer. Yet through the consideration that a completed morality would harbor a contradiction, the holiness of the moral essentiality would suffer, and absolute duty would show itself as something unactual.

The first postulate laid down a harmony between morality and objective nature, which is the _world's_ final purpose; the second laid down a harmony between morality and the sensuous will, which is the final purpose belonging to _self-consciousness_ as such; thus the former yields harmony under the form of the _in-itself_, the latter under the form of _being-for-itself_. But what joins these two extreme final purposes, both of them thought, as their middle, is the very movement of _actual_ acting. Harmonies they are, whose moments in their abstract differentiation have not yet become an object; that happens in actuality, where the sides come forward inside consciousness proper, each of them as the _other_ of the other. Whereas earlier the harmonies were only the separated ones that are _in themselves_ and _for themselves_, the postulates that arise in this way contain harmonies that are now _in and for themselves_.

Moral consciousness, as the _simple knowledge_ and the _willing_ of pure _duty_, is in its acting {{{ 558 }}} related to an object that is opposed to its own simplicity — to the actuality of the _manifold case_, and thereby has a manifold moral _relationship_. What arises here is, as regards content, the _many_ laws in general, and as regards form, the mutually contradicting powers of the consciousness that knows and of the unconscious. — Where the _many duties_ are concerned, first of all, moral consciousness holds nothing valid in them but _pure duty_; the _many duties_, taken as many, are _determinate_ ones, and as such they are nothing holy for moral consciousness. {{ 329 }} Yet since they are _necessary_ through the concept of _acting_, which encompasses a manifold actuality and hence a manifold moral relation, they have to be regarded as being in and for themselves. Since they can further exist only inside a moral _consciousness_, they are simultaneously present in a consciousness other than the one for which pure duty alone is the pure in-and-for-itself and holy.

It is postulated, therefore, that an _other_ consciousness exists, one that sanctifies these duties, or that knows them as duties and so wills them. The first consciousness keeps pure duty _indifferent_ toward every _determinate content_, and duty is nothing but this indifference toward content. The second, by contrast, harbors the relation to acting, no less essential, along with the _necessity_ of _determinate_ content; because duties count for it as _determinate_ duties, content as such is for that consciousness {{{ 559 }}} every bit as essential as the form by which content is duty. This consciousness is thereby one within which universal and particular are absolutely at one, so that its concept coincides with the concept of the harmony between morality and happiness. For this opposition expresses just as much how the _self-equal_ moral consciousness stands separated from actuality, which as _multiple being_ runs counter to the simple essence of duty. But whereas the first postulate expressed only the harmony between morality and nature as _being_, since nature there is this negative side of self-consciousness, namely the moment of _being_, now by contrast this _in-itself_ is posited essentially as consciousness. For what has being now stands in the form of the _content_ that _duty_ has, or it is the _determinateness_ found in a _determinate duty_. The in-itself is accordingly the unity of things that, as _simple essentialities_, essentialities of thinking, exist only within a consciousness. This consciousness is from now on a master and ruler over the world, one who produces the harmony between morality and happiness and who likewise sanctifies the duties as _many_. The latter amounts to saying that determinate duty cannot be immediately holy for the consciousness of _pure duty_; but since determinate duty is likewise _necessary_ on account of actual acting, which is something determinate, its necessity falls outside that consciousness into another one, which is thereby what mediates between determinate and pure duty and the ground on which determinate duty too is valid.

{{{ 560 }}} In actual action, however, consciousness relates as this self, as something entirely singular; it is aimed at actuality as such and takes actuality for its purpose, since what it wants is to accomplish something. _Duty as such_ therefore falls outside consciousness into another essence, which is the consciousness and the holy legislator of pure duty. For the acting consciousness, just because it is something acting, what has immediate validity is the other of pure duty; pure duty accordingly forms the content of a different consciousness and is holy for the first only mediately, namely in that other.

{{ 330 }} Since it is hereby posited that duty's validity as what is _in and for itself_ holy falls outside actual consciousness, this consciousness stands in general, on the one side, as the moral consciousness that is _imperfect_. In respect of its _knowledge_, accordingly, it knows itself as one whose knowledge, like its conviction, is incomplete and contingent; in respect of its _willing_, as one whose purposes are infected with sensibility. Because it is unworthy, it therefore cannot look upon happiness as necessary, but only as something contingent, and can look for it only out of grace.

Even though its actuality is imperfect, duty nevertheless counts as the essence in the eyes of its _pure_ will and knowledge; in the concept, taken as set over against reality, or in thinking, it is therefore perfect. The absolute essence, however, is just this thought-of being, postulated on the far side of actuality; {{{ 561 }}} hence it is that thought within which knowledge and willing that are morally imperfect pass for perfect, and which, taking them as of full weight, thereby dispenses happiness in proportion to worthiness, that is, in proportion to the _merit ascribed_ to it.

Herewith the worldview is completed; for within the concept that moral self-consciousness has, both sides, pure duty and actuality, are posited in one unity, and neither of them is thereby posited as being in and for itself, but rather as a _moment_, or as sublated. This comes to be for consciousness in the moral worldview's last part; consciousness, namely, places pure duty in an essence other than what it is itself, that is, it posits it partly as something _represented_, partly as something that is not what has validity in and for itself, since what is non-moral rather counts as perfect. In the same way it posits itself as one whose actuality, being unsuited to duty, is sublated and, as _sublated_, or in the _representation_ that belongs to the absolute essence, no longer stands in contradiction to morality.

For moral consciousness itself, however, its moral worldview carries no such meaning, that consciousness develops its own concept in it and makes that concept its object; it is aware neither of this opposition of form nor of the opposition on the side of content, {{{ 562 }}} whose parts it neither sets in relation nor compares with one another, but instead rolls onward in the course of its development, never being the _concept_ that holds the moments together. For its knowledge extends only to the _pure essence_, or to the object to the extent that this object is _duty_, insofar as it constitutes the _abstract_ object belonging to its pure consciousness, as pure knowledge or as its own self. It relates, then, only in a thinking way, not in a comprehending one. {{ 331 }} The object belonging to its _actual_ consciousness is therefore not yet transparent to it; nor is it that absolute concept which alone seizes _otherness_ as such, or seizes its own absolute opposite as itself. Its own actuality, along with every objective actuality, does indeed count for it as the _inessential_; its freedom, however, is that of pure thinking, and precisely for this reason nature has arisen over against pure thinking as something just as free. Because both are in it in the same way, the _freedom of being_ and the fact that being is enclosed within consciousness, its object turns into something _having being_ and yet _at the same time_ nothing but _thought_; in this view's last part the content gets posited essentially so that its _being_ counts as something _represented_, and this joining of being and thinking is pronounced to be what it actually is, namely _representing_.

If we look at the moral worldview in this manner, taking this objective mode to be nothing else than the very concept of moral self-consciousness, which it renders objective for itself, {{{ 563 }}} then through this awareness about the form of the worldview's origin a different shape of its presentation results. — The starting point, namely, is _actual_ moral self-consciousness, or the circumstance that _such a one exists_. For the concept posits it in the determination that all actuality whatever possesses essence for it only insofar as that actuality conforms to duty, and the concept posits this essence as knowledge, that is, in immediate unity with the self that is actual; consequently this unity is actual on its own account, it _is_ an actual moral consciousness. — Now this consciousness, being consciousness, represents to itself its content as an object, namely as the _world's final purpose_, as the harmony between morality and all actuality. But since it represents this unity as an _object_ and is not yet the concept that holds power over the object as such, then this unity is for it a negative of self-consciousness, or it falls outside consciousness as a beyond of its actuality, yet at the same time as something that is _also_ thought as _being_, though merely thought.

What then remains over for it, which as self-consciousness is an _other_ than the object, is the disharmony obtaining between the consciousness of duty on one side and actuality on the other, its own actuality at that. The proposition now reads: _there is no morally completed actual_ self-consciousness; — and since anything moral exists only where it is completed, duty being the _pure_ unmixed _in-itself_ and morality consisting solely in conformity to this {{{ 564 }}} purity, — the second proposition says quite generally that _nothing morally actual_ exists.

But since, thirdly, it is a single self, the unity of duty and actuality belongs to it _in itself_; that unity accordingly comes to be its object as completed morality, — though as a _beyond_ of its actuality, — a beyond that is nevertheless supposed to be actual.

{{ 332 }} Within this goal, which is the synthetic unity of the two propositions preceding, self-conscious actuality no less than duty is posited merely as a sublated moment; for neither stands singly, but since it belongs to their essential determination to be _free of the other_, in the unity neither is any longer free of the other, so each is sublated, and thus, as regards content, they become an object of which _each is valid for the other_, while as regards form, this mutual exchange of theirs is at the same time only _represented_. — Or: what is _actually not_ moral, since it is likewise pure thinking and raised above its own actuality, is nonetheless moral in representation and is taken for fully valid. Thereby the first proposition, that a moral self-consciousness _does exist_, is reinstated, but tied to the second, that there _is_ none: namely, one _does exist_, though only in representation; or there is indeed none, yet another consciousness lets it pass for such.
