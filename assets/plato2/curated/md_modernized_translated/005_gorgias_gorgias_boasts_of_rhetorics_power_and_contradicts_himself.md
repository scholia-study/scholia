## Gorgias Boasts of Rhetoric's Power, and Contradicts Himself

@ Gorgias

{{{ 456a }}} And whenever there's a choice to be made of the kind you were just talking about, Socrates, you'll see that it's the orators who give the advice, and who carry the vote on these matters.

@ Socrates

It's exactly because I find that astonishing, Gorgias, that I've been asking all along what the power of rhetoric actually is. Looking at it this way, its scale strikes me as something almost superhuman.

@ Gorgias

If you knew everything about it, Socrates, you'd see that it gathers up under itself, one might say, every other power there is. {{{ 456b }}} And I'll give you a striking proof of it: many times now I've gone in with my brother, or with other doctors, to see some patient who wouldn't drink his medicine, or let the doctor cut or cauterize him, and when the doctor couldn't persuade him, I did — by no other craft than rhetoric. And I say that if a rhetorical man and a doctor went to any city you like, and had to compete in speech before the assembly or some other gathering over which of them should be chosen as doctor, the doctor would be nowhere {{{ 456c }}} in the running, and the one able to speak would be chosen instead, if he wanted it. And if he competed against any other craftsman whatever, the rhetorical man would persuade them to choose him over anyone else; there's no subject on which the rhetorical man couldn't speak more persuasively than any other craftsman, before a crowd. So great and so remarkable, then, is the power of this craft; one must, however, Socrates, use rhetoric as one would any other competitive skill. For with any {{{ 456d }}} other competitive skill, too, one shouldn't use it against everyone just because one has learned boxing, or wrestling, or fighting in armour, so as to be stronger than both friends and enemies — that's no reason to strike one's friends, or stab and kill them. And by god, if someone goes to the wrestling school, gets his body into good shape, becomes a boxer, and then strikes his father and mother, or some other relative or friend, that's no reason to hate the {{{ 456e }}} trainers and the men who teach fighting in arms, and drive them out of their cities. Those men taught their skill so that it would be used justly, against enemies and wrongdoers, in self-defence, not as the aggressor; but the others turn it round and use their strength and their {{{ 457a }}} craft wrongly. So the teachers aren't wicked, and the craft is neither to blame nor wicked on that account — the ones not using it rightly are, I think. The same argument holds for rhetoric too. The orator is capable of speaking against everyone and about everything, so as to be more persuasive before a crowd, {{{ 457b }}} on virtually any subject he likes; but that's no more reason to strip the doctors of their reputation — just because he could do this — or the other craftsmen either; one ought to use rhetoric justly too, just like any competitive skill. And if, I suppose, someone becomes an orator and then uses this power and this craft to do wrong, that's no reason to hate his teacher and drive him out of the cities. For the teacher handed it over for {{{ 457c }}} just use, and the other man uses it the opposite way. So it's the one who misuses it who ought rightly to be hated, and driven out, and put to death — not the one who taught him.

@ Socrates

I think you too, Gorgias, are experienced in many discussions, and have noticed something about them: that the parties rarely manage to define their terms with each other, learn from each other, and teach each other, and so {{{ 457d }}} bring their meetings to a close that way; instead, if they disagree about something and one says the other isn't speaking correctly or clearly, they get annoyed, and think the other is speaking out of spite against them, competing to win rather than searching out the matter under discussion; and some of them end up parting in the most shameful way, having abused each other and said and heard such things about themselves that even the bystanders are distressed on their behalf, for having thought such people worth {{{ 457e }}} listening to. Why, then, am I saying this? Because right now you seem to me to be saying things that don't quite follow or agree with what you said at first about rhetoric; so I'm reluctant to press the argument against you, in case you take me to be speaking out of a wish to win against you personally, rather than to get the matter clear. {{{ 458a }}} Now, if you're the kind of man I am, I'd be glad to go on questioning you; if not, I'd let it drop. And what kind am I? The kind who would be glad to be refuted if I say something untrue, and glad to refute someone else if he says something untrue — no less glad, in fact, to be refuted than to refute; since I count being refuted the greater good, by as much as it's a greater good to be rid of the worst evil oneself than to rid someone else of it. For I don't think there's any evil for a man as great as a false {{{ 458b }}} opinion about the very things our discussion now happens to concern. So if you say you're that kind of man too, let's go on talking; but if you think we should drop it, let's say goodbye to it now and break off the discussion.

@ Gorgias

Well, I say that I myself am just the kind of man you describe, Socrates — though perhaps we ought to consider those present too. Even before you two arrived, I'd already given a long display to those here, and now we'll probably run on {{{ 458c }}} a good while, if we keep talking. So we ought to consider them as well, in case we're keeping some of them from something else they'd rather be doing.

@ Chaerephon

You can hear the commotion for yourselves, Gorgias and Socrates — these men want to hear whatever you say; and as for me, I hope I never get so busy that I'd give up a discussion like this, conducted this well, for something else that mattered more to do.

@ Callicles

{{{ 458d }}} By the gods, Chaerephon, I've been present at a great many discussions myself, and I don't know that I've ever enjoyed one as much as this; so as far as I'm concerned, you'll be doing me a favour even if you want to talk the whole day through.

@ Socrates

Well then, Callicles, nothing stands in my way, as far as I'm concerned, provided Gorgias is willing.

@ Gorgias

It would be shameful indeed, Socrates, for me now to refuse, after announcing myself that anyone could ask whatever he wanted. {{{ 458e }}} So if it suits these gentlemen, go ahead and talk, and ask whatever you want.

@ Socrates

Listen, then, Gorgias, to what puzzles me in what you've been saying; it may well be that you're speaking correctly and I'm the one misunderstanding. You say you're able to make someone into an orator, if he wants to learn from you?

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

So as to be persuasive before a crowd on every subject — not by teaching them, but by {{{ 459a }}} persuading them?

@ Gorgias

Exactly so.

@ Socrates

You were saying just now that the orator will even be more persuasive than the doctor on matters of health.

@ Gorgias

Yes, I did say that — before a crowd, that is.

@ Socrates

And doesn't that mean before people who don't know? Since surely he won't be more persuasive than the doctor among those who do know.

@ Gorgias

True.

@ Socrates

So if he's going to be more persuasive than the doctor, he becomes more persuasive than the one who knows?

@ Gorgias

Quite {{{ 459b }}} so.

@ Socrates

Without being a doctor himself — isn't that right?

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

And the one who isn't a doctor is presumably without the knowledge the doctor has.

@ Gorgias

Clearly.

@ Socrates

So the one who doesn't know will be more persuasive than the one who does, among people who don't know, whenever the orator is more persuasive than the doctor. Does that follow, or something else?

@ Gorgias

That's what follows, in this case.

@ Socrates

So the orator and rhetoric stand the same way toward all the other crafts too: rhetoric itself has no need to know how things actually stand, only to have discovered some {{{ 459c }}} device of persuasion so as to appear, to those who don't know, to know more than those who do.

@ Gorgias

So isn't it a great convenience, Socrates, not having to learn all the other crafts, only this one, and yet coming off no worse than the actual craftsmen?

@ Socrates

Whether the orator comes off worse than the others, or not, because of standing this way, we'll examine in a moment, if it's relevant to us; but first let's look at this: whether the rhetorical man is in fact related to {{{ 459d }}} the just and the unjust, the shameful and the fine, the good and the bad, the same way he's related to health and the other subjects of the other crafts — not knowing himself what's good or bad, or fine or shameful, or just or unjust, but having contrived some persuasion about them, so as to seem, among people who don't know, to know more {{{ 459e }}} than the one who actually knows? Or is it necessary to know, and must someone who's going to learn rhetoric from you already know these things beforehand? And if not, will you, the teacher of rhetoric, teach the one who comes to you none of this — since it isn't your business — but make him seem, before the crowd, to know these things when he doesn't, and seem to be good when he isn't? Or will you be altogether unable to teach him rhetoric at all, unless he already knows the truth about these matters beforehand? How do things stand here, Gorgias? {{{ 460a }}} And for god's sake, as you promised a moment ago, uncover it and tell me what the power of rhetoric actually is.

@ Gorgias

Well, Socrates, I suppose that if he happens not to know these things, he'll learn them from me too.

@ Socrates

Hold on there — that's well said. If you're going to make someone into an orator, then, he must know the just and the unjust — either beforehand, or having learned it from you afterward.

@ Gorgias

{{{ 460b }}} Quite so.

@ Socrates

Well, then? Is the one who's learned carpentry a carpenter, or not?

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

And the one who's learned music, musical?

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

And the one who's learned medicine, medical? And so with everything else, by the same reasoning — whoever has learned each thing becomes the sort of person that knowledge of it makes him?

@ Gorgias

Quite so.

@ Socrates

So by that same reasoning, isn't the one who's learned justice, just?

@ Gorgias

Absolutely, surely.

@ Socrates

And the just man does just things, presumably.

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

{{{ 460c }}} So must not the orator be just, and the just man want to do just things?

@ Gorgias

So it appears.

@ Socrates

So the just man will never want to do wrong.

@ Gorgias

He must not.

@ Socrates

And by our argument, the orator must be just.

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

So the orator will never want to do wrong.

@ Gorgias

So it appears not.

@ Socrates

Do you remember, then, saying a little earlier that we shouldn't blame the {{{ 460d }}} trainers, or drive them out of their cities, if a boxer uses his boxing unjustly and does wrong with it — and that, in just the same way, if an orator uses rhetoric unjustly, we shouldn't blame his teacher or expel him from the city, but rather the wrongdoer, the one misusing rhetoric? Was that said, or not?

@ Gorgias

It was said.

@ Socrates

But now {{{ 460e }}} this same man, the orator, turns out never to do wrong at all. Or not?

@ Gorgias

So it appears.

@ Socrates

And right at the start, Gorgias, it was said that rhetoric dealt not with arguments about odd and even, but with arguments about the just and the unjust — wasn't that so?

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

So when you said that at the time, I took it that rhetoric could never be an unjust thing, since it always deals in arguments about justice; but then, a little later, when you said that the orator {{{ 461a }}} could use rhetoric unjustly too, I was so struck by this, and thought what you were saying didn't hang together, that I said what I said — that if you counted being refuted a gain, as I do, it would be worth continuing the discussion, and if not, we should let it go. And now that we've examined it further, you see for yourself that it's agreed all over again that the orator is unable to use rhetoric unjustly, or to be willing to do wrong. So how all this actually stands, by the {{{ 461b }}} dog, Gorgias, is not a small matter to work out properly — it will take more than one sitting.
