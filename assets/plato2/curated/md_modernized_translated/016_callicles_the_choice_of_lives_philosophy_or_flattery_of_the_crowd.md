## The Choice of Lives: Philosophy or Flattery of the Crowd

@ Callicles

{{{ 497c }}} Go on and ask your petty little questions, then, since that's how Gorgias wants it.

@ Socrates

You're a happy man, Callicles, to have been initiated into the great mysteries before the small ones — I didn't think that was allowed. So answer from where you left off: doesn't each of us stop being thirsty and feeling pleasure at the same time?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

And doesn't he stop being hungry, and stop with the other appetites and pleasures, at the same time as well?

@ Callicles

That's so.

@ Socrates

And doesn't he stop feeling the pains {{{ 497d }}} and the pleasures at the same time too?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

But you're not stopping with the good and bad things at the same time, as you agreed — or do you not agree with that now?

@ Callicles

I do — so what of it?

@ Socrates

It's because the good and the pleasant don't turn out to be the same thing, my friend, and neither do the bad and the painful. Because he stops with some of these at the same time, but not with others, since they're different — so how could the pleasant be the same as the good, or the painful the same as the bad? But if you like, look at it this way too — I don't think you'll agree with me on this one either {{{ 497e }}}; think it over — you call good men good by the presence of good things in them, don't you, just as you call fine men fine when fineness is present in them?

@ Callicles

I do.

@ Socrates

What about this? Do you call foolish and cowardly men good? No, not just now — you spoke of the courageous and intelligent ones; or don't you call these men good?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

What about this? Have you ever seen a mindless child feeling pleasure?

@ Callicles

I have.

@ Socrates

And have you never seen a mindless man feeling pleasure?

@ Callicles

I think I have — but what of it?

@ Socrates

{{{ 498a }}} Nothing — just answer.

@ Callicles

I've seen it.

@ Socrates

What about this? A sensible man, feeling pain and feeling pleasure?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

Which of the two feel pleasure and pain more, the intelligent or the foolish?

@ Callicles

I think there isn't much difference.

@ Socrates

Well, even that's enough. Have you ever seen a cowardly man in war?

@ Callicles

Of course.

@ Socrates

Well then? When the enemy retreats, which men seemed to you to feel more pleasure, the cowards or the brave?

@ Callicles

Both, I'd say, {{{ 498b }}} [more so] — or if not, at least about equally.

@ Socrates

It makes no difference. So the cowards feel pleasure too?

@ Callicles

Very much so.

@ Socrates

And the foolish too, it seems.

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

But when the enemy advances, do only the cowards feel pain, or the brave as well?

@ Callicles

Both.

@ Socrates

Equally?

@ Callicles

The cowards more, perhaps.

@ Socrates

And when the enemy retreats, don't they feel more pleasure?

@ Callicles

Perhaps.

@ Socrates

So both the foolish and the intelligent, both the cowardly and the brave, feel pain and pleasure about equally, {{{ 498c }}} as you say — only the cowards more than the brave?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

But surely the intelligent and the brave are good, and the cowardly and foolish are bad?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

So the good and the bad feel pleasure and pain about equally?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

So are the good and the bad about equally good and bad? Or are the bad even more good?

@ Callicles

{{{ 498d }}} By Zeus, I don't know what you're saying.

@ Socrates

Don't you know you're saying that good men are good by the presence of good things, and bad men bad by the presence of bad things? And that the good things are the pleasures, and the bad things the pains?

@ Callicles

I do.

@ Socrates

So the good things, the pleasures, are present in those who feel pleasure, since they're feeling pleasure?

@ Callicles

Of course.

@ Socrates

So, with good things present, those who feel pleasure are good?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

What about this? Aren't the bad things, the pains, present in those who feel pain?

@ Callicles

They're present.

@ Socrates

{{{ 498e }}} And you say the bad are bad by the presence of bad things — or do you no longer say that?

@ Callicles

I do.

@ Socrates

So those who feel pleasure are good, and those who feel pain are bad?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

The more so, the more good or bad; the less so, the less; and those about equally, about equally good or bad?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

So you're saying the intelligent and the foolish, and the cowardly and the brave, feel pleasure and pain about equally — or even the cowards a bit more?

@ Callicles

I do.

@ Socrates

Now reason it out together with me, what follows for us from what we've agreed — since they say it's fine to state and examine fine things {{{ 499a }}} even twice and three times over. We say the intelligent and brave man is good. Isn't that so?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

And the foolish and cowardly man is bad?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

And again, the one feeling pleasure is good?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

And the one feeling pain is bad?

@ Callicles

That must be so.

@ Socrates

And the good man and the bad man feel pain and pleasure alike — perhaps even the bad man more?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

So doesn't the bad man turn out just as good as the good man — or even more good {{{ 499b }}} than him? Isn't this exactly what followed before too, if someone says the pleasant and the good are the same thing? Isn't this necessary, Callicles?

@ Callicles

I've been listening to you agree along for a while now, Socrates, thinking to myself that whenever someone concedes you anything, even in play, you grab onto it gladly, like the boys do. As if you really think I, or anyone else alive, don't believe some pleasures are better and others worse.

@ Socrates

Well, well, Callicles, what a schemer you are — you're treating me {{{ 499c }}} like a child, first claiming things are one way, then another, deceiving me. And yet I didn't think at the start I'd be willingly deceived by you, since I took you for a friend — but now I've been proven wrong, and it seems, as the old saying goes, I must make the best of what's here and accept this gift you're now offering. So what you're now saying, it seems, is that some pleasures are good and others bad — isn't that so?

@ Callicles

{{{ 499d }}} Yes.

@ Socrates

So the beneficial ones are good, and the harmful ones bad?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

And the beneficial ones are the ones that produce something good, and the harmful ones produce something bad?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

Do you mean something like this: take the bodily pleasures we were just talking about, in eating and drinking — of these, whichever produce health in the body, or strength, or some other excellence of the body, those are good, and their {{{ 499e }}} opposites bad?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

And likewise with pains, some are good and some are bad?

@ Callicles

Of course.

@ Socrates

So the good pleasures and pains are the ones to be chosen and acted on?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

But not the bad ones?

@ Callicles

Clearly not.

@ Socrates

Because, if you remember, it seemed to Polus and me that everything must be done for the sake of the good. Do you agree with this too, that the good is the goal of all our actions, and that everything else must be done for its sake, {{{ 500a }}} and not it for the sake of the other things? Will you make it unanimous with us, as the third vote?

@ Callicles

I will.

@ Socrates

So it's for the sake of good things that we must do the other things, including the pleasant ones, and not the good things for the sake of the pleasant ones.

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

So is it within any man's power to pick out which of the pleasant things are good and which are bad, or is a skilled person needed for each case?

@ Callicles

A skilled person.

@ Socrates

Let's remember what I happened to be saying to Polus and Gorgias. I was saying, if you recall, {{{ 500b }}} that there are some preparations that go only as far as pleasure, providing just that and nothing more, ignorant of what's better and what's worse, and others that know what's good and what's bad; and I put cookery among those concerned with pleasure — a knack, not a craft — and medicine among those concerned with the good, a craft. And by Philia's god, Callicles, don't you go thinking you need to joke around with me, answering whatever comes to mind against what you actually believe, and don't {{{ 500c }}} take what I say as a joke either — because you see that our discussion concerns the very thing a person with even a little sense would take most seriously: how one ought to live — whether following the way you're urging me toward, doing the things a real man does, speaking before the assembly and practicing rhetoric and engaging in politics the way you people do now, or living this life spent in philosophy, and what it is that makes this one different from that one. So perhaps it's best, {{{ 500d }}} as I just tried to do, to make a distinction, and once we've distinguished the two and agreed with each other whether there really are these two different lives, to examine how they differ from each other and which of the two is worth living. Perhaps you don't yet know what I mean.

@ Callicles

No, I don't.

@ Socrates

But I'll tell you more clearly. Since you and I have agreed that there's such a thing as the good, and such a thing as the pleasant, and that the pleasant is different from the good, and that each of the two has its own practice and pursuit for acquiring it, one the hunt for the pleasant, the other the hunt for the good — this very point, first, either agree with me on it {{{ 500e }}} or not. Do you agree?

@ Callicles

Yes, that's what I say.

@ Socrates

Come then, agree with me on the things I was saying earlier to Polus and Gorgias — whether I seemed to you to be speaking the truth then. I was saying, I believe, that cookery doesn't seem to me to be a craft but a knack, while medicine {{{ 501a }}} is a craft — because medicine has examined the nature of what it treats and the cause of what it does, and can give an account of each of these; while the other one, aiming entirely at pleasure, which is the whole of its business, goes at it in a completely unskilled way, having examined neither the nature of pleasure nor its cause, having reckoned nothing at all, so to speak, in any rational way, preserving through mere routine and knack only a memory {{{ 501b }}} of what usually happens, which is also how it supplies its pleasures. So first consider whether this seems to you to be said well enough, and whether there are, in the case of the soul too, other such pursuits — some of them skilled, taking some forethought for what's best for the soul, and others that neglect this, and instead, just as in the body's case, have looked only at the soul's pleasure, at how it might come about, without examining at all which pleasures are better and which worse, caring about nothing except {{{ 501c }}} gratifying it, whether for better or worse. Because to me, Callicles, it seems there are such pursuits, and I say that this sort of thing is flattery, whether it concerns the body, or the soul, or anything else — whenever someone attends to pleasure with no thought at all for what's better and what's worse; but you — do you agree with us on this, holding the same opinion, or do you disagree?

@ Callicles

I don't disagree — I concede it, so your argument can reach its conclusion, and so I can do Gorgias here a favor.
