## The True Political Craft, and a Trial Foreseen

@ Socrates

{{{ 519e }}} So it seems — at any rate, I'm stretching out long speeches now, since you refuse to answer. But, my good man, tell me, in Zeus's name, god of friendship — don't you think it's unreasonable for someone who claims to have made a person good to then blame that same person, made good and being good through him, for afterward turning out wicked?

@ Callicles

I think so.

@ Socrates

Now don't you hear men who claim to educate people in virtue saying things like that?

@ Callicles

{{{ 520a }}} I do — but why would you talk about men who aren't worth anything?

@ Socrates

Then why would you talk about those men who claim to lead the city and take care that it becomes as good as possible, and then turn around and accuse it, whenever it suits them, of being utterly wicked? Do you think these men are any different from the others? The sophist and the orator, my dear fellow, are the same thing, or nearly the same and closely related, as I was telling Polus — but out of ignorance you think one of them, rhetoric, {{{ 520b }}} is something altogether splendid, while you look down on the other. But in truth sophistry is as much finer than rhetoric as legislation is finer than the administration of justice, and physical training finer than medicine — and I, for my part, thought it was only public speakers and sophists who had no room to blame the very thing they teach as bad for its practitioners, or else, in that same breath, to accuse themselves of never having benefited the very people they claim to benefit. Isn't that how it is?

@ Callicles

{{{ 520c }}} Certainly.

@ Socrates

And surely, then, giving that benefit for free, as one would expect, was possible only for these men, if they were telling the truth. For with any other benefit, someone who's received it — say, made swift by a trainer — might perhaps cheat him of his due gratitude, if the trainer let him go without first striking a bargain for pay and taking the {{{ 520d }}} money at the very moment he passed on the speed — since it's not slowness, I imagine, that makes men do wrong, but injustice — isn't that so?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

So if someone removes this very thing, injustice itself, there's no danger of his ever being wronged, but for him alone it's safe to give away this particular benefit for free, if someone really could make people good. Isn't that so?

@ Callicles

I agree.

@ Socrates

That's why, it seems, there's nothing shameful in taking money for giving other kinds of advice — about building houses, say, or the other crafts.

@ Callicles

{{{ 520e }}} So it seems.

@ Socrates

But when it comes to this particular business — by what means someone might become as good as possible and best manage his own household or city — it's considered shameful to refuse to give advice unless someone pays for it. Isn't that so?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

Clearly the reason is that this, alone among benefits, makes the one who has received it want to do good in return — so it seems a fine sign, if a man who has done this particular good is treated well in return; and if not, not. Is that how things stand?

@ Callicles

{{{ 521a }}} It is.

@ Socrates

So which kind of care for the city are you calling me to — define it for me: the kind that fights hard with the Athenians so that they'll become as good as possible, like a doctor, or the kind that serves them and speaks to please them? Tell me the truth, Callicles — it's only fair, since you began speaking frankly with me, that you keep on saying what you actually think. So speak up now, clearly and nobly.

@ Callicles

Well then, I say the kind that serves them.

@ Socrates

{{{ 521b }}} So you're calling me, noblest of men, to a life of flattering them.

@ Callicles

If you'd rather call it playing the Mysian, Socrates — because if you won't do that—

@ Socrates

Don't say what you've said so often — that anyone who wants to can kill me — so that I don't in turn say, a wicked man killing a good one; nor that he'll take my property if I have any, so that I don't in turn say, but having taken it he won't know what to do with it, but just as he took it from me unjustly, so too {{{ 521c }}} he'll use it unjustly once he has it, and if unjustly, shamefully, and if shamefully, badly.

@ Callicles

You really do seem to me, Socrates, to be confident that none of this could happen to you — as if you lived out of harm's way and could never be hauled into court by some thoroughly rotten and worthless man.

@ Socrates

Then I really am a fool, Callicles, if I don't think that in this city anyone at all, whoever it happens to be, could suffer that. But this much I know well — that if I do go into court facing danger over {{{ 521d }}} one of the things you're talking about, the man bringing me in will be some scoundrel, since no decent man would bring in someone who's done no wrong — and it wouldn't be at all strange if I died for it. Do you want me to tell you why I expect this?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

I think that I, along with a few Athenians — not to say alone — am attempting the true political craft, and that I'm the only man alive today practicing politics; so since the speeches I make each time aren't spoken to please, but aim at the best, not at {{{ 521e }}} the most pleasant, and since I'm unwilling to do those clever tricks you recommend, I'll have nothing to say for myself in court. The same argument comes back to me that I made to Polus — I'll be judged the way a doctor would be judged before a jury of children, prosecuted by a cook. Just think what a man like that could say in his own defense, caught in that position, if someone brought a charge against him, saying: children, this man here has done a great deal of harm to you, to you yourselves — he ruins your youngest with cutting and burning, {{{ 522a }}} and by starving and choking you leaves you at a loss, giving you the bitterest draughts and forcing hunger and thirst on you, not like me, who feasted you on all sorts of good things — what do you think a doctor, caught in that bind, could find to say? Or if he told the truth — that he did all of this for your health, children — how loud a cry do you think jurors like that would raise? Wouldn't it be loud?

@ Callicles

Perhaps — one should assume so, at least.

@ Socrates

So don't you think he'd be at a complete loss for what {{{ 522b }}} he ought to say?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

And that, I know well, is just the kind of thing I would suffer if I went into court. For I won't be able to point to any pleasures I've supplied them with, the kind they count as benefits and services, and I envy neither the men who supply them nor those they're supplied to; and if someone claims I corrupt the young by putting them at a loss, or that I slander my elders with bitter words in private or in public, I'll have no way to say the true thing — that I say all of this justly, {{{ 522c }}} and that I'm acting, gentlemen of the jury, in your own interest — nor anything else at all; so whatever happens to come, that, I suppose, is what I'll suffer.

@ Callicles

So does it seem to you, Socrates, to be a fine thing, for a man in a city to be in such a state, and unable to help himself?

@ Socrates

Yes — provided he has that one thing, Callicles, which you've agreed to many times: provided he has already helped himself, by having neither said nor done anything unjust, {{{ 522d }}} whether toward men or toward gods. For this kind of help to oneself has often been agreed between us to be the strongest kind there is. Now if someone were to prove me unable to give this kind of help, to myself or to anyone else, I'd be ashamed, whether refuted before a crowd or a few, or alone by one man alone, and if I died because of that incapacity, I'd be indignant — but if I should die for lack of the flattering kind of rhetoric, I know well you would see me bear {{{ 522e }}} my death easily. For dying itself is feared by no one, unless he's utterly unreasoning and unmanly — what's feared is doing wrong, since arriving in Hades with a soul crammed full of wrongdoing is the worst of all evils. And if you like, I'm willing to tell you a story showing that this is so.

@ Callicles

Well, since you've finished everything else, finish this too.
