## The Many Prove Collectively Stronger Than the One

@ Socrates

If I happened to have a golden soul, Callicles, don't you think I'd be glad to find one of those touchstones people test gold with — the very best one — so that I could bring my soul up against it, and if it agreed that my soul had been properly cared for, I'd know for certain that I was in good shape and needed no other test?

@ Callicles

{{{ 486e }}} Why are you even asking that, Socrates?

@ Socrates

I'll tell you — I think that in running into you just now I've run into exactly that kind of stroke of luck.

@ Callicles

How so?

@ Socrates

I know perfectly well that if you agree with me about the things my soul holds an opinion on, those things are already the truth itself. Because I realize {{{ 487a }}} that anyone who's going to test properly whether a soul is living rightly or not needs three things, all of which you happen to have: knowledge, goodwill, and frankness. I run into plenty of people who aren't able to test me because they're not wise the way you are; others are wise, but they're unwilling to tell me the truth because they don't care about me the way you do; but these two strangers here, Gorgias and Polus, are wise and {{{ 487b }}} they're my friends, but they're rather short on frankness and more prone to embarrassment than they ought to be — how could they not be? Both of them have reached such a pitch of embarrassment that, out of sheer embarrassment, each of them dares to contradict himself in front of a great many people, and about the most important matters at that. But you have all these things that the others lack — you've been well enough educated, as plenty of Athenians would say, and you're well disposed toward me. {{{ 487c }}} What evidence am I using? I'll tell you. I know that the four of you, Callicles, have become partners in wisdom — you, Teisander of Aphidnae, Andron son of Androtion, and Nausicydes of Cholarges; and I once overheard you deliberating about how far one ought to pursue wisdom, and I know that the view that won out among you was not to be eager to philosophize down to the last precision, and you {{{ 487d }}} urged one another to be careful not to become wiser than you needed to be and ruin yourselves without noticing it. So when I hear you giving me the same advice you gave your closest companions, that's sufficient evidence to me that you're truly well disposed toward me. And as for being the kind of man who speaks frankly and isn't embarrassed, you say so yourself, and the speech you gave a little while ago backs you up on it. So this is clearly how things stand right now regarding these matters — {{{ 487e }}} if you agree with me on something in the argument, it will already have been sufficiently tested by both of us, and there will be no need to bring it before any further test. Because you'd never concede it either from lack of wisdom or from excess of embarrassment, and you wouldn't concede it to deceive me either — you're my friend, as you say yourself. So in truth, any agreement between you and me will already have reached the very end of the truth. And the finest inquiry of all, Callicles, concerns exactly what you criticized me over — what kind of man one ought to be, and what {{{ 488a }}} one ought to pursue, and up to what point, both as an older man and as a younger one. As for me, if I'm doing something wrong in my own life, you should know well that I'm not erring on purpose, but out of my own ignorance; so, since you've already begun to admonish me, don't stop now — show me clearly what it is I ought to be pursuing, and how I might acquire it, and if you ever catch me agreeing with you now, and then, later on, not doing the same things I agreed to, consider me a complete fool and {{{ 488b }}} never bother admonishing me again afterward, as being worth nothing at all. So tell me again, from the beginning — how do you and Pindar say the just thing stands, according to nature? That the stronger should take by force what belongs to the weaker, the better man should rule the worse, and the superior man should have more than the inferior one? Is that anything different from what you say justice is, or am I remembering correctly?

@ Callicles

Well, that's what I was saying then, and that's what I'm saying now.

@ Socrates

Do you call the same man better and stronger? {{{ 488c }}} Because even then I wasn't able to figure out what exactly you meant. Do you call the stronger men the better ones, and say the weaker must obey the stronger — which is what you seemed to me to be showing earlier too, when you said that big cities attack small ones according to what's just by nature, because they're stronger and more powerful, on the assumption that the stronger, the more powerful, and the better are the same thing — or can someone be better while also weaker and less powerful, and stronger while also more corrupt, or is the {{{ 488d }}} definition of the better and of the stronger one and the same? Just make this itself clear to me — are the stronger, the better, and the more powerful the same thing, or different?

@ Callicles

I'm telling you clearly — they're the same thing.

@ Socrates

So then the many are stronger than the one by nature? And it's the many who make the laws against the one, just as you were saying a moment ago.

@ Callicles

How could they not be?

@ Socrates

So the conventions of the many are the conventions of the stronger.

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

{{{ 488e }}} And so the conventions of the better men too? Because the stronger are far better, according to your account.

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

So then their conventions are fine by nature, since they belong to the stronger?

@ Callicles

I say so.

@ Socrates

So then, do the many believe this — just as you were saying a moment ago — that it's just to have an equal share, and more shameful to do wrong {{{ 489a }}} than to suffer it? Is that so, or not? And mind you don't get caught out being embarrassed here yourself. Do the many believe, or not, that it's just to have an equal share rather than more, and that doing wrong is more shameful than suffering it? Don't grudge me an answer to this, Callicles, so that if you agree with me, I can already treat it as settled, coming as it does from a man competent to judge.

@ Callicles

Well, the many do believe that.

@ Socrates

So it's not only by convention that doing wrong is more shameful than {{{ 489b }}} suffering it, and that having an equal share is just — it's by nature too; which means you run the risk of not having spoken the truth earlier, and of accusing me wrongly when you said that convention and nature are opposed, something I supposedly know and exploit to cheat in argument, dragging anyone who speaks by nature over to convention, and anyone who speaks by convention over to nature.

@ Callicles

This man will never stop talking nonsense. Tell me, Socrates — aren't you embarrassed, at your age, to go hunting for words, and {{{ 489c }}} to treat it as a windfall whenever someone makes a slip of the tongue? Do you really think I mean anything different by "the stronger" than "the better"? Haven't I been telling you for a while now that I say the better and the stronger are the same thing? Or do you think I mean that if a mob of slaves and every sort of worthless person gets together — worth nothing except perhaps for physical strength — and they say something, that's automatically a valid convention?

@ Socrates

All right, wisest of men, Callicles — is that what you're saying?

@ Callicles

That's exactly it.

@ Socrates

{{{ 489d }}} Well, my good man, I myself have suspected for a while now that this is roughly what you mean by "the stronger," and I've been asking because I'm eager to know clearly what you mean. Because surely you don't think two men are better than one, or that your own slaves are better than you, just because they're physically stronger than you. So tell me again, from the beginning, who you mean by "the better," since it's not the stronger? And, my extraordinary friend, teach me a bit more gently, so I don't run off and leave your school.

@ Callicles

{{{ 489e }}} You're being sarcastic, Socrates.

@ Socrates

By Zethus, Callicles — the very character you used a moment ago to poke fun at me — no, come on, tell me: who do you mean by "the better"?

@ Callicles

The superior men, that's who.

@ Socrates

Do you see, then, that you're just uttering words yourself and explaining nothing? Won't you tell me whether by "the better and the stronger" you mean the more intelligent, or some other people?

@ Callicles

Yes, by god, that's exactly who I mean, and emphatically so.

@ Socrates

{{{ 490a }}} So then, on your account, one intelligent man is often stronger than ten thousand unintelligent ones, and he ought to rule while they're ruled, and the ruler ought to have more than the ruled; this seems to me to be what you mean to say — and I'm not hunting for a slip of the tongue — if the one man is stronger than the ten thousand.

@ Callicles

Yes, that's exactly what I mean. Because I think this is what's just by nature — that the man who is better and more intelligent should both rule and have more than his inferiors.

@ Socrates

{{{ 490b }}} Hold on right there. What exactly are you saying now? If we were all together in one place, as we are now, a whole crowd of us, and we had plenty of food and drink in common, and we were all sorts — some strong, some weak — and one of us, being a doctor, were more knowledgeable than the rest about these things, and were, as is likely, stronger than some of us and weaker than others, wouldn't this man, being more intelligent than we are, turn out to be better and stronger than us in this area?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

{{{ 490c }}} So should he then have a greater share of this food than the rest of us, because he's better — or should the distribution all be up to him as ruler, while when it comes to using it up and consuming it for his own body he shouldn't get more than his share, unless he means to suffer for it, but should get more of some things and less of others — and if he happens to be the weakest of all, should the best man get the least of all, Callicles? Isn't that how it is, my good man?

@ Callicles

You're talking about food and drink and doctors and nonsense; {{{ 490d }}} that's not what I mean at all.

@ Socrates

Don't you mean the more intelligent man by "the better"? Say yes or no.

@ Callicles

I do.

@ Socrates

And shouldn't the better man have more?

@ Callicles

Not of food or drink, no.

@ Socrates

I see — clothes then, perhaps, and the best weaver ought to have the biggest cloak and go around wearing the most and the finest clothes?

@ Callicles

What clothes are you even talking about?

@ Socrates

All right, shoes then — clearly the man who is most intelligent {{{ 490e }}} and best at this ought to get more than his share. The shoemaker, perhaps, ought to walk around wearing the biggest and the most shoes.

@ Callicles

What shoes? You won't stop talking nonsense.

@ Socrates

Well, if you don't mean things like that, maybe you mean something like this — say, a man who's skilled, fine, and good when it comes to farming; perhaps he ought to get more seed than his share, and use as much seed as possible on his own land.

@ Callicles

You keep saying the same things over and over, Socrates.

@ Socrates

Not only that, Callicles — I also keep saying them about the same things.

@ Callicles

{{{ 491a }}} By the gods, you literally never stop talking about shoemakers and fullers and cooks and doctors, as if our discussion were about them.

@ Socrates

Then won't you tell me in what area the stronger and more intelligent man justly gets more than his share by having more? Or will you neither put up with my suggestions nor tell me yourself?

@ Callicles

I've been telling you for a while now. First of all, by "the stronger" I don't mean shoemakers or cooks, but {{{ 491b }}} the men who are intelligent about the business of the city, about how it should best be run — and not only intelligent, but also courageous, capable of carrying out whatever they've thought through, and not the sort to give up out of softness of soul.

@ Socrates

Do you see, my excellent Callicles, that your accusation against me and my accusation against you aren't the same? You say I always say the same things, and you find fault with me for it; but I find fault with you for the opposite reason — that you never say the same thing twice about the same subject, but first you defined the better and the stronger as {{{ 491c }}} the physically stronger, then as the more intelligent, and now you've come around with something else again — now you say the braver men are the stronger and the better. So, my good man, just tell me plainly, once and for all, who exactly you mean by the better and the stronger, and in what respect, and be done with it.

@ Callicles

I already told you — the men who are intelligent about the business of the city, and courageous. Because it's fitting for these men {{{ 491d }}} to rule the cities, and this is what justice is — for these men, the rulers, to have more than the others, the ruled.

@ Socrates

And what about this? What about themselves, my friend? Are they rulers of themselves, or ruled?

@ Callicles

What do you mean?

@ Socrates

I mean each man ruling himself; or is there no need at all for a man to rule himself, only to rule others?

@ Callicles

What do you mean by ruling himself?

@ Socrates

Nothing complicated — just what most people mean: being moderate and in control of oneself, ruling {{{ 491e }}} over the pleasures and desires within oneself.
