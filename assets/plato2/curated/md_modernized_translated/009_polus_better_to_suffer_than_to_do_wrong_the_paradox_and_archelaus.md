## Better to Suffer Than to Do Wrong: The Paradox and Archelaus

@ Socrates

{{{ 469c }}} For my part, I'd want neither — but if it were necessary to do wrong or to suffer wrong, I'd choose to suffer wrong rather than do it.

@ Polus

Then you wouldn't accept being a tyrant?

@ Socrates

No — not if by being a tyrant you mean what I mean.

@ Polus

Well, I mean exactly what I meant a moment ago — being free in the city to do whatever seems good to you, killing and banishing and doing everything according to your own judgment.

@ Socrates

My blessed friend, catch me up on the argument as I make it. {{{ 469d }}} For if I, in the crowded marketplace, taking a dagger under my arm, were to say to you, Polus — I've just now acquired a certain wonderful power, even a tyranny — since if it seems to me that one of these men you see here needs to die right away, this man will be dead, whoever I decide on; and if it seems to me that one of them needs his head broken, his head will be broken right away; and if I decide his cloak should be torn, it will be torn — that's how {{{ 469e }}} much power I have in this city — well, if I showed you the dagger when you doubted me, you'd probably say, on seeing it, Socrates, in that way everyone would have great power, since a house could be burned down that way, whichever one you liked, and the Athenians' dockyards too, and their triremes, and all their ships, public and private alike — but that isn't what having great power means, doing whatever seems good to you — or do you think it is?

@ Polus

No, certainly not that way.

@ Socrates

{{{ 470a }}} Can you say, then, why you criticize that kind of power?

@ Polus

I can.

@ Socrates

Why is that? Tell me.

@ Polus

Because a man who acts that way is bound to be punished.

@ Socrates

And being punished isn't a bad thing?

@ Polus

Certainly it is.

@ Socrates

So it turns out, my amazing friend, that [having great power] is a good thing after all, in your view, only if doing what seems best to him is also followed by acting beneficially — and that, it seems, is what having great power really is; if not, it's a bad thing, and no power at all. {{{ 470b }}} And let's consider this too — don't we agree that sometimes it's better to do the things we were just talking about, killing people and driving them out and confiscating property, but sometimes not?

@ Polus

Certainly.

@ Socrates

This much, then, it seems, is agreed by both of us.

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

So when do you say it's better to do these things? Tell me, what boundary do you draw?

@ Polus

No, you answer that [same] question yourself, Socrates.

@ Socrates

{{{ 470c }}} Well, I say, Polus, if you'd rather hear it from me, that when someone does these things justly, it's better, and when unjustly, worse.

@ Polus

You're a hard man to refute, Socrates — but couldn't even a child refute you and show that you're not telling the truth?

@ Socrates

Then I'll be very grateful to the child, and just as grateful to you, if you refute me and free me from talking nonsense. But don't grow tired of doing a friend a favor — go on and refute me.

@ Polus

Well, Socrates, there's certainly no need for me to refute you with ancient {{{ 470d }}} history — recent events, things that happened just yesterday, are enough to refute you and show that many people who do wrong are happy.

@ Socrates

What events do you mean?

@ Polus

You do see this Archelaus, son of Perdiccas, ruling Macedonia?

@ Socrates

If not seeing him, at least I hear about him.

@ Polus

Does he seem to you happy, or wretched?

@ Socrates

I don't know, Polus — I've never met the man.

@ Polus

{{{ 470e }}} What? You'd know if you met him, but otherwise, on the spot, you can't tell that he's happy?

@ Socrates

No, by Zeus, certainly not.

@ Polus

Clearly then, Socrates, you'll say you don't even know whether the Great King is happy.

@ Socrates

And I'll be telling the truth — since I don't know how he stands with respect to education and justice.

@ Polus

What? Does all happiness depend on that?

@ Socrates

That's what I say, Polus — I say that a fine and good man or woman is happy, and an unjust and wicked one is wretched.

@ Polus

{{{ 471a }}} So by your account, this Archelaus is wretched?

@ Socrates

Yes, my friend — if he's unjust.

@ Polus

But how is he not unjust? He had no right at all to the rule he now holds, being born of a woman who was a slave to Alcetas, Perdiccas's brother — by justice he was Alcetas's slave, and if he'd wanted to do what's just, he'd still be serving Alcetas, and he'd be happy by your own account. But as it is, he's become wonderfully wretched, since he's committed the greatest wrongs — {{{ 471b }}} first, he summoned this very man, his own master and uncle, as if to give back the rule that Perdiccas had taken from him, made him and his son Alexander, his own cousin and nearly his own age, his guests, got them drunk, threw them into a wagon, led them out by night, cut their throats, and made them both disappear. After doing this wrong, he failed to notice that he'd become the most wretched of men, and felt no regret for it; but a little {{{ 471c }}} later, given the chance to become happy by justly raising his brother — Perdiccas's legitimate son, a boy of about seven, to whom the rule belonged by right — and giving him back the throne, he refused, and instead threw him into a well, drowned him, and told the boy's mother Cleopatra that he'd fallen in chasing a goose and died. So now, since he's committed the greatest wrongs of anyone in Macedonia, he's the most wretched of all Macedonians, not the happiest, and there's probably some Athenian — starting with you, {{{ 471d }}} — who'd rather become any other Macedonian whatsoever than become Archelaus.

@ Socrates

And right at the start of our conversation, Polus, I praised you for seeming to me well trained in rhetoric, but for having neglected the art of discussion — and now, is this really any different, the argument by which even a child could refute me, and by which you now think I've been refuted, when I say the wrongdoer isn't happy? Where does that follow, my good man? In fact I don't agree with you on any of this.

@ Polus

{{{ 471e }}} That's because you're not willing to, since it does seem to you the way I say.

@ Socrates

My blessed friend, you're trying to refute me the way rhetoricians do, like the men in the law courts who think that's how refutation works. There too, one side thinks it refutes the other whenever it produces many reputable witnesses for what it says, while the side arguing the opposite produces only one witness, or none at all. But this kind of refutation is worth nothing {{{ 472a }}} when it comes to the truth — sometimes a man can be falsely accused by many people who seem to be somebody. And right now, on the point you're making, nearly everyone will agree with you and say the same things, Athenians and foreigners alike, if you want to produce witnesses against me that I'm not telling the truth; you can call on, if you like, Nicias son of Niceratus and his brothers with him, whose tripods stand in a row in the Dionysium; or, if you like, Aristocrates {{{ 472b }}} son of Scellias, whose fine offering stands at the Pythian temple; or, if you like, the whole house of Pericles, or any other family here you care to choose. But I, being just one man, don't agree with you — you're not compelling me, but you're bringing forward many false witnesses against me and trying to drive me out of my property and out of the truth. But unless I can produce you yourself as a single witness agreeing with what I say, I don't think I've proved anything worth mentioning about the matters {{{ 472c }}} our argument concerns; and I don't think you have either, unless I testify for you being just one man alone, and you let all these others go their way. So this is one kind of refutation, as you and many others think it is; but there's another kind too, which I in turn believe in. Let's set them side by side and see whether they differ from each other at all. For in fact the matters we're disputing aren't at all small — they're pretty much the very things it's finest to know and most shameful not to know, since the heart of the matter is knowing or not knowing who is happy and {{{ 472d }}} who is not. To begin with the very point our argument is about right now: you think it's possible for a man doing wrong and being unjust to be happy, given that you consider Archelaus unjust yet happy. Should we take it that this is your view?

@ Polus

Certainly.

@ Socrates

But I say it's impossible. That's one point we disagree on. Very well then — will the wrongdoer be happy if he actually meets with justice and punishment?

@ Polus

Not in the least — in that case he'd be the most wretched of all.
