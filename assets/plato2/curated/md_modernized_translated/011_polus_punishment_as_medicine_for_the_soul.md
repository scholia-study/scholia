## Punishment as Medicine for the Soul

@ Socrates

{{{ 476b }}} Can you say that just things aren't all fine, insofar as they're just? Think it over and tell me.

@ Polus

Yes, I think they are, Socrates.

@ Socrates

Now look at this too; if someone does something, must there also be something that's affected by the one doing it?

@ Polus

I think so.

@ Socrates

And does it suffer whatever the doer does, and in just the way the doer does it? Here's what I mean; if someone strikes, must something be struck?

@ Polus

It must.

@ Socrates

And if the one striking, strikes hard or fast, {{{ 476c }}} must the thing struck be struck in that same way?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

So the thing struck undergoes just the kind of experience the striker produces?

@ Polus

Certainly.

@ Socrates

Then if someone burns something, must something be burned?

@ Polus

How could it not?

@ Socrates

And if someone burns it intensely or painfully, must the thing burned be burned just as the burner burns it?

@ Polus

Certainly.

@ Socrates

Then if something cuts, isn't it the same account? Because something is cut.

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

And if the cut is large or deep or painful, does the thing cut get {{{ 476d }}} just that kind of cut the cutter makes?

@ Polus

It appears so.

@ Socrates

So in short, see whether you agree with what I just said, about everything — that whatever the doer does, the sufferer suffers just that.

@ Polus

Yes, I agree.

@ Socrates

Now that this is agreed, is paying the penalty a matter of suffering something or doing something?

@ Polus

It has to be suffering, Socrates.

@ Socrates

At the hands of someone doing it, then?

@ Polus

How could it not be? At the hands of the one correcting him.

@ Socrates

And does the one who corrects rightly {{{ 476e }}} correct justly?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

Doing just things, or not?

@ Polus

Just things.

@ Socrates

Then the one being corrected, in paying the penalty, suffers just things?

@ Polus

It appears so.

@ Socrates

And just things have been agreed to be fine, haven't they?

@ Polus

Certainly.

@ Socrates

So of these two, one does fine things, and the other — the one being corrected — suffers them.

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

{{{ 477a }}} Then if they're fine, are they also good? Because they're either pleasant or beneficial.

@ Polus

It must be.

@ Socrates

So the one paying the penalty suffers good things?

@ Polus

So it seems.

@ Socrates

So he's benefited?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

The benefit I have in mind? Does he become better in his soul, if he's justly corrected?

@ Polus

Likely so.

@ Socrates

So the one paying the penalty is rid of a badness of soul?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

So is he rid of the greatest {{{ 477b }}} evil? Look at it this way; in a man's property, do you see any badness other than poverty?

@ Polus

No, just poverty.

@ Socrates

And in the body's condition? Would you say weakness and disease and ugliness and things like that are its badness?

@ Polus

I would.

@ Socrates

Then you think there's a corresponding badness in the soul too?

@ Polus

How could there not be?

@ Socrates

And don't you call this injustice, and ignorance, and cowardice, and things like that?

@ Polus

Certainly.

@ Socrates

So, since there are three things — property, {{{ 477c }}} body, and soul — you've named three corresponding kinds of badness: poverty, disease, injustice?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

Which of these kinds of badness is the most shameful? Isn't it injustice, and in a word, the badness of the soul?

@ Polus

By far.

@ Socrates

And if it's the most shameful, is it also the worst?

@ Polus

What do you mean, Socrates?

@ Socrates

Like this; from what's been agreed before, the most shameful thing is always most shameful by producing the greatest pain, or harm, or both.

@ Polus

Certainly.

@ Socrates

And haven't we just agreed {{{ 477d }}} that injustice, and the soul's badness taken as a whole, is the most shameful?

@ Polus

Yes, it's been agreed.

@ Socrates

Then either it's the most distressing, and is the most shameful by exceeding in distress, or in harm, or in both?

@ Polus

It must be.

@ Socrates

So is being unjust and unrestrained and cowardly and ignorant more painful than being poor or sick?

@ Polus

I don't think so, Socrates, not judging by that.

@ Socrates

So it must be by some extraordinary, tremendous harm and astonishing badness, exceeding {{{ 477e }}} everything else, that the soul's badness is the most shameful of all — since it's not by pain, on your own account.

@ Polus

So it appears.

@ Socrates

But surely whatever exceeds in the greatest harm would be the greatest evil there is.

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

So injustice, and lack of restraint, and the soul's other badness, are the greatest evil there is?

@ Polus

So it appears.

@ Socrates

So what craft rids someone of poverty? Isn't it moneymaking?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

And of disease? Isn't it medicine?

@ Polus

{{{ 478a }}} It must be.

@ Socrates

And of badness and injustice? If you're not so ready with that, look at it this way; where, and to whom, do we take people whose bodies are sick?

@ Polus

To the doctors, Socrates.

@ Socrates

And where do we take wrongdoers and the unrestrained?

@ Polus

To the judges, you mean?

@ Socrates

To pay the penalty, isn't that right?

@ Polus

I do.

@ Socrates

So don't those who correct rightly correct by making use of a kind of justice?

@ Polus

Clearly so.

@ Socrates

So moneymaking rids us of poverty, {{{ 478b }}} medicine of disease, and justice of lack of restraint and injustice.

@ Polus

So it appears.

@ Socrates

So which of these [that you mention] is the finest?

@ Polus

Which ones do you mean?

@ Socrates

Moneymaking, medicine, justice.

@ Polus

Justice is far superior, Socrates.

@ Socrates

Then, in turn, it must produce the most pleasure or the most benefit, or both, if it's the finest?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

So is being treated by a doctor pleasant, and do people being treated enjoy it?

@ Polus

I don't think so.

@ Socrates

But it is beneficial. Isn't it?

@ Polus

{{{ 478c }}} Yes.

@ Socrates

Because he's rid of a great evil, so it's worth enduring the pain in order to be healthy.

@ Polus

How could it not be?

@ Socrates

So would a person be happiest in body this way — being treated — or by never being sick in the first place?

@ Polus

Clearly, by never being sick.

@ Socrates

So happiness, it seems, wasn't ridding oneself of an evil, but never having acquired it in the first place.

@ Polus

That's so.

@ Socrates

{{{ 478d }}} And what about this? Of two people who have an evil, either in body or in soul, which is more wretched — the one being treated and getting rid of the evil, or the one not being treated, but keeping it?

@ Polus

The one not being treated, it seems to me.

@ Socrates

Then wasn't paying the penalty a ridding of the greatest evil, badness?

@ Polus

Yes, it was.

@ Socrates

Because it chastens them, and makes them more just, and judicial correction becomes a kind of medicine for badness.

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

So the happiest person is the one who has no badness in his soul, since that turned out to be {{{ 478e }}} the greatest of evils.

@ Polus

Clearly so.

@ Socrates

And second, presumably, the one who's rid of it.

@ Polus

So it seems.

@ Socrates

And that was the one being admonished, and reproved, and paying the penalty.

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

So the one living worst is the one who has [injustice] and isn't rid of it.

@ Polus

So it appears.

@ Socrates

And isn't this the very man who, doing the greatest wrongs and making use of the greatest injustice, manages things so that he's neither admonished {{{ 479a }}} nor corrected nor made to pay the penalty — the way you say Archelaus and the other tyrants and orators and men of power have arranged things for themselves?

@ Polus

So it seems.

@ Socrates

Because these men, my excellent fellow, have managed just about the same thing as if someone gripped by the worst diseases managed to avoid paying his doctors the penalty for what's wrong with his body, and refused treatment, fearing like a child the burning and the cutting, because {{{ 479b }}} it's painful. Or don't you think so too?

@ Polus

I do.

@ Socrates

Not knowing, it seems, what health and the excellence of the body actually are. Because, based on what we've just agreed, Polus, those who run from paying the penalty are probably doing something like this too — seeing only the pain of it, but being blind to the benefit, and not knowing how much more wretched it is to live with an unhealthy soul than with an unhealthy body — a soul that's rotten and unjust {{{ 479c }}} and unholy — which is why they do everything they can to avoid paying the penalty and being rid of the greatest evil, arranging money and friends and making themselves as persuasive as possible; and if what we've agreed is true, Polus, do you notice what follows from the argument? Or would you like us to work it out together?

@ Polus

If you think we should.

@ Socrates

So does it follow that injustice, and doing wrong, {{{ 479d }}} are the greatest evil?

@ Polus

So it appears.

@ Socrates

And surely paying the penalty turned out to be a ridding of this evil?

@ Polus

It seems so.

@ Socrates

And not paying it is a persistence of the evil?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

So doing wrong is second in magnitude among evils; but the wrongdoer's not paying the penalty is by nature the greatest and first of all evils.

@ Polus

So it seems.

@ Socrates

So wasn't this, my friend, what we disputed — you counting Archelaus happy, the man doing the greatest wrongs and {{{ 479e }}} paying no penalty at all, while I believed the opposite: that whoever it is, Archelaus or anyone else on earth, who does wrong and doesn't pay the penalty, deserves to be wretched beyond all other men, and that the wrongdoer is always more wretched than the one wronged, and the one who doesn't pay the penalty more wretched than the one who does? Wasn't that what I was saying?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

Then hasn't it been proved that it was true?

@ Polus

So it appears.

@ Socrates

{{{ 480a }}} Well then; if this is true, Polus, what great use is rhetoric? From what we've just agreed, a man must above all guard himself against doing wrong, on the grounds that he'd have evil enough on his hands. Isn't that so?

@ Polus

Certainly.

@ Socrates

But if he does wrong, either himself or someone else he cares about, he should go there of his own accord, as fast as he can, to pay the penalty, to the judge just as he'd go to a doctor, {{{ 480b }}} hurrying so that the disease of injustice doesn't sit there festering and turn the soul incurable — or how should we put it, Polus, if the earlier agreements still hold for us? Mustn't this agree with what came before, exactly this way and no other?

@ Polus

Well, what else are we to say, Socrates?

@ Socrates

So when it comes to defending his own injustice, or that of his parents, or his friends, or his children, or a wrongdoing country, rhetoric is of no use to us at all, Polus — unless someone should suppose the opposite {{{ 480c }}}: that a man ought to accuse himself most of all, and then his own household, and anyone else among his friends who happens to be doing wrong at any given time, and not hide the wrongdoing but bring it out into the open, so that he pays the penalty and becomes healthy, forcing himself and others not to shrink back but to offer himself up, shutting his eyes and bravely, the way one offers oneself to a doctor for cutting and burning, pursuing what's good and fine and not counting the pain — if he's done something deserving a beating, {{{ 480d }}} offering himself to be beaten, if deserving imprisonment, to be bound, if a fine, paying it, if exile, going into exile, if death, dying — being himself the first accuser, both of himself and of the rest of his household, and using rhetoric for this very purpose, so that once the wrongdoings are made plain, they may be rid of the greatest evil, injustice. Shall we say this, or not, Polus?

@ Polus

{{{ 480e }}} It seems strange to me, Socrates, but I suppose it agrees with what came before.

@ Socrates

Then either those earlier points must be given up, or these must necessarily follow?

@ Polus

Yes, that's how it stands.

@ Socrates

But turning it around the other way — if one really must do harm to someone, whether an enemy or anyone else, provided he himself isn't wronged by the enemy, since that's what has to be guarded against — but if the enemy wrongs someone else, then every means must be prepared, {{{ 481a }}} in what one does and what one says, so that he doesn't pay the penalty and never comes before the judge; and if he does come, one must contrive a way for the enemy to escape and avoid paying the penalty — so that if he ⟨has⟩ stolen a great deal of gold, he keeps it instead of giving it back, and spends it on himself and his own people unjustly and godlessly, and if he's done things deserving death, that he not die — best of all, never, but remain immortal in his wickedness, and failing that, that he live for as {{{ 481b }}} long a time as possible in that condition. It's for purposes like these, Polus, that rhetoric seems useful to me, since for the man who isn't going to do wrong, I don't think its use amounts to much — if indeed it has any use at all, since in what came before it turned out to have none anywhere.
