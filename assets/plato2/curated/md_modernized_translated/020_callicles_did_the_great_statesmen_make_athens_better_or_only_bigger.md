## Did the Great Statesmen Make Athens Better, or Only Bigger?

@ Socrates

{{{ 515a }}} But now, best of men — since you are just now starting to handle the city's affairs yourself, while you keep urging me on and reproaching me for not taking part — come, let's examine each other: has Callicles by now made any citizen better? Is there anyone — foreigner or citizen, slave or free — who was wicked before, unjust and undisciplined and foolish, and has become fine and good because of Callicles? Tell me — {{{ 515b }}} if someone were to question you on this, Callicles — what will you say? Who will you say you have made better by keeping company with you? Are you reluctant to answer, if there is some deed of yours from when you were still a private citizen, before you attempted public life?

@ Callicles

You're a contentious man, Socrates.

@ Socrates

No, I'm not asking out of contentiousness, but because I genuinely want to know in what manner you think one ought to conduct politics among us. Or is it something else you'll take care of for us once you turn to {{{ 515c }}} the city's affairs, rather than making the citizens as good as possible? Or haven't we already agreed many times over that this is what the political man must do? Have we agreed, or not? Answer. We have agreed — I'll answer on your behalf. So if this is what a good man must provide for his own city, think back now and tell me about those men you were mentioning a little earlier — whether you still think they turned out to be good citizens — {{{ 515d }}} Pericles, Cimon, Miltiades, and Themistocles.

@ Callicles

I do.

@ Socrates

Then if they were good, clearly each of them made the citizens better instead of worse. Did he, or not?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

So when Pericles first began speaking before the assembly, were the Athenians worse than when he was giving his last speeches?

@ Callicles

Perhaps.

@ Socrates

Not perhaps, best of men, but a necessity from what we've agreed, if he really was a good citizen.

@ Callicles

{{{ 515e }}} What of it, then?

@ Socrates

Nothing — but tell me this too: whether the Athenians are said to have become better because of Pericles, or, on the contrary, to have been utterly ruined by him. For this is what I hear — that Pericles made the Athenians idle and cowardly and talkative and money-loving, by being the first to establish paid service.

@ Callicles

You're hearing that from the men with the battered ears, Socrates.

@ Socrates

But this I no longer merely hear — both you and I know clearly that Pericles was well regarded at first, and the Athenians voted no shameful conviction against him while they were worse; but once they had become fine and good {{{ 516a }}} because of him, at the end of Pericles's life, they convicted him of theft, and came close to sentencing him to death — clearly because they thought him wicked.

@ Callicles

What of it? Was Pericles bad because of that?

@ Socrates

Well, a keeper of donkeys or horses or cattle, at any rate, would be thought a bad one if this were his record — if he took them over not kicking or butting or biting him, and turned them into animals doing all of that out of savageness. Or don't you think any keeper of any animal whatever is a bad one, {{{ 516b }}} if he takes over gentler creatures and turns them out wilder than he received them? Do you think so, or not?

@ Callicles

Certainly — just to indulge you.

@ Socrates

Then indulge me on this too, and answer: is man one of the animals, or not?

@ Callicles

Of course he is.

@ Socrates

So Pericles was taking care of human beings?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

What then? Shouldn't they, as we just agreed, have become more just instead of more unjust because of him, {{{ 516c }}} if he really was taking care of them as a man good in political matters?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

Now the just are gentle, as Homer said — but what do you say? Isn't that so?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

And yet he made them come out wilder than he received them — and toward himself, of all people, whom he'd least have wished it.

@ Callicles

Do you want me to agree with you?

@ Socrates

If you think I'm telling the truth.

@ Callicles

All right, let it be so.

@ Socrates

Then if wilder, also more unjust and worse?

@ Callicles

{{{ 516d }}} Let it be.

@ Socrates

Then by this argument Pericles was not good at politics.

@ Callicles

Not by your account, anyway.

@ Socrates

No, by Zeus, nor by yours either, from what you've agreed. Now tell me again about Cimon — didn't the very men he served ostracize him, so as not to hear his voice for ten years? And didn't they do the same to Themistocles, and add exile as punishment? And didn't they vote to throw Miltiades, the hero of Marathon, {{{ 516e }}} into the pit, and if not for the presiding officer, wouldn't he have gone in? And yet if these men were good, as you claim, they would never have suffered any of this. Good charioteers, surely, don't fall out of their chariots at the start and then fall out later, once they've trained their horses and become better drivers themselves — that isn't how it works, either in chariot-driving or in any other line of work — or do you think it is?

@ Callicles

Not to me.

@ Socrates

So it seems our earlier arguments were true, {{{ 517a }}} that we know of no man who has turned out good at politics in this city. You agreed there was none among the men of today, but there was among the men of the past, and you picked out these particular men — but they've turned out to be no better than the men of today, so that, if they were orators, they used neither the true rhetoric — for then they wouldn't have fallen — nor the flattering kind.

@ Callicles

But it's far from the case, Socrates, that any man alive today could accomplish deeds like the ones any of these men — {{{ 517b }}} whichever one you please — accomplished.

@ Socrates

My good man, I'm not faulting them as servants of the city either — they seem to me to have been more capable servants than the men of today, and better able to supply the city with what it desired. But when it comes to redirecting people's desires instead of indulging them, persuading and compelling the citizens toward whatever would actually make them better — {{{ 517c }}} in that, they differed from the men of today, one might say, not at all; and that is the one task of a good citizen. As for ships and walls and dockyards and plenty of other things of that kind, I grant you those men were cleverer at supplying them. So you and I are doing something absurd in our discussion — the whole time we've been talking, we haven't stopped circling back to the same point, each failing to grasp what the other is saying. At any rate, I think you've many times agreed and recognized that this business, {{{ 517d }}} whether concerning the body or the soul, comes in two kinds: one of them is a service, capable of supplying, if our bodies are hungry, food, if thirsty, drink, if cold, clothes, bedding, shoes, or whatever else bodies come to desire — and I'm deliberately using the same images with you, so you'll follow more easily. For being able to supply {{{ 517e }}} any of these things — whether as a shopkeeper or a merchant or a craftsman of one of them, a baker or a cook or a weaver or a shoemaker or a tanner — it's no wonder that a man of that kind seems, both to himself and to others, to be a caretaker of the body, to anyone who doesn't know that there is, apart from all these, one craft, physical training together with medicine, which really is the caretaking of the body, and which rightly ought to rule over all these crafts and make use of their products, because it alone knows what is good and bad among foods and drinks for the {{{ 518a }}} excellence of the body, while all the others are ignorant of this — which is why these other crafts, in dealing with the body, are slavish and servile and unfree, while physical training and medicine are, by right, their mistresses. That the same holds for the soul as well — at times you seem to me to understand what I'm saying, and agree as though you know what I mean; but a little later you come round saying that men {{{ 518b }}} have become fine and good citizens in the city, and when I ask which men, you seem to me to be putting forward, for politics, men of exactly the same stripe as if, when I asked about physical training who has become or is a good caretaker of bodies, you told me quite in earnest, Thearion the baker, and Mithaecus, who wrote the book on Sicilian cookery, and Sarambus the wine-merchant — that these men have turned out to be wonderful caretakers of bodies, one {{{ 518c }}} preparing wonderful bread, another relish, another wine. Well, perhaps you'd be indignant if I told you: my good man, you understand nothing about physical training — you're telling me about servants, men who supply desires, with no understanding of anything fine and good about the matter, who, if it so happens, stuff and fatten people's bodies, get praised for it, and along the way destroy their old flesh too; while the {{{ 518d }}} people themselves, in their inexperience, won't blame the ones who feasted them as the cause of their sicknesses and the loss of their old flesh, but rather whoever happens to be present at the time offering some advice, once that earlier overindulgence brings on sickness a good while later, arriving as it does with no regard for health — these are the ones they'll blame and censure and do some harm to, if they can, while praising those earlier men, the actual causes of the trouble, {{{ 518e }}} to the skies. And that, Callicles, is exactly what you're doing now — you praise men who have feasted the citizens, filling them up with whatever they wanted. And people say these men made the city great — but that it's swollen and festering underneath {{{ 519a }}} because of those men of old, that they don't perceive. For without moderation and justice they've filled the city with harbors and dockyards and walls and tribute and nonsense of that kind; so when this collapse of its weakness comes, they'll blame whoever happens to be advising at the time, and praise Themistocles and Cimon and Pericles, the actual causes of the trouble — and perhaps they'll take hold of you too, if you aren't careful, and of my friend Alcibiades, once they start losing even the old possessions {{{ 519b }}} along with what they've newly gained — though you two aren't the causes of the trouble, only perhaps its accomplices. And yet I see a foolish thing happening even now, and I hear the same about the men of old. For I notice that whenever the city takes some political man to task as a wrongdoer, they grow indignant and complain bitterly at the terrible treatment they're suffering — as if, having done the city many good services, they're now being unjustly destroyed by it, or so their story goes. But the whole thing is a lie — no leader of a city {{{ 519c }}} could ever be unjustly destroyed by the very city he leads. It seems the same holds for all who claim to be statesmen as for all who claim to be sophists. For the sophists too, wise as they are in other respects, do this absurd thing — claiming to be teachers of virtue, they often accuse their own students of wronging them, cheating them of their fees and paying back no other gratitude either, {{{ 519d }}} after having been well treated by them. And what could be more unreasonable than this claim — that men who have become good and just, who have had injustice removed from them by their teacher and have acquired justice instead, should do wrong by means of something they no longer have? Doesn't that strike you as absurd, my friend? You've truly forced me into giving a speech, Callicles, by refusing to answer.

@ Callicles

And you wouldn't be able to talk, if no one answered you?
