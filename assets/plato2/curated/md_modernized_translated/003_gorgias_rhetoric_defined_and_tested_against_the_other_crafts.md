## Rhetoric Defined, and Tested Against the Other Crafts

@ Socrates

{{{ 449b }}} And should we say you're able to make others so too?

@ Gorgias

Yes, that's exactly what I profess — not only here, but elsewhere too.

@ Socrates

Would you be willing, Gorgias, to keep going the way we're talking now, one of us asking and the other answering, and set aside for another time that lengthy kind of speech Polus started with? But don't break your promise — be willing to answer briefly, whatever you're asked.

@ Gorgias

Some answers, Socrates, do need to be given at length; all the same, {{{ 449c }}} I'll try to be as brief as I can. In fact this is itself one of the things I claim — that no one could say the same things in fewer words than I can.

@ Socrates

That's exactly what's needed, Gorgias — give me a display of this very thing, brevity of speech, and save length for another time.

@ Gorgias

I will — and you'll say you've never heard anyone briefer.

@ Socrates

Come, then — you say you have knowledge of the craft of rhetoric {{{ 449d }}} and could make another person an orator too; what thing among the things there are does rhetoric turn out to be concerned with? The way weaving is concerned with the working of cloth — isn't that right?

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

And music is concerned with the composing of tunes?

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

By Hera, Gorgias, I do admire your answers — you answer as briefly as anyone could.

@ Gorgias

Yes, I think I do that reasonably well, Socrates.

@ Socrates

Good. Now answer me the same way about rhetoric: what among the things there are is it knowledge of?

@ Gorgias

{{{ 449e }}} Of speeches.

@ Socrates

What sort of speeches, Gorgias? The ones that show the sick how they might get well by following a regimen?

@ Gorgias

No.

@ Socrates

Then rhetoric isn't concerned with all speeches.

@ Gorgias

No, certainly not.

@ Socrates

But it does make people capable of speaking.

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

And also capable of understanding the very things they speak about?

@ Gorgias

Of course.

@ Socrates

Now does the craft we just mentioned {{{ 450a }}} — medicine — make people able to understand and speak about the sick?

@ Gorgias

It must.

@ Socrates

So medicine too, it seems, is concerned with speeches.

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

The ones about diseases?

@ Gorgias

Exactly.

@ Socrates

And physical training too is concerned with speeches about the good and bad condition of bodies?

@ Gorgias

Certainly.

@ Socrates

And indeed the other crafts too, Gorgias, are {{{ 450b }}} like this — each of them is concerned with the speeches that happen to be about the very subject matter of that craft.

@ Gorgias

So it appears.

@ Socrates

Then why in the world don't you call the other crafts forms of rhetoric too, since they're concerned with speeches, if you call this one rhetoric just because it's concerned with speeches?

@ Gorgias

Because, Socrates, the knowledge involved in the other crafts is, so to speak, entirely about manual work and actions of that kind, but rhetoric has no such manual work at all — its whole action and effect {{{ 450c }}} comes through speech. That's why I hold that the craft of rhetoric is concerned with speeches — and I'm speaking correctly, or so I claim.

@ Socrates

So do I understand what you want to call it? I'll soon know more clearly. But answer me: we have crafts. Isn't that so?

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

Of all the crafts, I think, some are mostly manual work and need little speech, while some need none at all — the work of the craft could be carried out even in silence, like painting and sculpture and many others. It's crafts like these {{{ 450d }}} you seem to me to mean, the ones you say rhetoric isn't concerned with — or not?

@ Gorgias

You've understood me very well indeed, Socrates.

@ Socrates

But there are other crafts that accomplish everything through speech, and need, so to speak, either no work at all or very little — arithmetic, calculation, geometry, and board games too, and many other crafts — some of which have speeches roughly equal to their actions, most have more, and in general their whole action and {{{ 450e }}} effect comes through speech. It's one of these that you seem to me to mean by rhetoric.

@ Gorgias

That's true.

@ Socrates

But I do not think you actually want to call any of these rhetoric — not that you did not say, in those very words, that whatever has its effect through speech is rhetoric, so that someone could object, wanting to be difficult in argument, then you call arithmetic rhetoric too, Gorgias? No, I don't think you mean to call either arithmetic or geometry rhetoric.

@ Gorgias

{{{ 451a }}} You're right to think so, Socrates, and fair to take it that way.

@ Socrates

Now you too, go ahead and complete the answer I asked for. Since rhetoric happens to be one of these crafts that rely mostly on speech, and other crafts happen to be like this too, try to say what rhetoric is, the one whose effect in speech is concerned with what. Just as if someone asked me, about any of the crafts I just mentioned: Socrates, what {{{ 451b }}} is the craft of arithmetic? I would tell him, as you just did, that it is one of those whose effect comes through speech. And if he asked me further: concerned with what? I would say it is concerned with the odd and the even — [a knowledge of] however many each of them turns out to be. And if he asked again: and what craft do you call calculation? I would say that this one too is among those whose whole effect is settled by speech, and if he asked further: concerned with what? I would say, like the men who draft {{{ 451c }}} decrees in the assembly, that calculation is like arithmetic in other respects, since it is concerned with the same thing, the odd and the even, but differs this much: it also examines how much the odd and the even amount to, both in themselves and against each other. And if someone asked about astronomy, when I said that it too has everything settled by speech, and he then said: and what are the speeches of astronomy about, Socrates? I would say they are about the motion of the stars and the sun and the moon, and how their speeds compare to one another.

@ Gorgias

You're right, Socrates.

@ Socrates

{{{ 451d }}} Now it's your turn, Gorgias. Rhetoric, as it happens, is one of the crafts that accomplish and settle everything through speech — isn't that so?

@ Gorgias

That's so.

@ Socrates

Tell me, then: concerned with what? ⟨What⟩ is this thing, among the things there are, that these speeches rhetoric uses are about?

@ Gorgias

The greatest and best of human affairs, Socrates.

@ Socrates

But, Gorgias, that's still disputable, and {{{ 451e }}} not at all clear yet. I think you've heard, at drinking parties, men singing that drinking song in which they list, as they sing, that being healthy is best, being fine second, and third, as the poet of the song says, being rich without deceit.

@ Gorgias

I have heard it — but why do you say this?

@ Socrates

{{{ 452a }}} Suppose the craftsmen of what that drinking song praised stood right there before you — a doctor, a trainer, and a moneymaker — and the doctor spoke first: Socrates, Gorgias is deceiving you, since his craft is not concerned with the greatest good for human beings, but mine is — and suppose I then asked him: and who are you, to say this? He would probably say a doctor. So what are you saying? Is the product of your craft the greatest good? Of course, he'd probably say, Socrates — health. And what greater good {{{ 452b }}} is there for people than health? And if after him the trainer said: I would myself be surprised, Socrates, if Gorgias could show you a greater good from his own craft than I can from mine — I would say to him in turn: and who exactly are you, my friend, and what is your product? A trainer, he would say, and my product is making people's bodies fine and strong. After the trainer the moneymaker would speak up, looking down on absolutely everyone, I imagine: {{{ 452c }}} just consider, Socrates, whether any good greater than wealth turns up for you, from Gorgias or from anyone else at all. So we would say to him: well, what of it? Are you the craftsman of this? He would say so. Being who? A moneymaker. Well then? Do you judge wealth to be the greatest good for human beings? We shall ask him. Of course it is. He'll say so. And yet Gorgias here claims that his own craft is responsible for a greater good than yours, we would say. Clearly the next thing he would ask is: and what {{{ 452d }}} is this good? Let Gorgias answer. So, Gorgias, taking it that you're being asked both by them and by me, answer: what is this thing you say is the greatest good for human beings, of which you are the craftsman?

@ Gorgias

Exactly what is, in truth, the greatest good, Socrates, and the cause at once of freedom for people themselves, and of each of them ruling over others in his own city.

@ Socrates

Then what exactly do you mean by that?
