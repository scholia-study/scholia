## Currying a Tyrant's Favour, and the Craft That Saves Lives

@ Callicles

{{{ 510a }}} Let's grant you that, Socrates, so that you can finish the argument.

@ Socrates

So for this too, it seems, we need to acquire some power and craft, so that we don't do wrong.

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

So what craft, then, provides for not being wronged at all, or as little as possible? See if you think the same as I do. Because it seems to me to be this: either a man must rule in the city himself, or even be tyrant, or be a partner of the government currently in power.

@ Callicles

You see, Socrates, how ready I am to praise you, {{{ 510b }}} whenever you say something well? This seems to me very well put.

@ Socrates

Then consider this too, and see if you think I'm right. It seems to me that each person is a friend to another as much as possible in the way the ancients and the wise put it — like to like. Don't you think so too?

@ Callicles

I do.

@ Socrates

So wherever a savage, uneducated man is tyrant and ruler, if someone in the city were far better than he is, the tyrant would surely be afraid of him, and could never wholeheartedly {{{ 510c }}} become his friend?

@ Callicles

That's so.

@ Socrates

Nor, if someone were far worse, could that man be his friend either; the tyrant would look down on him, and would never take him seriously as a friend.

@ Callicles

That's true too.

@ Socrates

So the only friend worth mentioning left for a man like that is someone of the same character, who blames and praises the same things, and is willing to be ruled and to submit to the ruler. This man will have great power {{{ 510d }}} in that city, and no one will wrong him and get away with it. Isn't that how it is?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

So if one of the young men in that city were to consider, in what way could I gain great power and have no one wrong me? This, it seems, is his path: to accustom himself, right from youth, to take pleasure and displeasure in the same things as his master, and to arrange things so that he becomes as much like him as possible. Isn't that so?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

So this man will have achieved both not being wronged and having great power {{{ 510e }}} in the city, according to your account.

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

But does he also achieve not doing wrong? Or is that far from true, if he's going to be like a ruler who's unjust, and have great power at his side? No — I think it's exactly the opposite: his preparation will be aimed at being able to do as much wrong as possible, and doing wrong without paying the penalty. Isn't that so?

@ Callicles

It seems so.

@ Socrates

{{{ 511a }}} So the greatest evil will befall him — being corrupt in his soul and maimed, because of his imitation of his master and his power.

@ Callicles

I don't know how you keep turning the arguments upside down every time, Socrates; or don't you know that the man who does the imitating will kill the one who doesn't, if he wants to, and take his property away?

@ Socrates

{{{ 511b }}} I know it, my good Callicles, unless I'm deaf — I've heard it just now from you, and from Polus, many times over, and from nearly everyone else in the city; but you listen to me too: he'll kill him, if he wants — but he'll be a wicked man killing a fine and good one.

@ Callicles

And isn't that exactly the outrageous part?

@ Socrates

Not to a man with sense, at any rate, as the argument shows. Or do you think a person ought to arrange to live as long as possible, and practice the crafts that always save us from {{{ 511c }}} danger — the way you tell me to practice rhetoric, the kind that saves people in the courtroom?

@ Callicles

Yes, by Zeus, and giving you good advice too.

@ Socrates

But what about this, my excellent fellow? Does the knowledge of swimming seem to you to be some solemn thing?

@ Callicles

No, by Zeus, not to me.

@ Socrates

And yet it too saves people from death, whenever they fall into a situation where that knowledge is needed. And if that seems too small a thing to you, I'll tell you of a greater one {{{ 511d }}}: the pilot's craft, which saves not only souls but also bodies and property from the direst dangers, the way rhetoric does. And this craft is modest and orderly, and doesn't put on airs, dressing itself up as though it were doing something magnificent, but having accomplished the very same result as the courtroom pleader's craft — if it brings someone safely here from Aegina, I think it charges two obols, and if from Egypt or the Black Sea, {{{ 511e }}} however great the distance, for this great service — having saved, as I was just saying, the man himself, his children, his property, and his womenfolk — it sets them ashore at the harbor and charges two drachmas; and the man who has the craft, having accomplished all that, steps off the ship and walks along the beach beside it in a modest manner; for I imagine he knows how to reckon that it's unclear which of his fellow passengers he benefited by not letting them drown, and which he harmed, knowing that he put none of them {{{ 512a }}} ashore any better than they were when they boarded, neither in body nor in soul. So he reckons that it isn't the case that if a man burdened with great, incurable diseases of the body failed to drown, that man is wretched for not having died, and got no benefit from him; but that if someone, on the other hand, has many incurable diseases in the more valuable part of the body, the soul, and this man is to go on living, the pilot will do him a service by saving him, whether from the sea, or the courtroom, or wherever else {{{ 512b }}} he saves him from — no, he knows that it's no better for a corrupt man to live; for such a man is bound to live badly.

That's why it isn't customary for the pilot to give himself airs, even though he saves us, nor for the engineer either, my remarkable friend, who's sometimes able to save no less than a general — to say nothing of a pilot — or anyone else; because sometimes he saves whole cities. Doesn't he seem to you to be on the same level as the courtroom pleader? And yet if he wanted to talk the way you people do, Callicles, giving the business a grand air {{{ 512c }}}, he could bury you in words, saying and urging that everyone ought to become an engineer, since nothing else amounts to anything; his argument would be more than adequate for it. But you look down on him and his craft all the same, and you'd use engineer as an insult, and you wouldn't want to give your daughter to his son, or take his daughter for yourself. And yet, given the grounds on which you praise your own pursuits, what fair reason do you have to look down on the engineer and {{{ 512d }}} the others I just mentioned? I know you'd say you're better, and from better people. But if the better isn't what I say it is, and instead virtue itself just is saving yourself and what's yours, whatever sort of person you happen to be, then your contempt for the engineer, the doctor, and the other crafts made for the sake of saving people becomes ridiculous. No, my good man, consider whether what's noble and good might be something other than saving and being saved. Because a man who's truly a man should let this — living for however long {{{ 512e }}} — go, and not cling to life, but leave these matters to the god and trust the women who say no one can escape his fated portion, and then turn to the question of how he might live as well as possible in whatever time he has left — whether by making {{{ 513a }}} himself like the government of the city he lives in, and so whether you now need to become as much like the Athenian people as possible, if you're going to be dear to them and have great power in the city; consider whether that actually pays off for you and for me, so that we don't end up, my good friend, suffering what they say the Thessalian witches suffer who pull down the moon — that we pay for this power in the city with what we hold dearest. But if you think any human being at all is going to hand you a craft {{{ 513b }}} that will let you have great power in this city while being unlike its government, whether for better or for worse, then, as it seems to me, you're not planning correctly, Callicles; you can't be an imitator — you have to be naturally like them, if you're going to produce anything genuine that wins the friendship of the Athenian people, and, by Zeus, of Pyrilampes' son too. So whoever makes you most like these people will make you into what you long to be — a politician and orator; for each group takes pleasure in speeches made to match its own character {{{ 513c }}}, and resents speeches that don't fit it, unless you have something else to say, my dear fellow. Do we have anything to say to that, Callicles?

@ Callicles

I don't know how it is, Socrates, but you seem to me to be right somehow, yet I feel what most people feel — I'm not entirely convinced by you.

@ Socrates

That's because the love of the people, Callicles, at home in your soul, is resisting me; but if we examine these same things over again many times, [perhaps even] better {{{ 513d }}}, you'll be convinced. Just recall that we said there were two ways of caring for each thing, body and soul alike: one that deals with pleasure, and another that deals with what's best, not indulging but fighting against it. Isn't that what we settled on back then?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

So the one that deals with pleasure is base, and turns out to be nothing but flattery — isn't that so?

@ Callicles

{{{ 513e }}} Let it be so for you, if you like.

@ Socrates

And the other one aims at making whatever we're caring for — body or soul, whichever it happens to be — as good as possible?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

So is this how we ought to go about caring for the city and its citizens — trying to make the citizens themselves as good as possible? Because without that, as we found earlier, there's no use in providing {{{ 514a }}} any other benefit at all, unless the intentions of the people who are going to acquire great wealth, or rule over others, or hold any other kind of power, are fine and good. Shall we say that's how it is?

@ Callicles

Certainly, if that's more agreeable to you.

@ Socrates

So if, Callicles, we were calling on each other to take up public affairs and turn to building projects — the greatest structures, whether walls, dockyards, or temples — should we first have examined and questioned ourselves {{{ 514b }}}, whether we knew the craft of building or not, and from whom we'd learned it? Should we have, or not?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

And here's the second question: whether we'd ever actually built a building on our own, either for a friend or for ourselves, and whether that building was fine or shameful; and if, on examining the matter, we found that our teachers had been good and {{{ 514c }}} distinguished, and that we'd built many fine buildings together with our teachers, and many more on our own once we'd parted ways with them — in that condition, it would make sense to go on to public works; but if we had no teacher of our own to point to, and either no buildings at all, or many that weren't worth anything, then surely it would be senseless to attempt public works and call on each other to undertake them. Shall we say that's rightly put, {{{ 514d }}} or not?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

And so it is with everything else too; and if we'd taken it upon ourselves to practice medicine in public and called on each other as competent doctors, surely I'd have examined you and you'd have examined me — come, by the gods, how is Socrates himself doing in bodily health? Or has anyone else, slave or free, ever been rid of a disease because of Socrates? I imagine I'd have questioned you along the same lines too; and if we found that no one had become better in body {{{ 514e }}} because of us, neither foreigner nor citizen, neither man nor woman, then by Zeus, Callicles, wouldn't it truly be laughable, for people to reach such a pitch of folly that, before practicing privately, doing a great many things as chance would have it, and getting a good many right, and training sufficiently in the craft, they attempt — as the saying goes — to learn pottery on a full-sized jar, and try to practice in public themselves, and call on other people like themselves to do the same? Doesn't it seem to you that acting that way would be senseless?

@ Callicles

It does to me.
