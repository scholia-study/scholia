## Rhetoric as Flattery, Not a Craft

@ Polus

What's this, Socrates? Do you actually believe about rhetoric what you're saying just now? Or do you think — because Gorgias was ashamed not to agree with you that the orator also knows what is just and fine and good, and that if someone came to him without knowing these things he would teach him himself, and then perhaps some contradiction turned up in the discussion {{{ 461c }}} out of that admission — this very thing ⟨which⟩ you're so pleased with, having led him into such questions yourself, since who do you think would deny knowing justice himself and being able to teach it to others? But it's real boorishness to drag arguments into that kind of place.

@ Socrates

My dear Polus, this is exactly why we acquire companions and sons — so that when we older men trip up, you younger ones will be there to set our lives straight, both in what we do and what we say. And now, if Gorgias and I are tripping up anywhere in this discussion {{{ 461d }}}, you're here — correct us, since you're a fair man — and I'm willing to take back anything from what's been agreed, if it seems to you not to have been agreed well, whatever you like, provided you guard just one thing for me.

@ Polus

What's that?

@ Socrates

Your long-windedness, Polus — rein it in, the kind you tried to use at first.

@ Polus

What? Won't I be allowed to say as much as I like?

@ Socrates

You'd be in real trouble, my excellent fellow, if you came all the way to Athens {{{ 461e }}} — where there's more freedom to speak than anywhere else in Greece — and then turned out to be the one man here denied it. But turn it around — if you go on talking at length and refuse to answer what you're asked, wouldn't I be the one in trouble, if I'm not allowed to get up {{{ 462a }}} and stop listening to you? But if you actually care about the argument we've been having and want to set it right, as I was just saying, take back whatever you like, and question and be questioned in turn, the way Gorgias and I did — test me, and let yourself be tested. You do say, I take it, that you know the same things Gorgias knows — or don't you?

@ Polus

I do.

@ Socrates

Then you're inviting people to ask you anything they want, whenever they want, on the assumption that you'll know how to answer?

@ Polus

Certainly.

@ Socrates

Well then, do whichever one you like right now {{{ 462b }}} — ask, or answer.

@ Polus

All right, I'll do that. Answer me this, Socrates: since Gorgias seems to you to be at a loss about rhetoric, what do you say it is?

@ Socrates

Are you asking what craft I say it is?

@ Polus

I am.

@ Socrates

None at all, Polus — to tell you the truth.

@ Polus

Then what do you think rhetoric is?

@ Socrates

A thing that you claim, in the treatise {{{ 462c }}} I read recently, to have turned into a craft.

@ Polus

What do you mean by that?

@ Socrates

A kind of knack, I'd say.

@ Polus

So rhetoric seems to you to be a knack?

@ Socrates

Yes, unless you have something else to say.

@ Polus

A knack for what?

@ Socrates

For producing a kind of gratification and pleasure.

@ Polus

Then don't you think rhetoric is a fine thing — being able to gratify people?

@ Socrates

What's this, Polus? Have you already heard from me what I say it is {{{ 462d }}}, that you're now asking on top of that whether I think it's fine?

@ Polus

Didn't I hear you say it's a kind of knack?

@ Socrates

Since you value gratifying people so much, would you like to do me a small favor?

@ Polus

I would.

@ Socrates

Ask me now what craft I think cookery is.

@ Polus

All right, I'm asking: what craft is cookery?

@ Socrates

None, Polus.

@ Polus

Then what is it? Say.

@ Socrates

I say a kind of knack.

@ Polus

What kind? Say.

@ Socrates

I say a knack for producing gratification and pleasure, Polus {{{ 462e }}}.

@ Polus

So cookery and rhetoric are the same thing?

@ Socrates

Not at all — but they're parts of the same pursuit.

@ Polus

What pursuit do you mean?

@ Socrates

I'm afraid saying the truth might come off as rather crude — I hesitate to say it for Gorgias's sake, in case he thinks I'm making fun of what he does for a living. As for whether this is the rhetoric Gorgias practices {{{ 463a }}}, I don't know — nothing in our conversation just now made clear what he actually thinks it is — but what I call rhetoric is a part of something that isn't fine at all.

@ Gorgias

A part of what, Socrates? Speak — don't feel embarrassed on my account.

@ Socrates

Well, it seems to me, Gorgias, that there's a certain practice that isn't a craft at all, but belongs to a soul that's good at guessing, bold, and naturally clever at dealing with people {{{ 463b }}} — and I call the general name for it flattery. This pursuit, it seems to me, has many other parts, and cookery is one of them — it looks like a craft, but by my account it isn't a craft at all, just a knack and a routine. I call rhetoric a part of this same thing, and cosmetics too, and sophistry — four parts over four things. So if Polus wants to ask about it, let him ask — because he hasn't yet learned what part of flattery I say rhetoric is {{{ 463c }}}; it's escaped him that I haven't answered that yet, and instead he's asking me on top of it whether I think it's fine. I won't answer him whether I think rhetoric is fine or shameful until I first answer what it is. That wouldn't be fair, Polus — but if you really want to find out, ask what part of flattery I say rhetoric is.

@ Polus

All right, I'm asking — tell me what part.

@ Socrates

Would you actually understand it if I answered {{{ 463d }}}? By my account, rhetoric is the image of a part of politics.

@ Polus

Well then? Do you say it's fine or shameful?

@ Socrates

Shameful, I'd say — I call bad things shameful — since I have to answer you as though you already know what I mean.

@ Gorgias

By Zeus, Socrates, I myself don't even understand what you mean.

@ Socrates

Naturally, Gorgias {{{ 463e }}} — I haven't said anything clear yet, and Polus here is young and sharp.

@ Gorgias

Well, never mind him — tell me how you mean that rhetoric is the image of a part of politics.

@ Socrates

I'll try to explain what rhetoric seems to me to be — and if it turns out not to be this, Polus here {{{ 464a }}} will refute me. You do speak of a body, and of a soul, don't you?

@ Gorgias

Of course.

@ Socrates

And you think each of these has a certain good condition?

@ Gorgias

I do.

@ Socrates

What about this? A good condition that only seems good, but isn't? Here's what I mean: many people appear to have bodies in good condition, and no one could easily tell they're not, except a doctor or someone skilled in physical training.

@ Gorgias

That's true.

@ Socrates

I'm saying the same thing exists in both body and soul — something that makes the body and the soul seem to be in good shape, {{{ 464b }}} without actually being any better off.

@ Gorgias

That's so.

@ Socrates

Come, let me try to show you more clearly what I mean, if I can. Since there are two things, I speak of two crafts: the one concerned with the soul I call politics; the one concerned with the body I can't give you a single name for that way, but since caring for the body is one thing, I name two parts of it, physical training and medicine; and within politics, corresponding to physical training, I put legislation, and corresponding to medicine, justice. These two pairs share something with each other {{{ 464c }}}, since they're concerned with the same subject — medicine with physical training, and justice with legislation — but all the same they differ somewhat from one another. Now, there being these four, all of them always tending toward what's best — some for the body, some for the soul — flattery notices this — not by knowing, I mean, but by guessing — and splits itself into four parts, and slips in under each of these, and pretends {{{ 464d }}} to be the very thing it has slipped under, caring nothing for what's best, and hunting after folly with whatever's most pleasant at the moment, and tricking it, so that it seems to be worth the most. So cookery has slipped in under medicine, and pretends to know the best foods for the body, so that if a cook and a doctor had to compete in front of children, or in front of grown men just as foolish as children, over which of the two understands good foods and {{{ 464e }}} bad, the doctor would die of hunger. This whole thing I call flattery, and I say it's shameful {{{ 465a }}}, Polus — this is what I'm saying to you — because it aims at what's pleasant without regard for what's best; and I say it isn't a craft but a knack, because it has no account to give of the nature of what it applies and why it applies it, so that it can't state the cause of anything. And I don't call a thing a craft when it's a reasonless business; if you dispute any of this, I'm willing to give my reasons. {{{ 465b }}}

So medicine, as I say, has cookery-flattery lying beneath it; and in the same way physical training has cosmetics beneath it — a mischievous, deceptive, low, and unfree thing, deceiving through shapes and colors and smooth finish and clothing, so that it makes people chase a borrowed beauty and neglect the true beauty that comes through physical training. So as not to go on too long, I want to put it to you the way geometers do — you'll probably follow now {{{ 465c }}} — that what cosmetics is to physical training, sophistry is to legislation, and what cookery is to medicine, rhetoric is to justice. This is what I mean: they're distinct by nature in this way, but because they're so close, sophists and orators get mixed up together over the same territory, and neither they themselves nor other people can tell what use to make of them. In fact, if the soul {{{ 465d }}} didn't govern the body, but the body governed itself, and cookery and medicine weren't observed and distinguished by the soul but the body itself judged them, weighing them by the gratification it got — dear Polus, you know Anaxagoras's saying well — everything would get mixed up together in one heap, with no way to tell apart what belongs to medicine, to health, and to cookery. So you've now heard what I say rhetoric is: the counterpart {{{ 465e }}}, in the soul, of cookery in the body. Maybe it's strange of me, forbidding you to make long speeches and then stringing out such a long one myself. But I deserve to be forgiven for it — when I spoke briefly you didn't follow, and you couldn't make any use of the answer I gave you, but needed a full explanation. So if I in turn can't make use of your answers when you answer {{{ 466a }}}, string out a speech yourself as well; but if I can make use of them, let me — that's only fair. And now, if there's anything in this answer you can use, use it.

@ Polus

Well then, what do you say? Does rhetoric seem to you to be flattery?

@ Socrates

I said a part of flattery. Don't you remember, at your age, Polus? What will you do later on?

@ Polus

So do you think good orators are looked down on in their cities as flatterers?
