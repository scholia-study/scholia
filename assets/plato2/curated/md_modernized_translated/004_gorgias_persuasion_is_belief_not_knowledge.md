## Persuasion Is Belief, Not Knowledge

@ Gorgias

{{{ 452e }}} I mean the ability to persuade with speeches — jurors in a courtroom, councillors in a council-chamber, assemblymen in an assembly, and every other gathering there is, whatever kind of political gathering it turns out to be. And yet with that power you will have the doctor as your slave, and the trainer as your slave too; and as for that businessman, he will turn out to be making his money for someone else, not for himself — for you, the one able to speak and persuade the masses.

@ Socrates

Now, Gorgias, I think you've made fairly clear what kind of craft you take {{{ 453a }}} rhetoric to be — if I understand you, you're saying rhetoric is a producer of persuasion, and that its whole business and its sum total comes down to that; or can you say that rhetoric is able to do anything more than produce persuasion for the soul of the listener?

@ Gorgias

Not at all, Socrates — I think you've defined it well enough, since that is indeed its sum total.

@ Socrates

Listen, then, Gorgias. Be assured that, as {{{ 453b }}} I persuade myself, if anyone ever converses with anyone wanting truly to know the very thing the discussion is about, I am one of those people — and I expect the same of you.

@ Gorgias

What is it, then, Socrates?

@ Socrates

I'll tell you now. The persuasion that comes from rhetoric — whatever it is that you mean, and whatever sort of things it's persuasion about — I certainly don't know for sure, though I do have a guess as to what you mean and what it concerns; but I'll ask you all the same what you mean by the persuasion that comes from {{{ 453c }}} rhetoric, and what it's about. Now why am I asking you, when I have my own guess, instead of just saying it myself? Not for your sake, but for the sake of the argument, so that it proceeds in whatever way will make the subject clearest to us. Consider whether you think I'm right to press you this way — suppose I happened to be asking you who Zeuxis is among painters, and you told me he's the one who paints animals, wouldn't I be right to ask further which animals, and where?

@ Gorgias

Quite so.

@ Socrates

{{{ 453d }}} Is that because there are other painters too, painting many other animals?

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

But if no one painted except Zeuxis, would your answer have been a good one?

@ Gorgias

How could it not?

@ Socrates

Come now, tell me about rhetoric too: do you think persuasion is produced by rhetoric alone, or by other crafts as well? What I mean is this: whoever teaches any subject at all — does what he teaches persuade, or not?

@ Gorgias

Certainly it does, Socrates — more than anything.

@ Socrates

{{{ 453e }}} Let's go back, then, to the same crafts we just mentioned — doesn't arithmetic teach us how many things there are, in number, and the arithmetician too?

@ Gorgias

Certainly.

@ Socrates

And doesn't it also persuade?

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

So arithmetic too is a producer of persuasion?

@ Gorgias

So it appears.

@ Socrates

So if someone asks us what kind of persuasion, and about what, we'll answer him, I suppose, that it's the teaching kind, about {{{ 454a }}} how much odd and even there is; and we'll be able to show that all the other crafts we just mentioned are producers of persuasion too, and of what kind, and about what — isn't that so?

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

So rhetoric isn't the only producer of persuasion.

@ Gorgias

True.

@ Socrates

Since, then, it isn't the only thing that produces this effect, but other crafts do too, we would rightly ask the same further question we asked about the painter: rhetoric is a craft producing what kind of persuasion, and about what? Or don't you think it's {{{ 454b }}} right to ask that further?

@ Gorgias

I do.

@ Socrates

Answer, then, Gorgias, since that's how it seems to you too.

@ Gorgias

I mean that persuasion, Socrates — the kind used in courtrooms and other crowds, as I said just now — and it concerns what is just and unjust.

@ Socrates

I did suspect you meant that persuasion, Gorgias, and about those things; but so that you won't be surprised if I ask you something similar again shortly, something that seems {{{ 454c }}} obvious enough but which I nonetheless ask again — as I say, I ask it for the sake of carrying the argument through in order, not for your sake, but so that we don't get into the habit of guessing ahead and snatching the words out of each other's mouths; rather, you can carry through your own position however you like.

@ Gorgias

And I think you're right to do that, Socrates.

@ Socrates

Come, then, let's look at this too. Do you speak of having learned something?

@ Gorgias

I do.

@ Socrates

And what about this? Believing something?

@ Gorgias

{{{ 454d }}} I do.

@ Socrates

Do you think having learned and believing are the same thing, then, and learning and belief, or something different?

@ Gorgias

I think they're different myself, Socrates.

@ Socrates

You're right to think so; you'll see from this. If someone were to ask you: Gorgias, is there such a thing as a false belief and a true one? You'd say yes, I imagine.

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

And what about this? Is there a knowledge that's false and one that's true?

@ Gorgias

Not at all.

@ Socrates

So it's clear again that they're not the same thing.

@ Gorgias

True.

@ Socrates

{{{ 454e }}} But surely those who have learned have been convinced, and so have those who have merely come to believe.

@ Gorgias

That's so.

@ Socrates

Do you want us, then, to set down two kinds of persuasion — one that supplies belief without knowing, and one that supplies knowledge?

@ Gorgias

Certainly.

@ Socrates

So which kind of persuasion does rhetoric produce in courtrooms and other crowds, about what is just and unjust? The kind that gives rise to believing without knowing, or the kind that gives knowledge?

@ Gorgias

Clearly, Socrates, the kind that gives believing.

@ Socrates

So rhetoric, it seems, is a producer of {{{ 455a }}} persuasion that makes people believe, not one that teaches them, about the just and the unjust.

@ Gorgias

Yes.

@ Socrates

So the orator isn't a teacher of juries and the other crowds about what's just and unjust, only a producer of belief — since surely he couldn't teach a crowd that large such important matters in so short a time.

@ Gorgias

Not at all.

@ Socrates

Come, then, let's look at what we're actually saying about {{{ 455b }}} rhetoric — I myself still can't quite work out what I mean by it. When the city holds an assembly to choose doctors, or shipbuilders, or some other body of craftsmen, surely the orator won't be the one giving advice then? For clearly, in each such choice, it's the most skilled person who ought to be chosen. Nor when it's a matter of building walls, or constructing harbours or dockyards — there it's the engineers; nor again when the advice concerns choosing generals, or how to deploy against an enemy, or {{{ 455c }}} seizing ground — there it's the generals who will give the advice, and not the orators; or how do you account for such cases, Gorgias? Since you say yourself that you're an orator and that you make other men into orators, it's only right to find out the facts of your craft from you. And believe that right now I'm working for your interest as well as my own; because it may well be that one of the people here wants to become your student — I sense quite a few of them do, in fact — and they might well be too embarrassed to question you themselves. So when I question you, {{{ 455d }}} take it that they're questioning you too — Gorgias, what will we get out of it, if we study with you? On what matters will we be able to advise the city? Only about the just and the unjust, or about the other things Socrates just mentioned too? Try, then, to answer for their sake.

@ Gorgias

Well, I'll try to reveal the whole power of rhetoric to you clearly, Socrates — you yourself have led the way to it nicely. You surely know that these dockyards {{{ 455e }}} and the walls of Athens, and the construction of the harbours, came about through the advice of Themistocles, and some through that of Pericles — not through the craftsmen.

@ Socrates

That's what's said about Themistocles, Gorgias; and as for Pericles, I myself heard him when he advised us about the middle wall.
