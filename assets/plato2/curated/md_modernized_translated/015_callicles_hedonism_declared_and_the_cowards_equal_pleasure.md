## Hedonism Declared, and the Coward's Equal Pleasure

@ Callicles

How sweet you are — you call the moderate ones fools.

@ Socrates

How could it [not]? There's no one who wouldn't know that isn't what I mean.

@ Callicles

Yes, very much so, Socrates. How could a man ever be happy while he's a slave to anything? But this is what's fine and just by nature — I'll say it to you now, speaking frankly — that the man who's going to live rightly should let his own appetites grow as large as possible and not restrain them, {{{ 492a }}} and when they're as large as possible, be capable of serving them through courage and intelligence, and satisfy them with whatever he happens to desire at any time. But most people, I think, can't do this — that's why they criticize men like that out of shame, hiding their own inability, and call lack of restraint shameful, exactly what I said before, enslaving the men who are naturally superior; and because they themselves can't secure enough to fill their pleasures, they praise moderation {{{ 492b }}} and justice, out of their own lack of manhood. Since for those who from the start happened to be sons of kings, or were naturally capable of securing some rule or tyranny or domination for themselves, ⟨what⟩ could truly be more shameful and worse than moderation and justice for men like this — men who could enjoy good things with nothing standing in their way, and yet bring upon themselves as master the law and the talk and the blame of the common crowd? Or how would they not turn out wretched, {{{ 492c }}} brought low by the so-called fineness of justice and moderation, giving no more to their own friends than to their enemies — and that while ruling their own city? No, the truth, Socrates — the very thing you claim to be pursuing — is this: luxury and lack of restraint and freedom, when they have support behind them, that is virtue and happiness; everything else, all this finery, is just conventions men have agreed on, against nature, nonsense and worth nothing.

@ Socrates

{{{ 492d }}} That's no cowardly way to pursue the argument, Callicles, speaking as frankly as you do — you're plainly saying now what everyone else thinks but isn't willing to say. So I ask you not to let up in any way, so that it can really become clear how one ought to live. Now tell me: you say the appetites shouldn't be restrained, if a person is going to be what he should be, but that he should let them grow as large as possible and provide them fulfillment from wherever he can, {{{ 492e }}} and that this is virtue?

@ Callicles

Yes, that's what I say.

@ Socrates

Then it's not right to say that those who need nothing are happy.

@ Callicles

Because on that view stones and corpses would be the happiest of all.

@ Socrates

Well, on your account life is a strange thing indeed. I wouldn't be surprised if Euripides is telling the truth in these lines, where he says—
| Who knows if to live is really to die,
| and dying, to live …
{{{ 493a }}} And it may be that we really are dead — I have in fact heard from the wise that we are dead right now, that the body is a tomb for us, and that the part of the soul in which our appetites live happens to be the kind of thing that can be talked around and flipped upside down, and that some clever storyteller — a Sicilian, perhaps, or an Italian — twisting the name for the sake of the pun, called it, because it is so persuadable and so easily swayed, a jar, and called the mindless the uninitiated {{{ 493b }}} — and that in the mindless, this same part of the soul where the appetites are, its unrestrained and leaky part, he compared, on account of its greed, to a jar full of holes. This man, then, is showing you the opposite, Callicles — that among those in Hades, playing on the "unseen" in the name, the most wretched would be exactly these uninitiated ones, and that they would carry water into that leaky jar using another vessel just as full of holes, a sieve. The sieve, so the man who told me this {{{ 493c }}} said, stands for the soul — he likened the soul of the mindless to a sieve because it's full of holes, since it can't hold anything, thanks to its faithlessness and forgetfulness. This is all admittedly rather strange, but it shows what I want to point out to you, in case I can somehow persuade you to change your mind and choose, instead of the insatiable and unrestrained life, the orderly life, one content and satisfied with whatever is at hand each time. So, {{{ 493d }}} am I persuading you at all, and getting you to change your mind that the orderly are happier than the unrestrained, or would you not shift your position even if I told many more such tales?

@ Callicles

That's truer, Socrates.

@ Socrates

Come now, let me give you another image, from the same school as the one just now. Consider whether this is what you mean about each life, the moderate one and the unrestrained one: suppose two men each had many jars {{{ 493e }}} — one man's healthy and full, one of wine, one of honey, one of milk, and many others full of many things, but the streams feeding each of these were scarce and hard to come by, procured only with much painful effort; so this first man, once he'd filled his jars, would draw no more from them and give them no further thought, but would be at ease on their account; while the other man, though he too could get the streams, with just as much difficulty, had jars that were cracked and full of holes, and was forced to keep filling them, night and {{{ 494a }}} day, or else suffer the worst pains — given that each life is like this, do you say the unrestrained man's life is happier than the orderly man's? Am I persuading you at all by saying this, to agree that the orderly life is better than the unrestrained one, or am I not persuading you?

@ Callicles

You're not persuading me, Socrates. Because that man who's filled his jars has no more pleasure left at all — that's exactly what I was saying just now, living like a stone once {{{ 494b }}} he's filled up, feeling neither joy nor pain any longer. No, living pleasantly consists in having as much as possible flowing in.

@ Socrates

Then isn't it necessary, if a lot is flowing in, that a lot is also flowing out, and that the holes for the outflow are pretty big?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

So now you're describing the life of some stone-curlew, not a corpse's or a stone's. Now tell me: do you mean something like this — being hungry, and eating while hungry?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

{{{ 494c }}} And being thirsty, and drinking while thirsty?

@ Callicles

Yes, and having all the other appetites too, and being able to satisfy them, and enjoying it — that's living happily.

@ Socrates

Well said, my excellent fellow — keep going the way you started, and don't get embarrassed. And it seems I mustn't get embarrassed either. So first tell me: is it possible to live happily by scratching, if one is scabby and itchy and has plenty to scratch with, spending one's whole life scratching?

@ Callicles

{{{ 494d }}} What a strange man you are, Socrates, and simply a crowd-pleaser.

@ Socrates

That's exactly why, Callicles, I managed to shock Polus and Gorgias and make them embarrassed, but you won't be shocked or embarrassed, because you're a brave man. Just answer, that's all.

@ Callicles

Well then, I say that even the man who scratches would live pleasantly.

@ Socrates

Then if pleasantly, also happily?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

{{{ 494e }}} What if it's only his head he itches — or why do I even keep asking you this? Watch what you'll answer, Callicles, if someone asks you everything that follows from this, one thing after another. And, taking all such things to their conclusion, isn't the life of catamites terrible and shameful and wretched? Or will you dare to say that they're happy, as long as they have plenty of what they need?

@ Callicles

Aren't you ashamed, Socrates, to drag the discussion into things like this?

@ Socrates

Am I the one dragging it there, my noble friend, or is it whoever claims, without qualification, that those who feel pleasure — however {{{ 495a }}} they feel it — are happy, and refuses to distinguish which pleasures are good and which are bad? But even now, tell me: do you say the pleasant and the good are the same thing, or is there some pleasant thing that isn't good?

@ Callicles

So as not to contradict myself, in case I'd have to say they're different, I say they're the same.

@ Socrates

You're ruining our earlier agreements, Callicles, and you won't be able to examine the truth properly with me any longer, if you're going to speak against what you actually believe.

@ Callicles

{{{ 495b }}} So do you, Socrates.

@ Socrates

Well then, neither of us is doing right, if I'm doing that, nor are you. But, my good man, consider whether the good might not simply be feeling pleasure in every case — because many shameful things clearly follow from what was just hinted at, if that's really so, and many other absurdities besides.

@ Callicles

So you think, Socrates.

@ Socrates

But do you really maintain this, Callicles?

@ Callicles

I do.

@ Socrates

{{{ 495c }}} Then shall we take up the argument on the assumption that you're serious?

@ Callicles

Yes, very much so.

@ Socrates

Come then, since that's how it seems to you, distinguish these things for me — you do call something knowledge, don't you?

@ Callicles

I do.

@ Socrates

And didn't you just say a moment ago that there's such a thing as courage, along with knowledge?

@ Callicles

Yes, I did.

@ Socrates

So weren't you speaking of these as two different things, courage as distinct from knowledge?

@ Callicles

Very much so.

@ Socrates

What about this? Are pleasure and knowledge the same, or different?

@ Callicles

{{{ 495d }}} Different, surely, wisest of men.

@ Socrates

And is courage also different from pleasure?

@ Callicles

Of course.

@ Socrates

Come then, let's remember this: that Callicles of Acharnae said the pleasant and the good are the same, but knowledge and courage are different both from each other and from the good.

@ Callicles

And Socrates of Alopece doesn't agree with this. Or does he?

@ Socrates

{{{ 495e }}} He doesn't agree — and I don't think Callicles does either, once he looks at himself properly. Tell me: don't you think those who fare well have had the opposite experience from those who fare badly?

@ Callicles

I do.

@ Socrates

So if these are opposite to each other, must things stand with them the way they stand with health and disease? Because a person surely isn't healthy and sick at the same time, and doesn't get rid of health and disease at the same time either.

@ Callicles

What do you mean?

@ Socrates

Take, for instance, whatever part of the body you like and {{{ 496a }}} examine it. A person gets sick in the eyes, something called ophthalmia, right?

@ Callicles

Of course.

@ Socrates

He's surely not healthy in those same eyes at the same time, is he?

@ Callicles

Not in the least.

@ Socrates

But what about when he gets rid of the ophthalmia? Does he then also get rid of the health of his eyes, and end up rid of both at once?

@ Callicles

Not at all.

@ Socrates

That would be strange, I think, and unreasonable {{{ 496b }}} — wouldn't it?

@ Callicles

Very much so.

@ Socrates

So I think he gets and loses each of the two in turn?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

And the same goes for strength and weakness?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

And speed and slowness?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

And does he also get good things and happiness, and their opposites, bad things and misery, in turn, getting rid of each in turn as well?

@ Callicles

Surely, in every case.

@ Socrates

{{{ 496c }}} So if we find some things a person gets rid of and has at the same time, clearly those things can't be the good and the bad. Do we agree on this? Think it over carefully and answer.

@ Callicles

I agree, extraordinarily so.

@ Socrates

Let's go back to what we agreed on earlier. Did you say being hungry is pleasant or painful? I mean hunger itself.

@ Callicles

Painful, I'd say — though eating while hungry, I say, is pleasant {{{ 496d }}}.

@ Socrates

I understand — but hunger itself is painful, at any rate. Or isn't it?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

And thirst too?

@ Callicles

Very much.

@ Socrates

Shall I keep asking more of these, or do you agree that every lack and every appetite is painful?

@ Callicles

I agree — just stop asking.

@ Socrates

Very well — and do you say that drinking while thirsty is anything other than pleasant?

@ Callicles

I do.

@ Socrates

So in what you're describing, the thirsty part is surely in pain?

@ Callicles

{{{ 496e }}} Yes.

@ Socrates

And the drinking is a filling of the lack, and a pleasure?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

So you're saying the enjoyment is in the drinking?

@ Callicles

Exactly.

@ Socrates

While thirsty, that is.

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

While in pain?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

Do you see what follows, that you're saying he's enjoying himself while in pain at the same time, when you say he drinks while thirsty? Or doesn't this happen at the same place and the same time, whether you mean the soul or the body? Because I don't think it makes any difference. Is this so, or not?

@ Callicles

It is.

@ Socrates

But you say it's impossible to fare well and {{{ 497a }}} fare badly at the same time.

@ Callicles

Yes, I do.

@ Socrates

But you've agreed that it's possible to feel pleasure while in distress.

@ Callicles

So it seems.

@ Socrates

So feeling pleasure isn't the same as faring well, nor is being in distress the same as faring badly — which means the pleasant turns out to be different from the good.

@ Callicles

I don't know what sophistries you're up to, Socrates.

@ Socrates

You do know it, but you're playing innocent, Callicles — go on a bit further still [since you're knowingly talking nonsense], so you can see how wise {{{ 497b }}} you sound, lecturing me. Doesn't each of us stop being thirsty and start feeling pleasure through drinking, at the same time?

@ Callicles

I don't know what you mean.

@ Gorgias

Don't say that, Callicles — answer, if only for our sake, so the discussion can be brought to a conclusion.

@ Callicles

But Socrates is always like this, Gorgias — he keeps asking about petty, worthless things and refuting people over them.

@ Gorgias

But what difference does that make to you? It's not your reputation at stake here anyway, Callicles — so submit yourself to Socrates, and let him refute you however he wants.
