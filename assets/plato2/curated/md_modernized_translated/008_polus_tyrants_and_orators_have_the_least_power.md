## Tyrants and Orators Have the Least Power

@ Socrates

{{{ 466b }}} Is this a question you're asking, or are you stating the opening of some argument?

@ Polus

I'm asking, yes.

@ Socrates

It doesn't even seem to me that they're regarded as such.

@ Polus

What? Don't they have the greatest power in their cities?

@ Socrates

No — not if by "having power" you mean something good for the one who has it.

@ Polus

Well, that is what I mean.

@ Socrates

Then it seems to me that orators have the least power of anyone in the city.

@ Polus

What? Don't they, like tyrants, put to death {{{ 466c }}} anyone they want, and confiscate property and banish from their cities anyone they see fit?

@ Socrates

By the dog, Polus, I honestly can't tell, with each of these claims of yours, whether you're stating your own opinion, or asking me a question.

@ Polus

Well, I'm asking you.

@ Socrates

Very well, my friend — but then are you asking me two things at once?

@ Polus

How, two things?

@ Socrates

Didn't you just say something like this — that orators put to death {{{ 466d }}} anyone they want, just like tyrants, and confiscate property and drive out of their cities anyone they see fit?

@ Polus

I did.

@ Socrates

Well, I tell you these are two questions, and I'll answer you on both. Because I say, Polus, that both orators and tyrants have the least power of anyone in their cities, just as I was saying a moment ago — since they don't do, so to speak, anything {{{ 466e }}} they want, but only what seems best to them.

@ Polus

Well then, isn't that having great power?

@ Socrates

No — not according to Polus.

@ Polus

I don't say that? I certainly do.

@ Socrates

By the— no, not you, since you said having great power was a good thing for the one who has it.

@ Polus

Yes, I did say so.

@ Socrates

So you think it's a good thing, if someone does what seems best to him without having any sense? And you call that having great power?

@ Polus

No, I don't.

@ Socrates

Then will you prove that orators have sense, and that rhetoric {{{ 467a }}} is a craft and not flattery, by refuting me? But if you leave me unrefuted, the orators who do what seems best to them in their cities, and the tyrants too, will have gained nothing good by it — while power, as you say, is a good thing, and doing what seems best without sense you yourself agree is a bad thing — or don't you?

@ Polus

I do.

@ Socrates

So how could orators or tyrants have great power in their cities, unless Polus refutes Socrates and shows that they do what they want?

@ Polus

{{{ 467b }}} This man—

@ Socrates

I say they don't do what they want — so refute me.

@ Polus

Didn't you just agree, [earlier than this], that they do what seems best to them?

@ Socrates

Yes, and I still agree now.

@ Polus

Then don't they do what they want?

@ Socrates

I say they don't.

@ Polus

While doing what seems best to them?

@ Socrates

I do say that.

@ Polus

That's outrageous and absurd, Socrates.

@ Socrates

Don't speak ill of me, my excellent Polus {{{ 467c }}} — to greet you in your own style — but if you're in a position to question me, show that I'm lying; if not, answer the questions yourself.

@ Polus

Well, I'm willing to answer, so I can find out what you mean.

@ Socrates

Do you think people want the very thing they're doing at any given moment, or that other thing for the sake of which they do what they do? For instance, those who drink medicine prescribed by doctors — do you think they want the very thing they're doing, drinking the medicine and being in pain, or that other thing, being healthy, for the sake of which they drink it?

@ Polus

Clearly, being {{{ 467d }}} healthy.

@ Socrates

In the same way, people who sail the sea and carry on any other kind of business don't want the very thing they're doing at each moment — after all, who wants to sail, run risks, and have troubles — but rather, I think, the thing for the sake of which they sail: growing rich, since it's for wealth's sake that they sail.

@ Polus

Certainly.

@ Socrates

So isn't it the same way with everything? If someone does something for the sake of something else, doesn't he want not the thing he's doing, but that other thing {{{ 467e }}} for the sake of which he's doing it?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

Now, is there anything among existing things that isn't either good, or bad, or in between the two — neither good nor bad?

@ Polus

That must be so, Socrates.

@ Socrates

And you'd say that wisdom, health, wealth, and other such things are good, and their opposites are bad?

@ Polus

I would.

@ Socrates

And by things that are neither good nor bad, do you mean things that sometimes share {{{ 468a }}} in the good, sometimes in the bad, and sometimes in neither — for instance, sitting, walking, running, and sailing, and again, stones and sticks and other such things? Isn't that what you mean? Or do you call something else neither good nor bad?

@ Polus

No, those are what I mean.

@ Socrates

Now, when people do these in-between things, do they do them for the sake of the good things, or the good things for the sake of the in-between ones?

@ Polus

The in-between things, {{{ 468b }}} surely, for the sake of the good ones.

@ Socrates

So it's in pursuit of the good that we walk, when we walk, thinking it's better to do so, and in pursuit of the same thing, the good, that we stand still, when we stand — or isn't that so?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

And so too, if we put someone to death, or banish someone, or confiscate property, we do it thinking it's better for us to do these things than not?

@ Polus

Certainly.

@ Socrates

So it's for the sake of the good that people do all these things, when they do them.

@ Polus

I agree.

@ Socrates

And didn't we agree that whatever we do for the sake of something else, we don't want the things themselves, {{{ 468c }}} but that other thing for the sake of which we do them?

@ Polus

Absolutely.

@ Socrates

So we don't want to slaughter people, or banish them from their cities, or confiscate property, simply as such — rather, if these things are beneficial, we want to do them, but if they're harmful, we don't want to. For we want the good things, as you say, and we don't want the things that are neither good nor bad, nor the bad things either. Isn't that so? Do you think I'm telling the truth, Polus, or not? Why don't you answer?

@ Polus

The truth.

@ Socrates

{{{ 468d }}} Then if we agree on this, whenever someone puts a person to death, or banishes him from the city, or confiscates his property, whether he's a tyrant or an orator, thinking it's better for himself, though it actually turns out worse, that man, surely, is doing what seems best to him — isn't that so?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

Then is he also doing what he wants, if these things really turn out to be bad? Why don't you answer?

@ Polus

Well, I don't think he's doing what he wants.

@ Socrates

Then is there any way such a man {{{ 468e }}} has great power in that city, if having great power is something good, by your own admission?

@ Polus

There isn't.

@ Socrates

So what I was saying is true — that it's possible for a man doing what seems best to him in a city neither to have great power nor to do what he wants.

@ Polus

As if you, Socrates, wouldn't accept being free to do whatever seems good to you in the city rather than not, and as if you don't envy someone when you see him put to death whoever he pleased, or confiscate property, or throw someone in prison.

@ Socrates

Justly, or unjustly?

@ Polus

{{{ 469a }}} Whichever way he does it, isn't it enviable either way?

@ Socrates

Mind your words, Polus.

@ Polus

Why?

@ Socrates

Because you shouldn't envy people who aren't enviable, or the wretched — you should pity them.

@ Polus

What? Is that how you think it is with the men I'm talking about?

@ Socrates

Of course it is.

@ Polus

So a man who puts to death whomever he pleases, justly putting him to death, seems to you wretched and pitiable?

@ Socrates

Not to me — but not enviable either.

@ Polus

Didn't you just say he was wretched?

@ Socrates

{{{ 469b }}} The one who kills unjustly, my friend, is wretched, and pitiable besides — the one who kills justly, merely not enviable.

@ Polus

Surely the man who dies unjustly is pitiable and wretched.

@ Socrates

Less so than the one who kills him, Polus, and less so than the one who dies justly.

@ Polus

How can that be, Socrates?

@ Socrates

Like this — doing wrong happens to be the greatest of evils.

@ Polus

Is that really the greatest? Isn't suffering wrong greater?

@ Socrates

Not in the least.

@ Polus

So you would rather suffer wrong than do it.
