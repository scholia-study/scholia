## What a Soul-Improving Statesman Would Look Like

@ Socrates

{{{ 501d }}} Now does this hold for just one soul, but not for two or many?

@ Callicles

No, it holds for two and for many as well.

@ Socrates

So it's possible to gratify a whole group at once, without any regard for what's best?

@ Callicles

I think so.

@ Socrates

Can you tell me, then, what practices do this? Or rather, if you like, as I ask about each one, say yes if it seems to you to be one of these, and no if it doesn't. {{{ 501e }}} Let's first consider flute-playing. Doesn't it seem to you to be the sort of thing, Callicles, that pursues only our pleasure and cares about nothing else?

@ Callicles

It seems so to me.

@ Socrates

And all the similar ones too, like competitive lyre-playing?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

What about the training of choruses, and the composing of dithyrambs? Doesn't it strike you as the same kind of thing? Or do you think Cinesias son of Meles gives any thought to saying something that would make his listeners better, rather than something likely {{{ 502a }}} to gratify the crowd of spectators?

@ Callicles

That much is clear, Socrates, at least about Cinesias.

@ Socrates

And what about his father, Meles? Did he seem to you to sing to the lyre with an eye toward what's best? Or didn't even he aim at what's most pleasant? Because his singing annoyed the audience. But consider — doesn't the whole art of singing to the lyre, and the composing of dithyrambs, seem to you to have been invented for the sake of pleasure?

@ Callicles

So it seems to me.

@ Socrates

{{{ 502b }}} And what about this solemn and wondrous thing, the composing of tragedy — what is it in earnest about? Is its whole undertaking and effort, as it seems to you, just to gratify the spectators, or does it also fight against this, so that if something is pleasant and gratifying to them but bad, it won't say that, and if something happens to be unpleasant but beneficial, it will say and sing that instead, whether they enjoy it or not? Which way does the composing of tragedies seem to you to be arranged?

@ Callicles

That much is clear, Socrates — it's aimed more at {{{ 502c }}} pleasure, and at gratifying the spectators.

@ Socrates

And didn't we just say, Callicles, that this sort of thing is flattery?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

Now, if someone stripped away the melody, the rhythm, and the meter from all poetry, wouldn't what's left just be speeches?

@ Callicles

Necessarily.

@ Socrates

And aren't these speeches delivered before a great crowd, the people?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

So poetry turns out to be a kind of public speaking to the crowd.

@ Callicles

{{{ 502d }}} So it appears.

@ Socrates

So it would be a rhetorical kind of public speaking — or don't the poets seem to you to be practicing rhetoric in the theaters?

@ Callicles

So it seems to me.

@ Socrates

So now we've discovered a kind of rhetoric addressed to a crowd made up of children and women and men together, and slaves and free men alike — one we don't much admire, since we say it's a flattering kind.

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

Very well — but what about the rhetoric addressed to the Athenian people {{{ 502e }}} and the other free citizens in other cities — what is this rhetoric, for us? Do the orators seem to you always to speak with an eye to what's best, aiming at making the citizens as good as possible through their speeches, or are they too driven toward gratifying the citizens, neglecting the common good for the sake of their own private interest, treating the people like children, trying only to please them, and giving no thought at all to whether {{{ 503a }}} this makes them better or worse?

@ Callicles

That's no longer a simple question you're asking — some of them say what they say out of genuine care for the citizens, while others are just as you describe.

@ Socrates

That's enough. Because if this too is twofold, then one part of it would be flattery and shameful public speaking, while the other would be fine — the part that works to make the citizens' souls as good as possible, and fights hard, saying what's best, whether it turns out more pleasant or less pleasant to the listeners. {{{ 503b }}} But you've never once seen this kind of rhetoric — or if you can name one of the orators who's like that, why haven't you told me who he is?

@ Callicles

By Zeus, I can't name a single one of the current orators for you.

@ Socrates

What about this? Among the men of old, can you name one on whose account the Athenians became better, when before his public speaking began they were worse? Because I myself don't know who this man could be.

@ Callicles

{{{ 503c }}} What about this? Haven't you heard that Themistocles became a good man, and Cimon, and Miltiades, and this Pericles here who died recently, whom you yourself have heard speak?

@ Socrates

If, Callicles, the virtue you spoke of earlier is the true one — satisfying one's own appetites and other people's — well and good; but if it isn't that, and instead it's what we were forced to agree to later on, that of the appetites, the ones that make a person better when filled {{{ 503d }}} should be satisfied, and the ones that make him worse should not, and that this takes some skill — then I myself have no way to say that any of these men [became] such a man.

@ Callicles

But if you look for one properly, you'll find one.

@ Socrates

Let's look at this quietly, examining whether any of these men actually became like that — because, consider: the good man, the one who speaks with a view to what's best, won't he say whatever he says not at random, but {{{ 503e }}} with his eye on something? Just as all the other craftsmen too, each one, don't select and apply things to their work at random, but so that the thing they're making takes on some definite form. For instance, if you want to look at painters, builders, shipwrights, all the other craftsmen, any one you like — see how each one places whatever he places into some arrangement, and forces one part to be fitting and to harmonize with another, until the {{{ 504a }}} whole stands together as an ordered and well-arranged thing; and the other craftsmen too, and the ones we were just talking about, those concerned with the body, trainers and doctors, they too, I take it, bring order and arrangement to the body. Do we agree this is so, or not?

@ Callicles

Let it be so.

@ Socrates

So a house that has arrangement and order would be a good one, and one with disorder a bad one?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

And a ship, the same way?

@ Callicles

{{{ 504b }}} Yes.

@ Socrates

And surely we say the same about our bodies too?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

And what about the soul? Will it be good if it has disorder, or if it has some arrangement and order?

@ Callicles

Given what we agreed before, I have to grant this too.

@ Socrates

So what's the name for the thing that arises in the body from arrangement and order?

@ Callicles

I suppose you mean health and strength.

@ Socrates

{{{ 504c }}} I do. And what about the thing that arises in the soul from arrangement and order? Try to find and give its name, just as you did for the body.

@ Callicles

Why don't you just say it yourself, Socrates?

@ Socrates

Well, if you'd rather I did, I'll say it — and you, if you think I'm putting it well, say so, and if not, refute me, don't just let it pass. Because it seems to me the name for the body's arrangements is "healthy," the thing from which health arises in it, along with the body's other excellence. Is this so, or not?

@ Callicles

It is.

@ Socrates

{{{ 504d }}} And for the soul's arrangements and orderings, the name is "lawful" and "law" — the source from which people become law-abiding and orderly; and these are justice and moderation. Do you say so, or not?

@ Callicles

Let it be so.

@ Socrates

So, keeping his eye on these things, that orator — the skilled and good one — will apply to people's souls whatever speeches he gives, and all his actions besides, and if he gives some gift, he'll give it, and if he takes something away, he'll take it away, always with his mind fixed on this: how justice might arise in his fellow citizens' {{{ 504e }}} souls and injustice be driven out, how moderation might come to be there and lack of restraint be driven out, and how their other virtue might arise and vice depart. Do you agree, or not?

@ Callicles

I agree.

@ Socrates

Because what good is it, Callicles, to give a body that's sick and in bad condition plenty of food, even the most pleasant kind, or drink, or anything else, if it won't do it any more good — and, by a fair reckoning, even less good than the opposite would? Isn't that so?

@ Callicles

{{{ 505a }}} Let it be so.

@ Socrates

Because I don't think it profits a person to live with a body in bad condition — since that means living badly, of necessity. Or isn't that so?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

So don't doctors, as a rule, allow a healthy person to satisfy his appetites, letting him eat as much as he wants when hungry, or drink when thirsty, while a sick person, one might say, they never allow to fill himself with what he desires? Do you agree with this too?

@ Callicles

I do.

@ Socrates

{{{ 505b }}} And isn't it the same way with the soul, my excellent friend? As long as it's in bad condition — being mindless, unrestrained, unjust, and unholy — it must be held back from its appetites, and not permitted to do anything except what will make it better; do you say so, or not?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

Because that's better for the soul, isn't it?

@ Callicles

Certainly.

@ Socrates

And isn't holding it back from what it desires a matter of correcting it?

@ Callicles

Yes.

@ Socrates

So being corrected is better for the soul than lacking restraint, just as you thought a moment ago.

@ Callicles

{{{ 505c }}} I don't know what you're talking about, Socrates — ask somebody else.

@ Socrates

This man can't stand being helped, or undergoing the very thing our discussion is about — being corrected.

@ Callicles

And I don't care in the least about what you're saying — and I only answered you this far as a favor to Gorgias.

@ Socrates

Very well — so what shall we do, then? Shall we break off the discussion in the middle?

@ Callicles

You'll have to decide that yourself.

@ Socrates

But they say it isn't even permitted to leave stories unfinished in the middle {{{ 505d }}} — you have to put a head on them, so they don't go around headless. So answer the rest too, so our discussion can get a head.

@ Callicles

What a bully you are, Socrates. If you'll listen to me, you'll let this discussion go, or else find someone else to argue with.

@ Socrates

Well, who else is willing? Because we really shouldn't leave the discussion unfinished.

@ Callicles

Couldn't you get through the discussion yourself, either talking it out on your own or answering your own questions?
