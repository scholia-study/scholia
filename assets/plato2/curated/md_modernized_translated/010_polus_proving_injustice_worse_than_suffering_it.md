## Proving Injustice Worse Than Suffering It

@ Socrates

{{{ 472e }}} But if the wrongdoer doesn't pay the penalty, will he be happy, on your account?

@ Polus

I do.

@ Socrates

But in my opinion, Polus, the wrongdoer — the unjust man — is wretched in every case, but more wretched if he doesn't pay the penalty and doesn't meet with punishment for his wrongdoing, and less wretched if he does pay the penalty and gets his due from gods and men.

@ Polus

{{{ 473a }}} That's a strange thing to try to say, Socrates.

@ Socrates

I'll try to make you say the same as I do, my friend; after all, I consider you a friend. Right now, this is what we disagree about — you take a look too. I said earlier that doing wrong is worse than suffering it.

@ Polus

Certainly.

@ Socrates

Whereas you say suffering it is.

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

And I said that wrongdoers are wretched, and you refuted me.

@ Polus

Yes, by Zeus.

@ Socrates

{{{ 473b }}} So you think, Polus.

@ Polus

And I think truly.

@ Socrates

Perhaps. And you, in turn, say wrongdoers are happy, if they don't pay the penalty.

@ Polus

Certainly.

@ Socrates

But I say they're the most wretched, and those who pay the penalty less so. Do you want to refute this too?

@ Polus

But that's even harder to refute than the last one, Socrates.

@ Socrates

Not at all, Polus — impossible, in fact; the truth is never refuted.

@ Polus

What do you mean? If a man doing wrong is caught plotting {{{ 473c }}} to set himself up as tyrant, and once caught is racked and mutilated and has his eyes burned out, and after suffering many other great torments of every kind himself, and watching his own children and wife suffer them too, is finally crucified or coated in pitch and burned — will this man be happier than if he escapes, sets himself up as tyrant, and lives out his life as ruler in the city doing whatever he wants, envied and counted happy by the {{{ 473d }}} citizens and by other foreigners too? Is that what you're saying is impossible to refute?

@ Socrates

You're just trying to scare me again, noble Polus, and that's not refuting — a moment ago you were just calling witnesses. Still, remind me of one small thing. Did you say plotting unjustly to become tyrant?

@ Polus

I did.

@ Socrates

Well then, neither of them will ever be happier than the other — neither the one who's gotten hold of the tyranny unjustly nor the one who pays the penalty — since, between two wretched men, {{{ 473e }}} neither could be happier — but the one who escapes and becomes tyrant is the more wretched of the two. What's this, Polus? Are you laughing? Is this some other kind of refutation, laughing whenever someone says something, instead of refuting it?

@ Polus

Don't you think you've been refuted, Socrates, when you say things no human being would agree to? Go ahead, ask any of these men here.

@ Socrates

Polus, I'm not one of the politicians — last year, when I was chosen by lot to sit on the council, and my tribe held the presidency and I had to put a matter to the vote, {{{ 474a }}} I made a fool of myself, because I didn't know how to conduct a vote. So don't tell me now either to put these men here to the vote, but if you have no better refutation than that, hand the argument over to me in my turn, as I was just saying, and try the kind of refutation I think is needed. For when I make a claim, I know how to produce just one witness — the very man I'm arguing with — and I let the majority go hang, and I know how to put it to the vote of just one man, but with {{{ 474b }}} the majority I don't even hold a discussion. So see whether you're willing, in your turn, to submit to a refutation by answering what you're asked. For I do think that I myself, and you, and everyone else, believe that doing wrong is worse than suffering it, and not paying the penalty worse than paying it.

@ Polus

But I don't think that — not about myself, and not about anyone else. Would you actually choose to suffer wrong rather than do it?

@ Socrates

So would you, and so would everyone else.

@ Polus

Far from it — neither I nor you nor anyone else would.

@ Socrates

{{{ 474c }}} Then won't you answer?

@ Polus

Certainly; in fact I'm eager to hear what you'll say.

@ Socrates

So tell me, so you'll know, just as if I were asking you from the beginning — which do you think is worse, Polus, doing wrong or suffering it?

@ Polus

Suffering it, in my view.

@ Socrates

And what about this? Which is more shameful, doing wrong or suffering it? Answer.

@ Polus

Doing wrong.

@ Socrates

Then it's also worse, if it's more shameful.

@ Polus

Not in the least.

@ Socrates

I see; apparently you don't think {{{ 474d }}} fine and good are the same thing, or bad and shameful.

@ Polus

No, I don't.

@ Socrates

And what about this? Do you call all fine things fine — bodies, colors, shapes, sounds, and practices — every time, without looking to anything? Take fine bodies first — don't you call them fine either in view of their use, whatever each is useful for, or in view of some pleasure, if they make the people looking at them enjoy the sight? Can you name anything about physical beauty outside these two?

@ Polus

{{{ 474e }}} I can't.

@ Socrates

Then don't you call everything else fine the same way too — shapes and colors — either because of some pleasure, or because of benefit, or both?

@ Polus

I do.

@ Socrates

And sounds too, and everything in music, the same way?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

And surely fine things in the realm of laws and practices are no exception either — they're fine by being beneficial or pleasant or both.

@ Polus

{{{ 475a }}} No, they aren't.

@ Socrates

Then the beauty of studies is the same way too?

@ Polus

Certainly; and you're defining it well now, Socrates, defining the fine by pleasure and good.

@ Socrates

Then the shameful is the opposite — by pain and bad?

@ Polus

It must be.

@ Socrates

So whenever, of two fine things, one is finer than the other, it's finer by exceeding in one of these two, or in both — either in pleasure or in benefit or in both.

@ Polus

Certainly.

@ Socrates

And when, of two shameful things, {{{ 475b }}} one is more shameful than the other, it will be more shameful by exceeding in pain or in bad — or isn't that necessary?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

Come now, what did we just say about doing wrong and suffering it? Didn't you say that suffering wrong is worse, and doing wrong more shameful?

@ Polus

I did.

@ Socrates

So if doing wrong is more shameful than suffering it, then it must either be more painful — more shameful by exceeding in pain — or in bad, or in both? Isn't that necessary too?

@ Polus

How could it not be?

@ Socrates

First, then, {{{ 475c }}} let's consider whether doing wrong exceeds suffering it in pain, and whether wrongdoers feel more pain than those who are wronged.

@ Polus

Not at all, Socrates, not that.

@ Socrates

Then it doesn't exceed it in pain.

@ Polus

No, it doesn't.

@ Socrates

Then if not in pain, it can't exceed in both any longer.

@ Polus

It doesn't appear so.

@ Socrates

So it's left with the other.

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

In bad.

@ Polus

So it seems.

@ Socrates

Then, exceeding in bad, doing wrong would be worse than suffering it.

@ Polus

Clearly so.

@ Socrates

{{{ 475d }}} Now wasn't it agreed by most people, and by you, earlier on, that doing wrong is more shameful than suffering it?

@ Polus

Yes.

@ Socrates

But now it's turned out to be worse.

@ Polus

So it seems.

@ Socrates

So would you actually choose the worse and more shameful thing over the lesser? Don't hesitate to answer, Polus; you won't be harmed at all; just answer bravely, submitting to the argument the way you'd submit to a doctor, and either {{{ 475e }}} say yes or no to what I ask.

@ Polus

No, I wouldn't choose it, Socrates.

@ Socrates

Would anyone else?

@ Polus

Not, I think, on this argument.

@ Socrates

So I was telling the truth, that neither I, nor you, nor anyone else on earth would choose to do wrong rather than suffer it; because it happens to be worse.

@ Polus

So it appears.

@ Socrates

So you see, Polus, when one refutation is set beside the other, they're nothing alike — everyone else agrees with you except me, but you alone are enough for me, {{{ 476a }}} agreeing and testifying by yourself, and I put it to the vote of you alone and let the rest go hang. Let's leave that as settled between us, then; and next let's consider the second point we disputed — whether the wrongdoer's paying the penalty is the greatest of evils, as you thought, or whether not paying it is greater, as I, in turn, thought.

Let's look at it this way; do you call paying the penalty and being justly corrected for doing wrong the same thing?

@ Polus

I do.
