## The Argument's Bonds of Iron and Adamant

@ Socrates

{{{ 505e }}} So Epicharmus's saying may come true for me — what two men used to say before, I, one man, may prove able to say. But it looks like this really is the most necessary thing. If we're actually going to do this, though, I think all of us need to be eager to find out what's true and what's false in what we're saying; it's a good shared by everyone alike once it comes out into the open. {{{ 506a }}} So I'll go through the argument the way it seems to me to stand; and if any of you thinks I'm not agreeing with myself about how things really are, you should take hold of it and refute me. I'm not saying what I say because I know it, but because I'm searching together with all of you, so that if the person disputing with me turns out to be saying something worthwhile, I'll be the first to agree. Still, I'm saying all this on the assumption that we ought to carry the argument through to the end; but if you don't want to, let's drop it right now and go.

@ Gorgias

But it doesn't seem to me yet time to leave, Socrates {{{ 506b }}} — you should go through the argument to the end; it looks to me like the others think so too. I myself, for one, want to hear you go through what's left yourself.

@ Socrates

But truly, Gorgias, I myself would happily go on talking with our friend Callicles here, until I paid him back Amphion's speech for Zethus's; but since you, Callicles, aren't willing to help me see the argument through, at least listen to me and step in if you think I'm not speaking well {{{ 506c }}}. And if you refute me, I won't be annoyed with you the way you were with me — instead you'll be registered as my greatest benefactor.

@ Callicles

Go on, my good man, speak for yourself and finish it.

@ Socrates

Listen, then, while I take up the argument again from the beginning. Is the pleasant the same as the good?—Not the same, as Callicles and I agreed.—Now, which is it: do we choose the pleasant because of the good, or the good because of the pleasant?—The pleasant, because of the good.—And the pleasant is that whose presence {{{ 506d }}} gives us pleasure, and the good is that whose presence makes us good?—Certainly.—And we, and everything else that's good, are good once some excellence is present in us?—That seems necessary to me, Callicles.—But surely the excellence of each thing — a tool, a body, a soul again, any living creature — comes to it at its finest not by chance, but through arrangement, correctness, and whatever craft has been assigned to each; is that so?—Yes, I say so.—{{{ 506e }}} So the excellence of each thing is arranged by arrangement and given order?—I'd say so.—So it's some order, arising in each thing and proper to it, that makes each existing thing good?—So it seems to me.—And a soul that has its own order is better than one without order?—Necessarily.—And surely one that has order is orderly?—How could it not be?—And the orderly one is moderate {{{ 507a }}}?—Quite necessarily.—So the moderate soul is good. I have nothing else to say against this, my dear Callicles; but if you do, teach me.

@ Callicles

Speak, my good man.

@ Socrates

I say this: if the moderate soul is good, then the one in the opposite condition to the moderate is bad; and that one was the foolish and unrestrained soul.—Certainly.—And surely the moderate man would do what's fitting, both toward gods and toward men; for he wouldn't be moderate if he did what's not fitting?—{{{ 507b }}} That must be so.—And doing what's fitting toward men, he'd be doing just things, and toward gods, pious things; and a man who does just and pious things must be just and pious.—That's so.—And he must be brave too; for it's not the mark of a moderate man to chase or avoid what he shouldn't, but to avoid and pursue whatever things, people, pleasures, and pains he ought to, and to hold his ground and endure where he must; so it's altogether necessary {{{ 507c }}}, Callicles, that the moderate man, as we've gone through, being just and brave and pious, is a completely good man; and that the good man does well and finely whatever he does; and that the one who does well is blessed and happy, while the wicked one, who does badly, is wretched; and this last would be the man in the condition opposite to the moderate — the unrestrained man, whom you were praising.

So I hold to all this, and I say it's true; and if it's true, then anyone who wants, it seems, to be happy {{{ 507d }}} must pursue and practice moderation, and flee licentiousness as fast as his feet will carry him, and above all arrange things so he never needs correcting, but if he does need it — himself, or anyone close to him, whether a private person or a city — then punishment must be imposed and correction applied, if he's going to be happy. This seems to me to be the goal a person should keep in view and live by, directing everything he does — his own affairs and the city's — toward making sure justice and moderation {{{ 507e }}} will be present in the man who's going to be happy, acting that way instead of letting his desires run unrestrained and trying to fill them up, an endless evil, living a bandit's life. A man like that couldn't be dear to anyone else, human or god; he's incapable of community, and where there's no community, there can be no friendship. The wise say, Callicles, that heaven and {{{ 508a }}} earth, gods and men, are all held together by community, friendship, orderliness, moderation, and justice — and that's why they call the whole of it order, my friend, not disorder or licentiousness. But you seem to me to pay no attention to any of this, clever as you are, and it has escaped you that geometric equality has great power among both gods and men, while you think one should practice getting more; that's because you neglect geometry. Well then; either this argument of ours must be refuted {{{ 508b }}} — that it's not by possessing justice and moderation that the happy are happy, and by possessing vice that the wretched are wretched — or, if it's true, we need to look at what follows from it. And all those earlier things follow, Callicles — the ones you asked me about, whether I was serious when I said that a person ought to accuse himself, his son, or his companion, if he does any wrong, and that rhetoric should be used for this purpose; and what you thought Polus admitted only out of shame turns out to be true after all — that doing wrong is worse than suffering it by exactly as much as it's more shameful {{{ 508c }}}; and that the man who's going to be a genuine orator must be just and knowledgeable about just things, which Polus in turn said Gorgias admitted only out of shame.

Since that's how things stand, let's consider what it actually is you're reproaching me with — whether it's well said or not — that I'm not capable of helping myself or any of my friends or family, or saving them from the greatest dangers, and that I'm at the mercy of anyone who wants, the way disgraced men are at the mercy of anyone who's willing {{{ 508d }}} — whether he wants to hit me across the face, that vigorous phrase of yours, or take my money, or drive me out of the city, or, worst of all, kill me; and that being in such a position is the most shameful thing there is, according to your argument. But my own position, whatever it is, has already been stated many times, and nothing stops me from stating it again now; I say, Callicles, that being struck unjustly across the face isn't the most shameful thing, and neither is having my body cut, or my purse {{{ 508e }}} — but striking me and mine unjustly, and cutting us, is more shameful and worse, and so is stealing, enslaving, breaking into my house, and in a word doing any wrong at all to me and mine — for the one doing the wrong, it's worse and more shameful than it is for me, the one wronged. These things, having appeared to us this way, up there in our earlier discussions, are held fast and bound, as I put it, and {{{ 509a }}} — if I may put it rather crudely — bound with bonds of iron and adamant, or so it would seem, at any rate; bonds which, unless you or someone even more forceful than you break them, no one can say anything other than what I'm now saying and still speak well; since, for my part, my position is always the same — that I myself don't know how these things stand, but that of everyone I've ever met, as now, no one can say anything different without being ridiculous. So I, for my part, maintain once again {{{ 509b }}} that this is how things stand; and if this is so, and doing wrong is the greatest of evils for the one doing it, and greater still than this greatest evil — if such a thing is possible — is for the wrongdoer to escape paying the penalty, then what kind of help, if a person can't provide it for himself, would make him truly ridiculous? Isn't it the help that would turn aside the greatest harm to us? It must surely be the most shameful thing, then, not to be able to provide that help either for oneself or for one's own friends and family, and the next most shameful {{{ 509c }}} not to be able to help against the second-worst evil, and the third most shameful against the third, and so on down the line; the greater the natural size of each evil, the finer it is to be able to help against it, and the more shameful not to be able to. Or does it stand some other way than this, Callicles?

@ Callicles

Not some other way.

@ Socrates

So, there being two things, doing wrong and suffering wrong, we say doing wrong is the greater evil, and suffering wrong the lesser. What, then, would a person need to acquire in order to help himself {{{ 509d }}} and get both these benefits — the one that comes from not doing wrong, and the one that comes from not suffering wrong? Power, or wishing? Here's what I mean: if he simply doesn't wish to suffer wrong, will he not suffer it, or does he need to acquire the power not to suffer wrong in order not to suffer it?

@ Callicles

That much is clear — if he has the power.

@ Socrates

And what about doing wrong? Is it enough for him simply not to wish to do wrong — since then he won't do it — or does he also need to acquire some power and craft for this {{{ 509e }}}, so that if he doesn't learn and practice it, he'll do wrong? Why don't you just answer me this, Callicles: do you think Polus and I were right to be forced into agreeing, earlier in the discussion, or not, when we agreed that no one does wrong willingly, and that everyone who does wrong does it unwillingly?
