## The Virtues in the Soul, and Justice as Health

Quite so, he said; you are right.

So then, I said, we have swum through this much with difficulty, and it has been fairly agreed between us that the same kinds are present both in the city and in the soul of each individual, and equal in number.

That is so.

Then is it not already necessary that, just as the city was wise, and by virtue of what, so the individual too is wise, and by virtue of the same thing?

What else? {{{ 441d }}}

And that by which, and the way in which, a private individual is courageous — is it not by that, and in that way, that a city too is courageous, and are not both of them alike in all other respects concerning virtue?

Necessarily.

And a man too, Glaucon, we shall say, I think, is just in the very same way in which the city was just.

And this too follows of complete necessity.

But surely we have not forgotten this — that the city was just because each of its three kinds was doing its own work within it.

I do not think we have forgotten it, he said.

Then we must remember that each of us too, {{{ 441e }}} whoever it is in whom each of the parts within him does its own work, will be just, and will be doing his own.

Yes indeed, he said, we must remember it.

Then does it not belong to the rational part to rule, since it is wise and has forethought on behalf of the whole soul, and to the spirited part to be its subject and ally?

Certainly.

Then is it not, as we were saying, a blending of music and gymnastic that will make them concordant, tightening and {{{ 442a }}} nourishing the one with fine words and studies, and relaxing the other, soothing and taming it with harmony and rhythm?

Quite so, he said.

And these two, thus reared, and having truly learned and been trained in their own tasks, will take charge of the appetitive part — which is indeed the largest part of the soul in each of us, and by nature the most insatiable for money — and will watch over it, lest, by being filled with the so-called pleasures of the body and becoming great and strong, {{{ 442b }}} it should no longer do its own work, but should attempt to enslave and rule over the parts to which it does not belong by its kind, and should overturn the whole life of them all.

Certainly, he said.

Then would not these two, I said, also guard best of all against enemies from outside, on behalf of the whole soul and body — the one deliberating, the other fighting in front, [and] following its ruler and, by its courage, carrying out what has been deliberated?

That is so.

And it is by this part, I think, that we call each and {{{ 442c }}} every one of us courageous, whenever his spirited part preserves, through pains and through pleasures, what has been declared by reason to be fearful or not.

Rightly so, he said.

And he is wise, in turn, by that small part which ruled within him and issued these declarations, since it too has within itself the knowledge of what is beneficial for each part and for the whole community formed by all three together.

Certainly.

What then? Is he not moderate by the friendship and concord of these same parts, whenever the ruling part and the two ruled parts {{{ 442d }}} agree that the rational part ought to rule, and do not raise faction against it?

Moderation, he said, is indeed nothing other than this, both for the city and for the individual.

But surely a man will also be just by that very thing, which we have so often mentioned, and in that same way.

It is quite necessary.

What then? I said — does it grow at all blurred for us, so that justice seems to be something other than what it appeared to be in the city?

It does not seem so to me, he said.

For in this way, I said, we could fully confirm {{{ 442e }}} the point, if anything within our soul still has doubts, by bringing forward commonplace examples to it.

What sort of examples?

For instance, if we had to reach agreement concerning that city, and the man born and raised in a manner similar to it, as to whether such a man, on receiving a deposit of gold or silver, would be likely to withhold it — who do you suppose would think {{{ 443a }}} that this man would do such a thing, rather than those who are not of that kind?

No one would, he said.

Then would not this man also be free of temple robberies and thefts and betrayals, whether of friends in private or of cities in public?

Free of them.

And moreover, he would in no way whatever be untrustworthy, either with regard to oaths or to other agreements.

How could he be?

Adulteries, neglect of parents, and failure to worship the gods belong to anyone else rather than to a man of this kind.

To anyone else indeed, he said. {{{ 443b }}}

Then is not the cause of all this that each of the parts within him does its own work, both with regard to ruling and being ruled?

This, and nothing else.

Do you still look for justice to be anything other than this power, which produces men and cities of this kind?

By Zeus, he said, I do not.

Then our dream has been perfectly fulfilled for us — the one we said we suspected, that right from the beginning, as we were founding the city, we were probably, by the guidance of {{{ 443c }}} some god, setting foot upon some starting point and outline of justice.

Absolutely.

And that, then, Glaucon — which is also why it does good — turns out to have been only an image of justice: that the man naturally suited for shoemaking should rightly practice shoemaking and do nothing else, and the man suited for carpentry should practice carpentry, and so on for the rest.

So it appears.

But the truth, as it seems, was that justice was something of this sort, concerned not with a man's outward doing of his own, but with the {{{ 443d }}} inward doing, truly concerning himself and what belongs to him, not allowing the parts within him each to do the work of another, nor allowing the kinds in the soul to meddle with one another, but rather, having truly set his own affairs in good order, and having ruled himself, and set himself in order, and become a friend to himself, and having fitted the three parts together, quite literally like three defining notes of a harmony — the lowest, the highest, and the middle — and whatever others there happen to be between them, having bound all these {{{ 443e }}} together, and having become entirely one out of many, moderate and harmonized, only then does he act, if he acts at all, whether about the acquisition of money, or the care of the body, or something political, or private contracts — in all these holding and naming a just and fine action whichever preserves and helps produce this condition, and calling the knowledge that oversees this action wisdom, and calling an {{{ 444a }}} unjust action whichever always destroys this condition, and naming ignorance the opinion that in turn oversees that action.

That is altogether true, Socrates, he said.

Well then, I said — if we were to say that we have found the just man, the just city, and justice itself, that is, what it actually is within them, I do not think we would seem to be altogether lying.

By Zeus, no indeed, he said.

Shall we say so, then?

Let us say so.

So be it, then, I said — for after this, I think, we must examine injustice.

Clearly. {{{ 444b }}}

Then must not injustice, in turn, given that there are these three parts, be a kind of civil strife among them, and a meddling and doing of another's work, and a rebellion of some part against the whole of the soul, so that it might rule within it though it has no right to, being of such a nature that it is fitting for it to be a slave, while the other, being of the ruling kind, ought not to be enslaved? Something of this kind, I think, we shall say — that this turmoil and wandering astray among them is injustice, and lack of restraint, and cowardice, and ignorance, and, in a word, vice entire.

Just that, he said. {{{ 444c }}}

Then, I said, doing unjust things, and acting unjustly, and, in turn, doing just things — are not all these already clearly evident, if injustice and justice themselves are?

How so?

Because, I said, they turn out to be no different from what is healthy and diseased — as those are in the body, so these are in the soul.

In what way? He said.

Healthy things, surely, produce health, while diseased things produce disease.

Yes.

Then does not doing just things produce justice, {{{ 444d }}} while doing unjust things produces injustice?

Necessarily.

And to produce health is to establish the parts in the body according to nature, so that they rule and are ruled by one another, while to produce disease is to establish them contrary to nature, so that one rules and is ruled by another.

Yes, it is so.

Then, in turn, I said, is it not that to produce justice is to establish the parts in the soul according to nature, so that they rule and are ruled by one another, while to produce injustice is to establish them contrary to nature, so that one rules and is ruled by another?

Quite so, he said.

Virtue, then, as it seems, would be a kind of health, {{{ 444e }}} beauty, and good condition of the soul, while vice would be disease, ugliness, and weakness.

So it is.

Then do not fine practices also lead to the acquisition of virtue, and shameful ones to that of vice?

Necessarily.

What remains for us now, then, as it seems, is to examine {{{ 445a }}} whether it is more profitable, in turn, to do just things and practice fine ones and be just, whether or not one goes unnoticed in being such, or to do unjust things and be unjust, provided one does not pay the penalty and is not made better by being punished.

But, Socrates, he said, this inquiry now seems to me to be growing ridiculous, if, when the nature of the body is being destroyed, life is thought not to be worth living, even with every kind of food and drink and all wealth and all power, yet when the very nature of that by which we live is being disturbed and {{{ 445b }}} destroyed, life will nonetheless be worth living, provided only that a person does whatever he wishes, except the one thing by which he would be rid of vice and injustice, and would acquire justice and virtue, since each of these has now been shown to be just as we have described them.

It is ridiculous, I said — but nevertheless, since we have come this far, we must not grow weary before seeing as clearly as possible that this is indeed so.

Least of all things, by Zeus, he said, should we grow weary. {{{ 445c }}}

Come here, then, I said, so that you may also see how many forms vice has, as it seems to me — ones that are indeed worth beholding.

I am following, he said — only speak.

Well then, I said, as though from a lookout point, now that we have climbed this far in the argument, it appears to me that there is one form of virtue, but countless forms of vice, four of which, among them, are worth mentioning.

What do you mean? He said.

There are likely to be as many kinds of soul, I said, as there are kinds of constitution that have distinct forms.

How many, then? {{{ 445d }}}

Five kinds of constitution, I said, and five of soul.

Tell me, he said, what they are.

I say, I replied, that this one, which we have already gone through, would be one kind of constitution, and it might also be named in two ways — for if a single man of outstanding quality should arise among the rulers, it would be called kingship, but if several such men should arise, aristocracy.

True, he said.

This, then, I said, I call one form — for neither {{{ 445e }}} several men, nor a single one, arising among the rulers, would disturb any of the laws of the city worth mentioning, if they had made use of the upbringing and education we have described.

No, that is not likely, he said.
