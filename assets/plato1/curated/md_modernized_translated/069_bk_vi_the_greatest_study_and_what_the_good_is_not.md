## The Greatest Study, and What the Good Is Not

{{{ 502c }}} I do not think so myself, he said.

And yet, as I think, we discussed sufficiently before this that our proposals are the best, if indeed they can be realized.

Yes, sufficiently so.

Now then, it seems, this is how matters stand for us regarding our legislation: what we propose is best, if it could come to be, but difficult to bring about — not impossible, however.

So it does, he said.

Then, since this has at last, with difficulty, reached its conclusion, must we not now say what remains after it — in what way, and out of what studies {{{ 502d }}} and practices, the saviors of the constitution will be found among us, and at what age each will take up each of these?

We must indeed say it, he said.

It was no cleverness on my part, I said, that made me pass over earlier the difficulty of the possession of women, and child-bearing, and the establishment of the rulers — I knew how invidious the wholly true arrangement is, and how hard to bring about; for now, nonetheless, the need to go through it has come. {{{ 502e }}} And the matters of the women and children have now been settled, but the matter of the rulers must be taken up again as if from the beginning. We said, {{{ 503a }}} if you remember, that they must be shown to be lovers of their city, tested in pleasures and pains, and must be seen not casting out this conviction in toils, nor in fears, nor in any other change of fortune — or else the one unable to hold to it must be rejected, while the one who comes through everywhere unspoiled, like gold tested in fire, must be set up as a ruler, and must be given honors and prizes, both in life and after death. Such, more or less, were the things said, while the {{{ 503b }}} argument slipped sideways and veiled itself, afraid to stir what now lies before us.

What you say is most true, he said — I do remember.

Yes, my friend, I said, I shrank from saying what has now been dared — but let this much now be dared: that our most exact guardians must be established as philosophers.

So let it be said, he said.

Understand, then, how naturally there will be few such men for you — for the nature we described as needing to belong to them rarely wants its parts to grow together in the same person; for the most part it grows up scattered. {{{ 503c }}}

How, he said, do you mean?

You know that quick learners, men of good memory, keen-witted, sharp, and whatever else goes along with these, are not apt to be born at once both vigorous and magnificent in mind, of the sort willing to live in an orderly way, with quiet and steadiness; rather, such quick men are carried by their sharpness wherever they happen to go, and all their steadiness deserts them.

What you say is true, he said.

Now these steady characters in turn, the ones not easily changed, which one would rather rely on {{{ 503d }}} as trustworthy, and which in war are not easily moved by fears, do the same thing again with respect to learning — they are sluggish and slow to learn, as if benumbed, and they are filled with sleep and yawning whenever there is need to work hard at anything of that sort.

That is so, he said.

But we say that a man must have a good and fine share of both — or else he must be given no share, either of the most exact education, or of honor, or of rule.

Rightly so, he said.

Then do you not think this will be rare?

How could it not? {{{ 503e }}}

He must be tested, then, in the toils and fears and pleasures we spoke of before, and further — this we now say, what we passed over then — he must be exercised in many studies too, while we watch whether that nature will be able to bear even the greatest studies, or whether it will {{{ 504a }}} play the coward, as those who play the coward in other things do.

It is indeed fitting, he said, to look at it this way. But which studies do you call the greatest?

You remember, I suppose, I said, that when we had distinguished three kinds of soul, we worked out, concerning justice and moderation and courage and wisdom, what each one is.

If I did not remember, he said, I would rightly deserve not to hear what remains.

And do you remember, too, what was said about them beforehand?

What was that? {{{ 504b }}}

We said, I believe, that to see them in the finest way there was another, longer road, by going round which they would become plain — though it was possible to attach to them proofs consistent with what had been said before. And you said that was enough, and so what was said then fell short, as it seemed to me, of exactness; but whether it was satisfactory to you, you yourselves would be the ones to say.

But to me, at least, he said, it seemed adequate — and indeed it seemed so to the others too. {{{ 504c }}}

But, my friend, I said, a measure of such things that falls short of what is by anything at all is not really adequate — for nothing incomplete is the measure of anything. But sometimes some people think that things are already good enough, and that there is no need to inquire further.

Yes indeed, he said, many people feel this way, out of laziness.

But this is a feeling, I said, that a guardian of the city and its laws needs least of all.

So it would seem, he said.

The longer road, then, my friend, I said, such a man must travel, {{{ 504d }}} and he must labor no less in learning than in physical training — or else, as we just said, he will never reach the end of the greatest and most fitting study.

What, he said, are these not the greatest things — is there something greater still than justice and the other things we discussed?

Something greater, I said — and of these very things it is not an outline that we must look at, as we do now; rather, we must not pass over the most finished working of them out. Or is it not absurd that, over other matters of little worth, {{{ 504e }}} we strain ourselves to do everything so that they will be as exact and as pure as possible, while for the greatest matters we do not think that the greatest exactness is due as well?

Very much so, he said, [the thought is a worthy one] — but as for this greatest study you speak of, and what it concerns, do you think, he said, that anyone would let you off without asking what it is?

Hardly, I said — but do ask, you too. At any rate you have heard it not just once or twice — but now either you do not recognize it, or {{{ 505a }}} you mean to make trouble for me by picking a quarrel with it. And I think it is rather this — since you have often heard that the form of the good is the greatest study, the one by whose further use just things, and everything else too, become useful and beneficial. And now you know well enough that this is what I am about to say, and, further, that we do not know it adequately; but if we do not know it, then, however well we might know everything else without it, you know that it does us no good at all — just as it would do no good to {{{ 505b }}} possess anything at all without the good. Or do you think there is any advantage in possessing every possession there is, if it is not a good one? Or in understanding everything else without the good, while understanding nothing that is fine and good?

No, by Zeus, not I, he said.

But surely you also know this much: that to the many the good seems to be pleasure, while to the more sophisticated, it is practical wisdom.

How could I not?

And you know too, my friend, that those who hold this view are unable to say which practical wisdom they mean, but in the end are forced to say it is the practical wisdom of the good.

Yes indeed, he said, and ridiculously so. {{{ 505c }}}

How could it not be, I said, when, after reproaching us for not knowing the good, they turn around and speak to us as though we did know it? For they say it is the practical wisdom of the good, as if we in turn understood what they mean whenever they utter the word good.

That is very true, he said.

But what about those who define the good as pleasure? Are they perhaps any less full of confusion than the others? Or are these people too not forced to admit that there are bad pleasures?

Very much so.

It follows, I think, that they end up admitting that the same things are both good and bad. Isn't that so? {{{ 505d }}}

Of course.

So is it not clear that there are great and many disputes about it?

How could there not be?

But what of this? Is it not clear that, with things just and fine, many would choose the things that merely seem so, even ⟨if⟩ they are not, and would nonetheless practice, and possess, and be thought to have them; but with good things, it is no longer enough for anyone to possess what merely seems good — instead they seek what really is good, and here, at last, everyone already disdains mere opinion?

Very much so, he said.

That which every soul pursues, and for the sake of which it does {{{ 505e }}} everything, divining that it is something real, yet at a loss, unable to grasp well enough what it actually is, or to make use of the sort of stable belief about it that it has about other things, and because of this missing whatever benefit there was in those other things as well — about something so important and so great, {{{ 506a }}} shall we then say that even those best men in the city, to whom we shall entrust everything, must be left in the dark?

Least of all, he said.

I think, at any rate, I said, that just things and fine things, so long as it is not known in what way they are good, will have got themselves no very valuable guardian in the man who does not know this — and I divine that no one will know them adequately before he knows that.

Yes, you divine rightly, he said.

Then will our constitution be perfectly ordered, if such a {{{ 506b }}} guardian oversees it — one who has knowledge of these things?
