## The Guardians at War, and How Greeks Should Treat Greeks

I agree, he said.

Then is it not left, I said, to determine this — whether, just as with the other animals, this community can also come about among human beings, and if so, in what way it is possible?

You have anticipated me, he said, in saying what I was about to raise. {{{ 466e }}}

As for the matters of war, I think, I said, it is clear in what manner they will make war.

How so? He asked.

That they will campaign together, and moreover will take with them to the war those of their children who are old enough, so that, like the children of the other craftsmen, they may look on at the things they will have to practice themselves once they are grown; {{{ 467a }}} and besides looking on, they will help and serve in everything connected with the war, and wait on their fathers and mothers. Or have you not noticed how it is with the crafts — for instance the children of potters — how for a long time they serve and look on before they lay a hand to the potter's craft themselves?

Very much so.

Should those craftsmen, then, train their children more carefully by experience and observation of what belongs to their work than the guardians train theirs?

That would be ridiculous, he said.

But surely every animal fights with special ferocity {{{ 467b }}} when its own offspring are present.

That is so. But there is no small danger, Socrates, that if things go wrong, as often happens in war, they will lose their children along with themselves and leave the rest of the city unable to recover.

What you say is true, I said. But do you think, first of all, that we should arrange things so that they are never in danger at all?

Not at all.

What then? If they must run some risk, should it not be where success will make them better?

Clearly. {{{ 467c }}}

But do you think it makes only a small difference, and is not worth the risk, whether or not children who are going to be warriors as men watch the business of war?

No, it makes a difference, in view of what you are saying.

This, then, must be our starting point: to make the children spectators of war, but to contrive safety for them as well, and things will go well; is that not so?

Yes.

Then, I said, in the first place their fathers, as far as human beings can be, will not be ignorant but skilled in judging {{{ 467d }}} which campaigns are dangerous and which are not?

That is likely, he said.

So they will take them to some, and be cautious about others.

Rightly so.

And, I said, they will surely put in charge of them not the most inferior commanders, but those competent by experience and age to be both leaders and guides.

That is fitting.

But, we shall say, many things have happened to many people contrary to expectation.

Very much so.

Against such possibilities, my friend, we must give them wings while they are still children, so that, if need be, they can fly away and escape. {{{ 467e }}}

What do you mean? He asked.

We must, I said, set them on horses as early as possible, and once they have been taught to ride, take them on horseback to watch, mounted not on spirited or battle-eager horses, but on the swiftest and most manageable ones we can find. For in this way they will get the finest view of their own work, and, should there be need, will make their escape most safely by following their older guides.

You seem to me to be right, he said. {{{ 468a }}}

And what, I said, of the business of war? How should the soldiers behave toward one another and toward the enemy? Does what occurs to me seem right, or not?

Tell me, he said, what these are this time.

As for our own men, I said, must not one of them who deserts his post, or throws away his weapons, or does anything of that sort out of cowardice, be made into some kind of craftsman or farmer?

Certainly.

And must not one who is taken alive by the enemy be given as a gift to his captors, to do with their catch whatever they wish? {{{ 468b }}}

Quite so.

And as for the one who distinguishes himself and wins honor, do you not think that first, while still on campaign, he should be crowned by each of the young men and boys serving alongside him, one after another? Or not?

I do.

What then? Should he also have his hand clasped?

That too.

But this, I think, I said, you no longer agree with.

What is that?

That he should kiss and be kissed by each of them.

Most of all, he said — and I would add to the law, {{{ 468c }}} for as long as they are on this campaign, that no one is allowed to refuse a kiss to anyone who wants to kiss him, so that, if anyone happens to be in erotic love with someone, male or female, he may be more eager to carry off the prize for valor.

Well put, I said. For it has already been said that for the man who is good there will be more marriages ready than for the others, and more frequent choice of such partners than the rest have, so that as many children as possible may come from such a man.

Yes, we did say that, he said.

And indeed, according to Homer too, it is just to honor such men {{{ 468d }}} among the young who prove themselves good. For Homer said that Ajax, who had distinguished himself in war, was honored with long cuts of the chine, this being a fitting honor for a man who is in his prime and courageous, one from which, along with the honor, his strength would also increase.

Quite right, he said.

In this, then, I said, we shall follow Homer. For we too, at sacrifices and on all such occasions, will honor the good, in proportion to how good they show themselves, both with hymns and with the things we were just now speaking of, and besides these, with seats of honor and meat {{{ 468e }}} and full cups, so that in honoring them we may also train our good men and women.

Very well put, he said.

Very well — of those who die on campaign, whoever dies having won honor, shall we not say, first of all, that he belongs to the golden race?

Most of all.

But shall we not follow Hesiod in saying, whenever any of this race die, that indeed— {{{ 469a }}}
+ they turn into holy spirits who dwell upon the earth,
+ good beings, defenders against harm, guardians of speaking mortals?

We shall follow him, then.

So, having inquired of the god how we should lay to rest those who are daimonic and divine, and with what distinction, shall we lay them to rest in just that way he directs?

Why should we not?

And for all time to come, shall we tend and revere their tombs {{{ 469b }}} as we would those of daimonic beings? And shall we establish these same customs whenever anyone dies of old age or in some other way, among those judged to have been outstandingly good in life?

That is only just, he said.

What then? How will our soldiers behave toward the enemy?

In what respect?

First, regarding enslavement: does it seem just for Greeks to enslave Greek cities, or should we not permit it to anyone else either, so far as we can, and instead make it a habit to spare the Greek {{{ 469c }}} race, out of caution against enslavement at the hands of the barbarians?

Sparing them, he said, is altogether and entirely the better course.

Then should they themselves not own any Greek as a slave, and should they advise the other Greeks to do likewise?

Certainly, he said; in this way they would turn their attention more toward the barbarians, and keep their hands off one another.

What then? Is it right, I said, to strip the dead of anything except their weapons, once they have won the victory? Or does it not give cowards {{{ 469d }}} a pretext for not going up against the man still fighting, as though they were doing something necessary when they linger over the corpse, and have not many an army already been destroyed through plunder of this sort?

Very much so.

And does it not seem ignoble and greedy to plunder a corpse, and does it not show a womanish and petty mind to regard the body as the enemy, when the real enemy has flown off and left behind only the thing it fought with? Or do you think those who do this {{{ 469e }}} act any differently from dogs, which get angry at the stones thrown at them without touching the one who threw them?

Not in the least, he said.

So must we give up the plundering of corpses and the obstruction of their recovery for burial?

We certainly must give it up, he said, by Zeus.

Nor, surely, will we carry weapons off to dedicate at the temples, especially not weapons taken from Greeks, {{{ 470a }}} if we care at all about goodwill toward the rest of the Greeks; rather we will be afraid that bringing such things to a temple from our own kin might be a kind of pollution, unless the god says otherwise.

Quite right, he said.

What of the ravaging of Greek land and the burning of houses? What sort of thing will our soldiers do to the enemy in this regard, in your view?

I would gladly hear you declare your opinion, he said.

Well, I said, it seems to me that they should do neither of these things, {{{ 470b }}} but only take away the year's crop. And do you want me to tell you why?

Certainly.

It seems to me that, just as there are two names for these things, war and civil strife, so too there are two realities, resting on two different kinds of conflict. By the two, I mean on one hand what is one's own and akin, and on the other what is foreign and alien. So hostility toward what is one's own is called civil strife, and hostility toward what is foreign is called war.

And what you say, he said, is not at all off the mark. {{{ 470c }}}

Consider, then, whether this too is to the point in what I am saying. For I say that the Greek race is, in itself, its own kin and akin to itself, but is foreign and alien to the barbarian race.

Well put, he said.

Then we shall say that when Greeks fight barbarians and barbarians fight Greeks, they are at war and are enemies by nature, and this hostility must be called war; whereas when Greeks do such a thing to Greeks, they are friends by nature, but in such a case Greece is sick and torn by faction, {{{ 470d }}} and this sort of hostility must be called civil strife.

For my part, he said, I agree that we should think of it in this way.

Consider then, I said, that in what is now agreed to be civil strife, wherever something of this kind occurs and a city is divided, if each side ravages the other's fields and burns their houses, the strife is regarded as abominable and neither side as loving its own city — for they would never dare to ravage their own nurse and mother — but that the measured course is for the victors to take away the crops {{{ 470e }}} of the defeated, and to bear in mind that they will one day be reconciled and will not go on making war forever.

For this way of thinking, he said, is far gentler than the other.

What then? I asked — will not the city you are founding be a Greek city?

It must be, he said.

Then will they not also be good and gentle?

Very much so.

And will they not be lovers of Greece? Will they not regard Greece as their own, and share in the same sacred rites as the other Greeks?

Very much so indeed. {{{ 471a }}}

Then will they not regard a quarrel with Greeks, since it is with their own people, as civil strife, and refuse even to give it the name of war?

No, they will not.

And will they carry on their disputes as people who intend to be reconciled?

Certainly.

So they will discipline them with goodwill, punishing them not with a view to enslavement or destruction, since they are correctors, not enemies.

Just so, he said.

So, being Greeks, they will not ravage Greece, nor will they burn down dwellings, nor will they agree that in each city everyone is their enemy — men, women, and children alike — but only ever a few, those responsible for the conflict. {{{ 471b }}} And for all these reasons they will be unwilling to ravage their land, since most of them are friends, or to tear down their houses, but will carry the conflict only to the point where those responsible are compelled, by the suffering of those not responsible, to pay the penalty.

For my part, he said, I agree that our citizens should treat their opponents in this way; but toward the barbarians, as the Greeks now treat one another.

Shall we then lay down this law too for our guardians, that they neither ravage the land {{{ 471c }}} nor burn down houses?
