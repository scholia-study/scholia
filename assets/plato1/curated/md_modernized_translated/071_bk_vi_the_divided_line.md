## The Divided Line

{{{ 509d }}} Understand, then, I said, as we are saying, that there are two of them, and that the one rules over the kind and region of the intelligible, while the other, in turn, over the visible — I avoid saying the heaven, so that I may not seem to you to be quibbling about the name. But in any case, do you grasp these as two kinds, visible and intelligible?

I have them.

So then, take a line cut into two unequal segments, and cut each of the two segments again by the same ratio — the segment of the visible kind and that of the intelligible — and you will have them, in respect of clarity and obscurity relative to one another, so that in the visible {{{ 509e }}} the one segment is images — and by images I mean, first {{{ 510a }}} of all, shadows, then reflections in water and in whatever is close-grained, smooth, and bright, and everything of that sort — if you follow.

But I do follow.

Now set down the other segment as that which this resembles: the living things around us, the whole class of what grows, and the whole class of what is manufactured.

I set it down, he said.

Would you also be willing to say, I said, that it has been divided with respect to truth and untruth, so that as the opinable is to the knowable, so the likeness is to that which it resembles? {{{ 510b }}}

I would indeed, he said, very much so.

Now consider, in turn, how the intelligible must be divided.

How?

In this way — in the one part of it the soul, using as images the things imitated before, is compelled to inquire from hypotheses, proceeding not toward a first principle but toward a conclusion, while in the other part, in turn — the part that goes toward an unhypothesized first principle — it goes from a hypothesis, and without the images the other part uses, makes its way by means of the forms themselves, through themselves.

What you are saying, he said, I did not follow well enough. {{{ 510c }}}

But let me try again, I said; for once I have said certain things first, you will understand more easily. For I think you know that those who occupy themselves with geometry, calculation, and such things posit the odd and the even, the figures, the three kinds of angles, and other things akin to these in each branch, treating them as known — having made these into hypotheses, they no longer think it worthwhile to give any account of them, either to themselves or to others, {{{ 510d }}} on the ground that they are evident to everyone — and starting from these, going through the remaining steps, they arrive, consistently, at that very thing toward which they set out to inquire.

Quite so, he said — that much I know.

And also that they make further use of visible shapes and construct their arguments about them, thinking not about these but about those things which they resemble — making their arguments for the sake of the square itself and its diagonal, not {{{ 510e }}} for the sake of this one which they draw — and so with everything else in the same way: these very things which they mold and draw, of which there are also shadows and images in water, they now use, in turn, as images, while seeking {{{ 511a }}} to see those things themselves which one could not see otherwise than by understanding.

That is true, he said.

This, then, is what I called the intelligible kind — that the soul is compelled to use hypotheses in its inquiry into it, not proceeding toward a first principle, on the ground that it is unable to step above the hypotheses, but using as images those very things which are copied by the things below, and which, set beside those, are regarded and honored as being clear and evident. {{{ 511b }}}

I understand, he said, that you mean what falls under geometry and its sister crafts.

Now understand that by the other segment of the intelligible I mean this: that which argument itself takes hold of by the power of dialectic, treating its hypotheses not as first principles but truly as hypotheses — as footholds and springboards — so that, going as far as the unhypothesized, to the first principle of the whole, and having grasped it, and then again holding on to the things that hang from it, it thus descends to a conclusion, {{{ 511c }}} making no use whatsoever of anything perceptible, but of the forms themselves, through themselves, into themselves, and ends in forms.

I understand, he said — not fully, for you seem to me to be describing quite a task — but that you wish to mark off as clearer what is studied by the knowledge of dialectic concerning being and the intelligible, than what is studied by the so-called crafts, for which hypotheses are first principles, and those who study them are compelled to view them by understanding rather than by the senses; yet because they do not ascend to a first principle {{{ 511d }}} in their examination, but proceed from hypotheses, they seem to you not to possess intellect about these things, even though they are intelligible together with a first principle. And you seem to me to call the condition of geometers and such people understanding, but not intellect, treating understanding as something between opinion and intellect.

You have grasped it most adequately, I said. And for me, take these four states arising in the soul corresponding to the four segments — reason for the topmost, understanding {{{ 511e }}} for the second, and assign belief to the third and imagination to the last — and arrange them in proportion, considering that just as the things they are set over share in truth, so these states share in clarity.

I understand, he said, and I agree, and I arrange them as you say.
