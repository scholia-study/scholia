## Diction: Narrative, Imitation, and the Mixed Kind

{{{ 392c }}} Then as for human beings, that stories of this sort must be told about them — is that something we shall agree on only once we have discovered what justice is, and how it is by nature advantageous to the one who has it, whether or not he is thought to be such a man?

Very true, he said.

Let the matter of content, then, have its end; the matter of diction, I think, is what must be examined next, and then our inquiry into both what must be said and how it must be said will be complete.

And Adeimantus said, I do not understand what you mean by this. {{{ 392d }}}

But indeed you must, I said; perhaps you will grasp it better this way. Is it not true that everything said by storytellers or poets turns out to be narrative concerning things that have happened, are happening, or are going to happen?

What else, he said, could it be?

Then do they not carry it through either by plain narrative, or by narrative through imitation, or by both together?

And this too, he said, I still need to understand more clearly.

I seem, I said, to be a ridiculous and unclear teacher; so then, like people who are no good at speaking, I will not take it as a whole but take up {{{ 392e }}} one part of it and try in that to make my meaning clear to you. And tell me: do you know the opening of the Iliad, where the poet says that Chryses begs Agamemnon to release his daughter, that Agamemnon grows angry, and that Chryses, since {{{ 393a }}} he is getting nowhere, prays against the Achaeans to the god?

I do.

Do you know, then, that up to these verses—
+ …and he pleaded with all the Achaeans,
+ but above all with the two sons of Atreus, orderers of the host—
it is the poet himself who speaks, and he does not try to turn our thought elsewhere, as if someone other than himself were the speaker; but after this he speaks as though he himself were Chryses, and tries {{{ 393b }}} his very best to make us think that it is not Homer who is speaking but the priest, an old man. And in just this way he has composed nearly all the rest of his narrative, concerning the sufferings both in Troy and in Ithaca and throughout the whole Odyssey.

Quite so, he said.

Then is it not narrative both when he delivers the speeches themselves and when he gives what falls between the speeches?

How could it not be? {{{ 393c }}}

But when he delivers some speech as though he were someone else, shall we not then say that he likens himself as closely as possible, in his own diction, to whatever person he has announced beforehand as about to speak?

We shall say so; why would we not?

Then to liken oneself to another, whether in voice or in bearing, is surely to imitate that person to whom one likens oneself?

What else could it be?

In such a case, then, it seems, both this poet and the other poets carry out their narrative through imitation.

Quite so.

But if the poet were nowhere to conceal himself, then his whole {{{ 393d }}} composition and narrative would have come about without imitation. And so that you will not say again that you do not understand, I will explain how this would come about. For if Homer, after saying that Chryses came bringing ransom for his daughter and as a suppliant of the Achaeans, and above all of their kings, had gone on speaking after this not as though he had become Chryses but still as Homer, you know that it would not have been imitation but plain narrative. It would have gone something like this — I will put it without meter, for I am no poet — the priest came {{{ 393e }}} and prayed that the gods would grant that they, having taken Troy, should themselves come home safe, and that they would release his daughter to him, accepting the ransom and showing reverence for the god. When he had said this, the rest of them showed reverence and gave their assent, but Agamemnon grew fierce, commanding him to leave at once and never come back, lest his staff and the garlands of the god should fail to protect him; and he said that, before his daughter should be released, she would grow old in Argos together with him — and he ordered him to depart and not provoke him, {{{ 394a }}} so that he might reach home safely. And the old man, hearing this, was afraid and went away in silence; but withdrawing from the camp he prayed at length to Apollo, calling on the god by his titles, and reminding him, and claiming a return for any pleasing gift he had ever made him, whether in the building of temples or in the sacrifices at his shrines; and for the sake of these he prayed that the Achaeans should pay for his tears with the god's arrows. In this way, I said, my friend, plain narrative comes about without {{{ 394b }}} imitation.

I understand, he said.

Understand, then, I said, that the opposite of this comes about when someone removes what the poet says between the speeches and leaves only the exchanges.

This too I understand, he said — that this is what happens in tragedies.

You have understood quite rightly, I said, and I think I can now make clear to you what I was unable to before — that of poetry and storytelling {{{ 394c }}} one kind is wholly through imitation, as you say, namely tragedy and comedy; another is through the poet's own report — you would find this especially in dithyrambs; and yet another, in turn, is through both together, as in epic poetry, and in many other places besides, if you follow me.

But I do understand, he said, what you meant to say then.

And recall also what came before this — that we said the question of what must be said had already been settled, but the question of how it must be said still had to be examined.

I do remember. {{{ 394d }}}

This, then, was just what I meant — that we must come to an agreement whether we shall allow the poets to make their narratives for us by imitating, or by imitating some things and not others — and which are which — or not to imitate at all.

I suspect, he said, that you are considering whether we shall admit tragedy and comedy into the city, or not.

Perhaps, I said, and perhaps even more than these; for I myself do not yet know, but wherever the argument, like a wind, carries us, there we must go.

And you are right to say so, he said.
