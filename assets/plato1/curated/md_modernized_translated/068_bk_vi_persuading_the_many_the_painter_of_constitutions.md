## Persuading the Many: The Painter of Constitutions

Not in the least.

Nor again, my dear fellow, have they become adequate listeners to noble, free discourse — of the kind that strains after truth by every means for the sake of knowing it — while merely saluting from a distance whatever is clever and contentious and aims at nothing beyond reputation and rivalry, whether in the courts or in private company.

Not of these either, he said.

It was for just this reason, I said — foreseeing it even then — that {{{ 499b }}} we spoke as we did, however much we feared to, compelled by the truth: that neither a city nor a constitution, nor a man either, will ever become perfect until some necessity of chance compels these few philosophers — who are not wicked, though now called useless — whether they wish it or not, to take care of a city, and the city to be obedient to them, or until, upon the sons of those now holding power or kingship, or upon those men themselves, {{{ 499c }}} a true erotic love of true philosophy falls from some divine inspiration. Now that either one of these things, or both, should happen is impossible — for that, I say, no one has any argument. For in that case we should justly be laughed at for saying things no better than wishful prayers. Or is that not so?

So it is.

If, then, it is a necessity that men supreme in philosophy should take charge of a city — whether that has already happened in the boundless time now past, or is even now the case in some barbarian place, far off {{{ 499d }}} and beyond our view, or will come to be hereafter — on this point we are ready to contend by argument: that the constitution we have described has come to be, and is, and will indeed come to be, whenever this Muse becomes mistress of a city. For it is not impossible for it to come to be, nor are we saying impossible things — though that it is difficult is something we too admit.

So it seems to me too, he said.

But will you say, I said, that the many, for their part, do not think so?

Perhaps, he said.

My dear fellow, I said, do not accuse the many so harshly. {{{ 499e }}} They will have quite a different opinion, if, instead of contending for victory, you show them — in a spirit of reassurance, clearing away the slander against the love of learning — which men you mean by philosophers, and if you distinguish, {{{ 500a }}} just as you did a moment ago, their nature and their pursuit, so that they do not suppose you mean the men they have in mind. Or, even if they should view it that way [and you will say they would take on a different opinion and answer differently], do you suppose that anyone who is himself free of envy and gentle would be harsh toward one who is not harsh, or envious of one who is not envious? For I will say it before you do: I think a nature so harsh as that arises in only a few people, not in the multitude.

I too, of course, share that belief, he said. {{{ 500b }}}

Then do you also agree with this: that those responsible for the many being harshly disposed toward philosophy are the outsiders who have burst in where they do not belong, reviling one another, behaving contentiously, and always making their arguments about persons — doing what is least fitting for philosophy?

Very much so, he said.

For surely, Adeimantus, a man whose mind is truly fixed on the things that are has no leisure to look down at {{{ 500c }}} human business, to quarrel with people and be filled with envy and ill will; instead, looking at and contemplating things that are ordered and always constant, that neither wrong one another nor are wronged by one another, but are all in order and according to reason, he imitates them and assimilates himself to them as much as he can. Or do you think there is any way that a man who keeps admiring company with something will fail to imitate it?

Impossible, he said.

Then, keeping company with what is divine and orderly, the philosopher becomes orderly {{{ 500d }}} and divine, so far as is possible for a man — though slander is plentiful in every quarter.

Yes, altogether so.

Now if some necessity should come upon him, I said, to practice putting what he sees there into human characters — both privately and publicly — and not merely to shape himself alone, do you think he will turn out to be a poor craftsman of moderation and justice and popular virtue as a whole?

Least of all, he said.

But if the many should come to perceive that what we say about him {{{ 500e }}} is true, will they then be harsh with the philosophers, and disbelieve us when we say that a city could never be happy in any other way, unless it were painted by those painters who use the divine pattern?

They will not be harsh, he said, if indeed they perceive it. But what manner of sketching {{{ 501a }}} do you mean?

Taking the city and the characters of men, I said, as if they were a tablet, they would first wipe it clean — which is not at all an easy thing to do; but in any case you know that they would immediately differ from everyone else on this very point: that they would be unwilling to lay a hand on either a private person or a city, or to write laws, until they had either received it clean or made it clean themselves.

And rightly so, he said.

Then after this, do you think they would sketch in outline the form of the constitution?

What else? {{{ 501b }}}

Then, I think, as they filled in the work, they would glance frequently in each direction — toward what is by nature just and fine and moderate, and all such things, and again toward that other image they were producing in men, blending and mixing from human ways of life the very likeness of a man, taking their measure from that quality which Homer too, when it arises in men, called godlike and god-resembling.

Rightly so, he said.

And I think they would rub out one part and paint in another, {{{ 501c }}} until they had made human characters as dear to the gods as could possibly be.

It would certainly be the most beautiful painting, he said.

Are we then, I said, in some way persuading those men whom you said were coming at us up in arms, that the painter of constitutions we were praising to them at the time — the one because of whom they grew angry with us, since we were handing cities over to him — is a painter of just this kind, and are they, now that they hear this, growing somewhat gentler?

Yes, much gentler, he said, if they are in their right minds. {{{ 501d }}}

For how indeed will they have grounds to dispute it? Will they dispute that philosophers are lovers of what is and of truth?

That would be absurd, he said.

Or will they dispute that their nature — the one we went through — is akin to the best?

Not that either.

What then? Will such a nature, if it meets with the practices proper to it, not become perfectly good and philosophic, if any nature ever will? Or will he rather say it is those men, the ones we ruled out? {{{ 501e }}}

Surely not.

Will they then still grow savage when we say that, until the philosophic kind becomes master of a city, there will be no rest from evils for either the city or the citizens, nor will the constitution we have been telling as a tale in speech ever reach its completion in deed?

Perhaps less so, he said.

Do you want us, then, I said, to say not that they have become less harsh, but {{{ 502a }}} that they have become altogether gentle and are persuaded, so that, if nothing else, they may at least agree out of shame?

Certainly, he said.

Let these men, then, I said, be taken as persuaded of this — but will anyone contend, on this next point, that the offspring of kings or of potentates could not turn out to be philosophic in their natures?

Not a single person, he said.

But can anyone say that, once such men have come to be, there is a great necessity for them to be corrupted? That it is difficult for them to be preserved, we too {{{ 502b }}} agree — but is there anyone who would dispute that, in all time, out of all such men, not a single one could ever be preserved?

How could there be?

But surely, I said, one man, if he proves sufficient and has a city obedient to him, could accomplish everything that is now disbelieved.

For one is enough, he said.

For surely, I said, when a ruler establishes the laws and practices we have gone through, it is not, I take it, impossible for the citizens to be willing to carry them out.

Not in the slightest.

But then, is it anything to marvel at, or anything impossible, that what seems right to us should seem right to others as well?
