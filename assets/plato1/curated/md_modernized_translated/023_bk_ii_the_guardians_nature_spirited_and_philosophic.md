## The Guardian's Nature: Spirited and Philosophic

{{{ 375a }}} No, certainly not, he said.

Do you think, I said, that the nature of a well-bred puppy is any different, for guard-duty, from that of a well-born young man?

What do you mean?

Something like this — each of the two needs to be sharp of perception, and quick to chase down whatever it perceives, and strong besides, if it has to catch something and then fight for it.

Yes, he said, all of that is required.

And courageous too, certainly, if it is to fight well.

Of course.

But will anything be courageous without spirit — horse, dog, or any other creature? Or have you not noticed that {{{ 375b }}} spirit is irresistible and unbeatable, and that wherever it is present, the whole soul becomes fearless and unconquerable, whatever it faces?

I have noticed.

What the guardian's body should be like, then, is clear.

Yes.

And so is what belongs to his soul — that it must be spirited.

That too.

Then how, Glaucon, I said, will men of such a nature avoid turning savage, both toward one another and toward the rest of the citizens?

By Zeus, he said, not easily. {{{ 375c }}}

And yet they need to be gentle toward their own people and harsh toward their enemies — otherwise they will not wait for others to destroy them, but will get there first and do it themselves.

True, he said.

What, then, I said, are we to do? Where will we find a temperament that is gentle and high-spirited at once? A gentle nature is, after all, more or less the opposite of a spirited one.

So it appears.

And yet if he lacks either one, he can never become a good guardian — and these two look impossible to combine, so {{{ 375d }}} it turns out a good guardian is impossible to produce.

So it seems, he said.

And I, being at a loss and looking back over what had gone before, said, With good reason, my friend, are we at a loss, for we have let go of the very image we set out with.

What do you mean?

We failed to notice that there are, after all, natures of a kind we did not suppose, which have both of these opposite qualities.

Where?

One could see it in other animals as well, and not least {{{ 375e }}} in the very creature we compared to the guardian. You know, I take it, that this is the natural temperament of well-bred dogs — to be as gentle as possible toward those they are used to and know, and just the opposite toward strangers.

I do know that.

This, then, I said, is possible after all, and we are not asking for something unnatural in wanting our guardian to be like this.

It doesn't seem so.

Then does it seem to you that our future guardian needs one thing more besides — that a philosophic nature be joined to his spirited one? {{{ 376a }}}

How so? So he asked — I do not follow.

This too, I said, you will see in dogs — a thing about the creature well worth marveling at.

What is that?

That when it sees someone it doesn't know, it turns fierce, even though it has suffered no wrong from him at all — but when it sees someone it knows, it greets him warmly, even if it has never received any good from him. Or have you never marveled at that?

Not really, he said — I hadn't given it that much thought before, but that it does this much is plain.

Well, this feature of its {{{ 376b }}} nature really does look refined, and truly philosophic.

In what way?

In this, I said — it marks off a friendly face from a hostile one by nothing other than knowing the one and not knowing the other. And yet how could a creature not be a lover of learning, when it draws the line between what is its own and what is alien by understanding and ignorance?

There is no way it could fail to be, he said.

But surely, I said, love of learning and philosophy are the same thing?

The same, yes, he said.

Then can't we confidently lay it down for man too, that if anyone is going to be gentle {{{ 376c }}} toward his own and those he knows, he must by nature be philosophic and a lover of learning?
