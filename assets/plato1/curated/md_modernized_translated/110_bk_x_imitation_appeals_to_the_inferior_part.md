## Imitation Appeals to the Inferior Part

{{{ 602c }}} By Zeus, I said, is this business of imitating not concerned with something a third remove from the truth? Is that not so?

Yes.

And in relation to what element in a human being does it have the power that it has?

What sort of thing do you mean?

This sort of thing: the same magnitude, I suppose, does not appear equal to us through sight when seen from nearby and from far off.

No, it doesn't.

And the same things appear bent and straight when seen in water and out of it, and concave and convex too, owing to the eye's wandering, again, over colors, and it is plain that this whole disturbance {{{ 602d }}} is present in our soul; and it is this very affection of our nature that scene-painting exploits and, in doing so, falls nothing short of sorcery, as do conjuring and the many other such devices.

True.

Then didn't measuring and counting and weighing prove to be the most welcome aids against these things, so that what rules in us is not what appears larger or smaller or more or heavier, but what has calculated and measured — or indeed weighed?

How could it be otherwise? {{{ 602e }}}

But this, at least, would be the work of the rational part in the soul.

Yes, of that.

But to this part, often, when it has measured and declared that some things are larger or smaller than others, or equal, the opposite appears at the same time about the same things.

Yes.

Now didn't we say that it is impossible for the same thing to hold contrary opinions about the same things at the same time?

And we were right to say so. {{{ 603a }}}

Then the part of the soul that judges contrary to measure could not be the same as the part that judges according to measure.

No, indeed it could not.

But surely the part that trusts in measure and calculation would be the best part of the soul.

Of course.

Then whatever opposes this must be one of the inferior things in us.

Necessarily.

Wanting, then, to secure agreement on this point, I was saying that painting, and imitation generally, produces its work far from the truth, and further, that it keeps company {{{ 603b }}} with that in us which is far from practical wisdom, and is its comrade and friend for no healthy or true purpose.

Absolutely, he said.

Then imitation, itself inferior, consorting with what is inferior, begets inferior offspring.

So it seems.

Which imitation, I said — the kind concerned only with sight, or also the kind concerned with hearing, which we call poetry?

Likely this one too, he said.

Then let us not rely, I said, on the mere likelihood we draw from painting, but let us go once more to {{{ 603c }}} that very part of the mind with which poetry's imitation keeps company, and see whether it is inferior or worth taking seriously.

We must indeed.

Let us set it out this way: we say that imitation imitates people performing actions, whether forced or voluntary, and, as a result of what they do, thinking they have fared well or badly, and in all this feeling either pain or joy. Was there anything besides this?

Nothing.

Is a person, then, of one mind in all these situations? {{{ 603d }}} Or, just as in the case of sight he was at odds with himself and held opposing opinions about the same things at the same time, does he likewise, in his actions, quarrel and fight with himself? But I recall that there is no need for us to reach agreement on this point now; for in our earlier discussions we agreed sufficiently on all this — that our soul is full of countless such contradictions occurring at once.

Rightly, he said.

Rightly so, I said; but what we left aside then now {{{ 603e }}} seems to me necessary to examine fully.

What is that? He said.

A decent man, I said, who meets with a misfortune of this kind — losing a son, say, or something else he values most — will bear it, as we said even then, I believe, more easily than other men.

Certainly.

But now let us examine this — whether he will feel no distress at all, or whether that is impossible, and he will instead be measured somehow in his grief.

That is closer to the truth, he said. {{{ 604a }}}

Now tell me this about him: do you think he will fight and resist his grief more when he is seen by his equals, or when he is by himself, alone in some deserted place?

There will be a great difference, he said, when he is seen.

But when he is alone, I imagine, he will dare to say many things that would shame him if someone heard him, and will do many things he would not want anyone to see him doing.

That is so, he said.

Now isn't it reason and law that bid him resist, {{{ 604b }}} while it is the suffering itself that draws him toward grief?

True.

And since opposite pulls arise in a person about the same thing at the same time, we say that these must be two things.

How could it be otherwise?

Is not one of them ready to obey the law, wherever the law directs?

How so?

The law says, I believe, that it is best to keep as calm as possible amid misfortunes and not to be indignant, since it is not clear what is good and what is bad in such things, nor does taking it hard get one anywhere, nor is anything {{{ 604c }}} human worthy of great seriousness, while grief stands in the way of the very thing that must come to our aid as quickly as possible in such cases.

What, he asked, do you mean by that?

I mean, I said, deliberating about what has happened, and — as in a throw of dice — arranging one's affairs according to how they have fallen, in whatever way reason determines would be best, rather than, like children who have stumbled, clinging to the hurt place and wasting time in crying out; but always habituating the soul to turn {{{ 604d }}} as quickly as possible to healing and setting right what has fallen and grown sick, banishing lament by the physician's craft.

That, at any rate, he said, would be the most correct way to face one's misfortunes.

Then, we say, the best part of us is willing to follow this reasoning.

Clearly.

And the part that draws us toward remembering our suffering and toward lamentation, and can never get enough of it, shall we not say is irrational, idle, and a friend of cowardice?

We shall indeed say so. {{{ 604e }}}

Then the indignant type surely admits of much and varied imitation, while the prudent and quiet character — being always nearly the same as itself — is neither easy to imitate, nor, when it is imitated, easy to grasp, especially for a festival crowd, men of all kinds gathered together in theaters; for the imitation, I suppose, is then of a feeling foreign to them. {{{ 605a }}}

Absolutely.

It is clear, then, that the imitative poet is not naturally suited to this part of the soul, and that his wisdom is not set on pleasing it, if he means to win a good reputation among the many, but is suited rather to the indignant and variable character, because it is easy to imitate.

Clearly.

So we might now justly lay hold of him, and set him down as the counterpart of the painter — for he resembles him both in producing work that is inferior with respect to truth, and in keeping company {{{ 605b }}} with a similarly inferior part of the soul rather than with the best part, and in this too he has become like him. And so we would now be right not to admit him into a city that is to be well governed, because he rouses this part of the soul and nourishes it, and by making it strong destroys the rational part — just as in a city, when someone makes wicked men powerful and hands the city over to them while ruining the more decent; in the same way, we shall say, the imitative poet implants a bad constitution privately in each person's soul, by gratifying the mindless part of it, {{{ 605c }}} which discerns neither the greater nor the less, but regards the same things now as great, now as small, fashioning images of images and standing very far indeed from the truth.
