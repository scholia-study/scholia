## Wealth, and Justice as Telling the Truth and Paying Debts

What you say is true, he said; for they do not accept it. And there is something in what they say, though not as much as they think; but Themistocles's retort is a good one — when the man from Seriphus was abusing him {{{ 330a }}} and saying that he was well regarded not on his own account but because of his city, he replied that neither would he himself have become famous had he been a Seriphian, nor would that man, had he been an Athenian. And for those who are not rich and find old age hard to bear, the same account holds good — that a decent man would not bear old age altogether easily along with poverty, nor would an indecent man, however rich, ever be at ease with himself.

And tell me, Cephalus, I said, of what you now possess, did you inherit most of it, or did you add to it yourself? {{{ 330b }}}

What would I have added to it, Socrates? he said. I have turned out to be a middling money-maker, somewhere between my grandfather and my father. For my grandfather, who shared my name, inherited about as much property as I now have and made it many times over; but my father Lysanias made it even smaller than it is now; and I am content if I leave to these sons of mine not less than I inherited, but a little more.

That is why I asked, I said, because you seemed to me not to be very {{{ 330c }}} fond of money — and this is generally true of those who have not made it themselves, while those who have made it are twice as attached to it as everyone else. For just as poets love their own poems, and fathers love their children, so too those who have made money take it seriously as their own achievement, and value it for its use besides, just as everyone else does. So they are hard company to keep, too, since they are willing to praise nothing but wealth.

What you say is true, he said. {{{ 330d }}}

Very true, I said. But tell me this much more — what do you think is the greatest good you have gained from possessing great wealth?

That, he said, is something I might not persuade many people of, were I to say it. For you may be sure, Socrates, he said, that when a man draws near to thinking he will die, fear and worry come over him about matters that never troubled him before. For the stories told about the underworld, that whoever has done wrong here must pay the penalty {{{ 330e }}} there — stories he laughed at until then — now twist his soul with the fear that they might be true; and he himself — whether from the weakness of old age, or perhaps because, being now nearer to what lies there, he sees a little more of it — grows full of suspicion and dread, and starts reckoning up and examining whether he has wronged anyone. So the man who finds many wrongs of his own in his life, waking often out of sleep as children do, is filled with fear {{{ 331a }}} and lives with a bad hope; but the man who is conscious of no injustice in himself has a sweet hope always at his side, and a good nurse for old age, as Pindar too says. For he put this gracefully indeed, Socrates — that for whoever leads his life justly and in reverence of the gods,
+ hope is sweet, and tends his heart,
+ a nurse for his old age, keeping him company —
+ hope, which beyond everything else steers
+ the ever-turning mind of mortals.
So he puts it well — remarkably well indeed. It is with this in view that I count the possession of money most valuable, not {{{ 331b }}} for every man, but for the decent and orderly one. For not deceiving or lying to anyone, even unwillingly, and not having to go off there in fear because one owes some sacrifice to a god or money to a man — to this the possession of money contributes a great deal. It has many other uses besides; but weighing one thing against another, I myself would count wealth most useful, for this purpose at least, to a man of sense, Socrates. {{{ 331c }}}

Beautifully put, Cephalus, I said. But as for this very thing, justice — shall we simply say it is nothing more than telling the truth and giving back whatever one has taken from another, or are even these very actions sometimes done justly and sometimes unjustly? For instance, take a case like this: everyone would surely agree that if a man took weapons from a friend who was at the time of sound mind, and that friend later went mad and demanded them back, one ought not to give such things back, and the one who did give them back would not be acting justly — nor, again, would a man be acting justly who was willing to tell the whole truth to someone in that condition. {{{ 331d }}}

You are right, he said.

Then this is not the definition of justice — to tell the truth and give back whatever one has taken.

It certainly is not, Socrates, said Polemarchus, breaking in — if indeed we are to trust Simonides at all.

And indeed, said Cephalus, I hand the argument over to you both; for I must now attend to the sacred rites.

Then am I not, said Polemarchus, the heir at least to what is yours?

Certainly, he said, laughing, and at once went off to the sacred rites.
