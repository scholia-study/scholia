## The People's Slavery

But indeed, he said, he does make use of such men.

And do these companions admire him, I said, and do the young citizens keep his company, while the decent ones hate him and avoid him?

Why would they not?

It is no wonder, I said, that tragedy in general is thought to be wise, and Euripides outstanding in it.

Why is that?

Because he too uttered a saying that holds close-packed thought, that {{{ 568b }}} tyrants are wise through the company of the wise. And he plainly meant that the wise are those whose company the tyrant keeps.

And how he praises tyranny as something godlike, he said, and many other things besides — both he and the other poets.

Accordingly, I said, the poets of tragedy, being wise, forgive both us and those who govern themselves in ways close to our own, because we will not admit them into our constitution, as singers of tyranny's praises.

I for my part think, he said, that those of them who are {{{ 568c }}} refined do forgive us.

But going around to the other cities, I think, gathering the crowds, hiring beautiful, loud, and persuasive voices, they drag constitutions toward tyrannies and democracies.

Very much so.

So they also receive pay for this and are honored — most of all, as is likely, by tyrants, and second by democracies; but the higher up they go toward {{{ 568d }}} the steep ascent of constitutions, the more their honor gives out, as if it were unable to go on for want of breath.

Certainly.

But indeed, I said, here we have wandered off the point; let us return again to that army of the tyrant — the fine, numerous, varied army, never the same — and ask from where it will be fed.

Clearly, he said, if there are sacred funds in the city, he will spend these, for as long as the proceeds of what has been sold off keep sufficing, compelling the people to pay in smaller contributions. {{{ 568e }}}

But what, when these run short?

Clearly, he said, he himself, and his drinking companions, and his male and female friends, will be fed from his father's estate.

I understand, I said — that the people who bred the tyrant will feed him and his companions.

He has no choice in it, he said.

What [then] do you mean? I said — suppose the people grow indignant and say that it is not just for a grown son to be supported by his father, but the reverse, for a father to be supported by his son, and that it was not for this reason that they {{{ 569a }}} bred him and set him up, so that, once he had grown great, they, enslaved to their own slaves, should then support him and the slaves together with a rabble of others, but rather so that, with him as their protector, they might be freed from the rich and from those called fine and good in the city, and now they bid him and his companions leave the city, just as a father drives a son out of the house along with troublesome drinking companions?

He will find out then, by Zeus, he said, what sort of {{{ 569b }}} creature it was that the people bred, embracing and rearing it, and that, being weaker, they are driving out one who is stronger.

How, I said, do you mean that? Will the tyrant dare to use force against his father, and even to strike him, if he will not obey?

Yes, he said, once he has taken his weapons away.

You are calling the tyrant, I said, a father-beater and a harsh keeper of his father's old age, and it seems that this, now, would be tyranny fully avowed, and, as the saying goes, the people, fleeing the smoke {{{ 569c }}} of slavery to free men, would have fallen into the fire of the mastery of slaves, having put on, in place of that great and unseasonable freedom, the harshest and bitterest slavery to slaves.

Yes indeed, he said, that is how it happens.

What then? I said — will it not be a fitting thing for us to say, if we claim to have gone through adequately how tyranny arises out of democracy, and, once it has come to be, what sort of thing it is?

Quite adequately, he said.
