## Whom Rule Serves, and Why the Decent Rule Reluctantly

{{{ 344d }}} Having said this, Thrasymachus meant to leave, like a bathman who had drenched our ears with a sudden, copious flood of speech; but the people present did not let him go — they made him stay and give an account of what he had said. And I too, for my part, begged him earnestly and said: My dear Thrasymachus, what sort of argument is this that you mean to hurl at us and then leave, before teaching us properly, or learning for yourself, whether things {{{ 344e }}} stand this way or some other? Or do you think you are undertaking to settle some small matter — the way a whole life is to be led, the course by which each of us would live to the greatest profit?

Why, said Thrasymachus, do you think I believe it is any different?

You seem so, I said — seem, at any rate, to care nothing for us, and to give no thought to whether we shall live worse or better for our ignorance of what you claim to know. But, my good fellow, do take the trouble to show it to us as well — {{{ 345a }}} surely it will do you no harm to confer this benefit on so many of us — for I will tell you my own view: that I am not convinced, nor do I think, that injustice is more profitable than justice, not even if one lets it alone and does not prevent it from doing what it wishes. But, my good fellow, let him be unjust, and let him be able to do injustice either by escaping notice or by fighting it out openly — still he does not persuade me that it is more profitable than justice. Perhaps, then, {{{ 345b }}} someone else among us feels this too, and not only I; so persuade us adequately, my good man, that we are wrong to prize justice more than injustice.

And how, he said, shall I persuade you? For if you are not persuaded by what I just said, what more shall I do for you? Or shall I carry the argument and thrust it into your soul?

By Zeus, I said, don't you do any such thing; but first, stand by whatever you say, or if you change your position, change it openly and do not deceive us. But now you see, Thrasymachus — for {{{ 345c }}} let us look again at what came before — that although you began by defining the true doctor with precision, you no longer thought you had to keep to the true shepherd afterward with the same precision, but you think that, insofar as he is a shepherd, he fattens the sheep not with a view to what is best for the sheep, but — as though he were some banqueter about to feast — with a view to good cheer, or again with a view to {{{ 345d }}} selling them, like a moneymaker rather than a shepherd. But the shepherd's craft, surely, cares for nothing but the thing it is set over, and how to provide what is best for that — since its own needs, all that it takes for it to be at its best, are surely provided for well enough, so long as it falls short in nothing of being the shepherd's craft; and so I, for my part, thought we were bound to agree just now that every kind of rule, insofar as it is rule, seeks what is best for nothing other than the thing {{{ 345e }}} ruled and cared for, in public office and in private alike. But do you think that the rulers in our cities — the true rulers — rule willingly?

No, by Zeus, he said — I know it for a fact.

What then, I said, Thrasymachus? Do you not notice, concerning the other offices, that no one is willing to rule of his own accord, but they demand pay, on the ground that the benefit from ruling will come not to themselves but to {{{ 346a }}} those ruled? For tell me this much: do we not say that each of the crafts is different from the others in just this, in having a different power? And, my good man, do not answer contrary to your real opinion, so that we may actually get somewhere.

It is different in just that, he said.

Then does not each of these provide us with some benefit of its own, and not a common one — medicine health, for instance, and the captain's craft safety in sailing, and the rest likewise?

Certainly. {{{ 346b }}}

Then does not wage-earning provide wages? For that is its own power; or do you call medicine and the captain's craft one and the same? Or, if you are willing to define things precisely, as you proposed, will you call the captain's craft medicine any the more, just because someone at the helm grows healthy because sailing on the sea does him good?

Certainly not, he said.

Nor, I imagine, wage-earning, if someone stays healthy while earning wages.

Certainly not.

What then? Would you call medicine the craft of wage-earning, if someone earns wages while healing? {{{ 346c }}}

No, he said.

Then did we not agree that the benefit of each craft is its own, peculiar to it?

Granted, he said.

Then whatever benefit all craftsmen enjoy in common, it is clear that they get it by making additional use of some one and the same thing.

So it seems, he said.

And we say that the benefit craftsmen get by earning wages comes to them from this additional practice of the craft of wage-earning.

He agreed, reluctantly. {{{ 346d }}}

Then this benefit — the receiving of wages — does not come to each from his own craft; but, if we must examine the matter precisely, medicine produces health, while wage-earning produces wages, and building produces a house, while the wage-earning that accompanies it produces wages, and all the others likewise: each of them performs its own work and benefits that for which it is appointed. But if no wage accrues to it besides, is there anything the craftsman is benefited by from the craft itself?

It does not appear so, he said. {{{ 346e }}}

Then does he not still confer a benefit, whenever he works for nothing?

I think he does.

Then this, Thrasymachus, is now clear: that no craft or rule provides what is beneficial to itself, but, as we said long ago, it provides and prescribes what is beneficial to that which is ruled, looking to the advantage of the weaker, and not that of the stronger. For these reasons, then, dear Thrasymachus, I was just now saying that no one is willing to rule of his own accord and take in hand the troubles of others to set them right, {{{ 347a }}} but instead demands wages, because the one who means to practice his craft well never does or prescribes what is best for himself, when he prescribes according to the craft, but rather what is best for the one ruled; and it is for these reasons, it seems, that there must be wages available for those who are to be willing to rule — either money or honor, or a penalty if they do not rule.

What do you mean by this, Socrates? Glaucon said: I recognize the two wages you mean, well enough — but I did not understand what penalty you are speaking of, or how you meant it as a kind of wage.

Then you do not understand, I said, the wage of the best men, through which {{{ 347b }}} the most decent rule, whenever they are willing to rule. Or do you not know that being fond of honor and fond of money is called, and is, a reproach?

I do, he said.

For these reasons, then, I said, the good are willing to rule neither for money nor for honor; for they do not wish to be called hirelings by openly taking pay for ruling, nor thieves by secretly taking it from their office. Nor again for the sake of honor; for they are not lovers of honor. So there must be {{{ 347c }}} compulsion and a penalty upon them, if they are going to be willing to rule at all — which is presumably why it has come to be considered shameful to go after ruling willingly, instead of waiting to be compelled — and the greatest of these penalties is being ruled by someone worse than oneself, if a man himself is unwilling to rule; and it is fear of this, it seems to me, that makes decent men rule, when they do rule, and then they go to their ruling not as though going to something good, nor as expecting to fare well in it, but {{{ 347d }}} as to something necessary, and because they have no one better than, or even equal to, themselves to whom they can hand it over. For if a city of good men should come to be, it is likely that men would fight to avoid ruling, just as they now fight to rule, and there it would become clear that a true ruler by nature does not seek his own advantage but that of the one ruled; so that everyone who knew this would rather choose to be benefited by another than to take on the trouble of benefiting another. This much, then, I for my part in no way concede to {{{ 347e }}} Thrasymachus, that the just is the advantage of the stronger. But this we shall examine again another time; what Thrasymachus is now saying seems to me a far greater matter — that the life of the unjust is better than that of the just. Which of the two, then, I said, Glaucon, do you choose? And which do you think is spoken more truly?

I, for my part, hold that the life of the just is more profitable.
