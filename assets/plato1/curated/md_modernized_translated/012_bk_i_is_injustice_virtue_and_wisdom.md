## Is Injustice Virtue and Wisdom?

{{{ 348a }}} Did you hear, I said, how many good things Thrasymachus just now ran through on behalf of the unjust man?

I heard, he said, but I am not convinced.

Do you want us, then, to persuade him, if we can somehow find a way, that what he says is not true?

How could I not want to? So he said.

If, then, I said, we set ourselves against him and match speech with speech, listing all the good things that belong to being just, and then he makes another speech, and we another, we will have to count up {{{ 348b }}} the good things and measure how many each of us claims in each speech, and at that point we will need some judges to decide between us; but if we instead examine the matter as we were just doing, reasoning our way toward agreement with each other, we will ourselves be both judges and advocates at once.

Certainly, he said.

Whichever way pleases you, then, I said.

That way, he said.

Come now, Thrasymachus, I said, answer us from the beginning. Do you say that complete injustice is more profitable than complete justice? {{{ 348c }}}

Certainly I do say so, he said, and I have stated my reasons.

Come then, what do you say about the following point regarding them? Do you call the one virtue, and the other vice?

How could I not?

Justice, then, is virtue, and injustice vice?

That's likely, he said, my sweet fellow, since I do say that injustice is profitable and justice is not.

But what then?

The opposite, he said.

Is justice vice, then?

No, but a very noble kind of naivety. {{{ 348d }}}

So you call injustice malice?

No, but good judgment, he said.

Do the unjust seem to you, Thrasymachus, to be both prudent and good?

Those, he said, who are completely capable of injustice — the ones able to bring whole cities and peoples under their own power; but you perhaps think I mean the men who cut purses. Such things are profitable too, he said, provided they go unnoticed; but they are not worth mentioning next to what I was just talking about. {{{ 348e }}}

As for that, I said, I am not unaware of what you mean, but I was astonished at this: that you place injustice on the side of virtue and wisdom, and justice among their opposites.

But that is exactly how I place it.

This, I said, is a harder position now, my friend, and it is no longer easy to know what to say to it. For if you had claimed that injustice is profitable, but still admitted, as some others do, that it is a vice or shameful, we would have had something to say, arguing on the basis of ordinary belief; but as it is, you clearly mean to say that it is both admirable and strong, and you will attach to it all the other qualities {{{ 349a }}} that we were attaching to the just man, since you have dared to place it within virtue and wisdom.

You guess quite rightly, he said.

But still, I said, one must not shrink from pursuing the argument through to the end and examining it, so long as I take you to be saying what you really think. For it seems to me, Thrasymachus, that right now you are genuinely not joking, but saying what you believe to be true.

What difference does it make to you, he said, whether I believe it or not, instead of refuting the argument itself? {{{ 349b }}}

None, I said. But try to answer me this as well: does it seem to you that the just man would want to have more than another just man?

Not at all, he said; otherwise he would not be as charming and naive as he now is.

What about this? Than a just action?

Not than that either, he said.

But would he think it fitting to outdo an unjust man, and consider that just, or would he not think so?

He would think so, he said, and think it fitting, but he would not be able to.

But that is not what I am asking, I said, but whether the just man does not think it fitting, nor wants, {{{ 349c }}} to have more than a just man, but does want more than an unjust one?

Yes, that is how it is, he said.

But what about the unjust man? Does he think it fitting to outdo the just man, and the just action?

How could he not? He said — is he not the one who thinks it fitting to have more than everyone?

So the unjust man will also outdo an unjust person and an unjust action, and will strive to take the most for himself out of everything?

That is so.

Let us put it this way, I said: the just man does not outdo his like, but does outdo his unlike, while the unjust man outdoes both his like and {{{ 349d }}} his unlike?

You have put it excellently, he said.

And the unjust man, I said, is prudent and good, while the just man is neither?

That too is well put, he said.

So then, I said, the unjust man is also like the prudent and the good, while the just man is not like them?

How could he not, he said, being such a man himself, resemble such men, while the other, not being such, does not resemble them?

Good. Each of them, then, is the same sort as those he resembles?

But what else would you expect? So he said.

Well then, Thrasymachus: do you speak of one man as musical, and another {{{ 349e }}} as unmusical?

I do.

Which of the two is prudent, and which imprudent?

The musical man, surely, is prudent, and the unmusical man imprudent.

And is he not good in whatever respect he is prudent, and bad in whatever respect he is imprudent?

Yes.

And what about the medical man? Is it not the same?

The same.

Does it seem to you, then, my excellent fellow, that a musical man tuning a lyre would want to outdo another musical man in the tightening and loosening of the strings, or think it fitting to have more than he does?

Not to me.

What about this? Than an unmusical one?

That is necessary, he said. {{{ 350a }}}

And what about the medical man? In eating or drinking, would he want to outdo a medical man in anything — whether the man himself or the matter at hand?

Certainly not.

But one who is not medical?

Yes.

Now consider, regarding every kind of knowledge and lack of knowledge, whether it seems to you that any person with knowledge at all would want to choose to do or say more than another person with knowledge, rather than the same as his like in the same action.

Perhaps, he said, this at least must be so.

And what about the one without knowledge? Would he not outdo {{{ 350b }}} the one who knows just as much as the one who does not?

Perhaps.

And the one with knowledge is wise?

I say so.

And the wise man is good?

I say so.

The good and wise man, then, will not want to outdo his like, but only the unlike and the opposite.

So it seems, he said.

But the bad and ignorant man will want to outdo both his like and his opposite.

So it appears.

So then, Thrasymachus, I said, our unjust man outdoes both his unlike and his like? Or is that not what you were saying?

I was, he said. {{{ 350c }}}

And the just man will not outdo his like, but will outdo his unlike?

Yes.

The just man, then, I said, resembles the wise and good, and the unjust man resembles the bad and ignorant.

Probably.

But surely we agreed that each is such as the one he resembles.

We did agree.

The just man, then, has been shown to be good and wise, and the unjust man ignorant and bad.

Thrasymachus, then, agreed to all of this — not {{{ 350d }}} as smoothly as I now recount it, but dragged into it and only with difficulty, sweating an astonishing amount, since it was summer besides (and it was then, for the first time, that I saw Thrasymachus blush) — and once we had agreed together that justice is virtue and wisdom, and injustice vice and ignorance, well then, I said, let this stand so for us; and we also said that injustice is strong. Or do you not remember, Thrasymachus?
