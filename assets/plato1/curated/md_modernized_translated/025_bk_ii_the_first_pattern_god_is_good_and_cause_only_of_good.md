## The First Pattern: God Is Good, and Cause Only of Good

Rightly, he said; but this is the very question — what would the patterns concerning theology be?

Something like these, I said — whatever sort the god actually is, that is surely always how he must be represented, whether one composes about him in epic, in lyric, or in tragedy.

Yes, one must. {{{ 379b }}}

Is the god not in truth good, and must he not be spoken of as such?

How could he not?

But surely nothing good is harmful — is it?

It does not seem so to me.

Does then what is not harmful cause harm?

Not at all.

And does what causes no harm do anything bad?

Not that either.

And could what does nothing bad be the cause of anything bad?

How could it?

And what of this? Is the good beneficial?

Yes.

It is the cause of well-being, then?

Yes.

The good, then, is not the cause of everything, but is the cause of things that go well, while it bears no responsibility for the bad. {{{ 379c }}}

Entirely so, he said.

Then, I said, the god, since he is good, would not be the cause of all things, as most people say, but is the cause of few things for human beings, and not the cause of many — for the good things that come to us are far fewer than the bad, and for the good things no other cause is to be assigned, but for the bad things some other causes must be sought, and not the god.

You seem to me to be speaking most truly, he said.

Then, I said, we must not accept, either from Homer or {{{ 379d }}} from any other poet, this error about the gods, when he errs senselessly and says—
+ that two jars stand upon the threshold of Zeus,
+ filled with dooms, one full of good, the other of evil;
and to whomever Zeus gives, mixing from both,
+ at one time he meets with evil, at another with good;
but to whomever he does not, but gives the other unmixed,
+ him a harsh hunger drives across the shining earth;
{{{ 379e }}} nor that Zeus has been made for us a steward—
+ of good things and of bad things.
But the breaking of oaths and truces, which Pandarus broke, we shall not approve if anyone says it came about through Athena and Zeus, nor the strife and judgment of the gods {{{ 380a }}} through Themis and Zeus, nor again, as Aeschylus says, must the young be allowed to hear that—
+ god plants guilt in mortals,
+ whenever he wishes to ruin a house utterly.
But if anyone composes poetry containing these iambic lines — the sufferings of Niobe, or those of the house of Pelops, or the Trojan sufferings, or anything else of the kind — either we must not allow these to be called the works of a god, or, if they are a god's work, the poet must work out for them nearly the account we are now seeking, and {{{ 380b }}} must say that the god was doing what was just and good, and that those punished were benefited by it; but that those paying the penalty were wretched, and that it was a god who did this, the poet must not be allowed to say. But if they were to say that the wicked were wretched because they needed punishment, and that in paying the penalty they were benefited by the god, this may be allowed; but that a god, being good, becomes for anyone the cause of evils, we must fight against in every way — no one is to say this in his own city, if it is to be well governed, {{{ 380c }}} and no one is to hear it, younger or older, whether the tale is told in verse or without it, since such things, if said, would be neither pious, nor advantageous to us, nor consistent with themselves.

I am with you on this law, he said, and it pleases me.

This, then, I said, would be one of the laws and patterns concerning the gods, by which both speakers must speak and poets must compose: that the god is the cause not of everything, but only of what is good.

Quite so, he said — that is enough.
