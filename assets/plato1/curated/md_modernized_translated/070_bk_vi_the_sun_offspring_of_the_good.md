## The Sun, Offspring of the Good

Necessarily, he said. But you yourself, Socrates, do you say that the good is knowledge, or pleasure, or something else besides these?

You there, my good man, I said, it has for a long time now been obvious that what satisfies other people about these matters would not satisfy you.

For it does not seem right to me either, Socrates, he said, that someone who has worked at these questions for so {{{ 506c }}} long should be able to report what others hold, but not what he holds himself.

And what of that? I said — do you think it right to speak as if one knew about things one does not know?

Not at all as one who knows, he said — but as one who supposes, wishing to say what he believes.

And what of that? I said — have you not noticed that opinions without knowledge are all ugly? The best of them are blind — or do men who hold something true but have no intellect strike you as any different from blind men who take the right road?

Not at all, he said.

Do you want, then, to look at ugly things, blind and crooked, when it's possible {{{ 506d }}} to hear bright and beautiful ones from others?

In the name of Zeus, Socrates, said Glaucon, do not stop now, just when you seem to be at the end. For it will satisfy us if you go through the good the same way you went through justice and moderation and the rest.

It will certainly satisfy me too, my friend, I said — but I'm afraid I won't be able to, and in my eagerness I'll make a fool of myself and provoke laughter. But, my blessed friends, let us for now leave aside just what the good itself {{{ 506e }}} is — for it seems to me more than the present impulse can reach, even to arrive at what I now think about it — but I am willing to speak about what appears to be the offspring of the good and most like it, if that is agreeable to you; if not, we shall let it go.

Well, speak, he said — you can pay back the account of the father another time. {{{ 507a }}}

I would wish, I said, to be able to pay it back myself, and for you to receive it in full, rather than, as now, only the interest. But receive, then, this interest and offspring of the good itself. Be careful, though, that I don't somehow deceive you without meaning to, paying out a counterfeit account of the interest.

We will be careful, he said, as far as we can — just speak.

Only once I've come to an agreement with you, I said, and reminded you of what was said before, and of what has already been said many times on other occasions. {{{ 507b }}}

What sort of things? So he asked.

There are many beautiful things, I said, and many good things, and we say that each set of them is so, and we distinguish them by an account.

We do indeed say that.

And also the beautiful itself and the good itself — and in the same way, concerning everything we then posited as many, we turn back and, positing a single form for each on the assumption that it is one, address each as what it is.

That is so.

And we say that the many are seen but not thought, while the forms, in turn, are thought but not seen.

Absolutely. {{{ 507c }}}

With what part of ourselves, then, do we see the things that are seen?

With sight, he said.

And is it not, I said, by hearing that we hear the things that are heard, and by the other senses all the things that are perceptible?

Of course.

Have you noticed, then, I said, how the craftsman of the senses made the power of seeing and being seen by far the most costly of all?

Not really, he said.

Well, consider it this way. Is there anything else, of a different kind, that hearing and voice need in addition, so that the one may hear and the other be heard — which, {{{ 507d }}} if it isn't present as a third thing, the one won't hear and the other won't be heard?

Of nothing, he said.

And I think, I said, that many of the other senses too — not to say none of them — need nothing of this kind. Or can you name one?

I certainly cannot, he said.

But don't you realize that sight and the visible do need something?

How so?

Sight being present in the eyes, I said, and the one who has it trying to use it, and color being present in the objects — unless a third kind of thing, whose nature is specifically fitted for this very purpose, {{{ 507e }}} is also present — you know that sight will see nothing, and the colors will remain invisible.

And what, he said, is this thing you mean?

What you call light, I said.

What you say is true, he said.

It is in no small measure, then, that the sense of seeing and the power of being {{{ 508a }}} seen have been yoked together by a more honored yoke than the other pairings, if indeed light is not without honor.

But indeed, he said, it's far from being without honor.

Which of the gods in heaven, then, can you point to as responsible for this, whose light lets our sight see in the best way, and the things seen be seen?

The very one you would name, he said, and everyone else too — for clearly you're asking about the sun.

Is sight, then, related by nature to this god in the following way?

How do you mean?

Sight itself is not the sun, nor is that in which it comes to be — {{{ 508b }}} what we call the eye.

No, indeed not.

But I think it is the most sunlike of the organs concerned with the senses.

By far.

And doesn't it possess the power it has as something dispensed from the sun, like an inflow?

Certainly.

Then is it not also true of the sun, that it is not sight, but, being the cause of sight, is seen by this very sight?

That is so, he said.

Say, then, I said, that this is what I mean by the offspring of the good, which the good begot as its analogue: what the good itself is, {{{ 508c }}} in the intelligible region, in relation to intellect and the things thought, this same thing is, in the visible region, in relation to sight and the things seen.

How do you mean? Go through it for me still further, he said.

Eyes, I said — you know that when someone turns them no longer toward things whose colors the light of day covers, but toward things lit by the gleams of night, they grow dim and seem nearly blind, as if clear sight were not present in them?

Very much so, he said. {{{ 508d }}}

But when, I think, they turn toward things the sun shines on, they see clearly, and the same eyes plainly have sight present in them.

Of course.

In just this way, then, think of the soul too: when it fixes itself on that which is illuminated by truth and what is, it apprehends and knows it and appears to possess intellect; but when it fixes on that which is mixed with darkness — that which comes to be and passes away — it forms opinion and grows dim, shifting its opinions up and down, and again resembles something without intellect.

So it does resemble that. {{{ 508e }}}

Say, then, that this thing — which supplies truth to the things known, and renders to the knower his power — is the form of the good; and conceive of it as the cause of knowledge and of truth, and as itself something known; and, beautiful though both of these are, knowledge and truth, you will judge rightly in holding that it is other than they, and still more beautiful than they; and as for knowledge {{{ 509a }}} and truth: just as there it was right to think light and sight sunlike, but not right to hold either to be the sun, so here too it is right to think both of these goodlike, but not right to hold either of them to be the good — rather, the condition of the good must be honored still more highly.

You are describing an inconceivable beauty, he said, if it provides knowledge and truth, and yet is itself beyond these in beauty — for surely you don't mean that it is pleasure.

Hush, I said — rather, go on examining its image still further in this way. {{{ 509b }}}

How do you mean?

You will say, I think, that the sun provides the things that are seen not only with the power to be seen, but also with their becoming and growth and nourishment, though it is not itself becoming.

How could it not?

Say, then, likewise, that for the things known, not only is their being known granted by the good, but their existence and their being are added to them by it as well, though the good itself is not being — rather, it stands still beyond being, surpassing it in seniority and power. {{{ 509c }}}

And Glaucon, quite comically, said, by Apollo, what a supernatural extravagance.

You are to blame, I said, for forcing me to say what I think about it.

And do not stop at all, he said — if nothing else, at least go through the likeness to the sun again, in case you are leaving something out.

Well, I said, I'm certainly leaving out a great deal.

Then don't leave out even a small part, he said.

I think, I said, that I'll leave out a great deal — but still, as much as is possible for now, I won't willingly leave anything out.

Please don't, he said.
