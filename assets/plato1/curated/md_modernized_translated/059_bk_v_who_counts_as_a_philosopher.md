## Who Counts as a Philosopher

It would be time, he said, to define it.

Come then, follow me this way, if we can somehow explain it adequately.

Lead on, he said.

Then will I need to remind you, I said, or do you remember that when we say someone loves something, he must be shown, if the claim is to be made correctly, not loving part of it and not caring for another part, but cherishing it whole? {{{ 474d }}}

It seems, he said, that I do need reminding, for I do not quite follow.

It would suit someone else, Glaucon, I said, to say what you are saying, but it does not suit a man given to erotic love to forget that all those in their bloom sting and stir the lover of boys and the erotic man in some way or other, seeming to them worth attention and embrace. Or do you not all behave this way toward the beautiful? One, because he is snub-nosed, is called charming and praised by you, another's hooked nose you say is kingly, the one in between {{{ 474e }}} you say has the most measured look, dark ones you find manly to see, pale ones you call children of the gods, and as for honey-pale, do you think that name too is anyone's invention but a lover's, coining a pet-name and finding the sallowness easy to bear, if it belongs to one in bloom? And in a word you all fabricate every excuse {{{ 475a }}} and let loose every phrase, so as to let none of those in flower slip away.

If you want to use me as your example, he said, in saying that lovers behave this way, I grant it for the sake of the argument.

What about this? I said, do you not see wine-lovers doing these same things? Welcoming every kind of wine, on any pretext at all?

Very much so.

And surely you see, I think, that lovers of honor, if they cannot be generals, become captains of a regiment, and if they cannot be honored {{{ 475b }}} by the great and the distinguished, are content to be honored by the small and the trivial, since what they desire is honor as such.

Quite so.

Tell me this, then, yes or no: whenever we say someone has an appetite for something, shall we say he desires the whole of that kind, or part of it and not another part?

The whole, he said.

Then shall we not say that the philosopher too desires wisdom — not part of it and not another part, but the whole of it?

True.

So we shall not call someone who is squeamish about learning, especially {{{ 475c }}} while young and not yet able to give an account of what is useful and what is not, a lover of learning or a philosopher, just as we do not say that someone squeamish about food is hungry or desires food, and call him not a lover of food but someone with a poor appetite for it.

And we shall be right to say so.

But the one who readily wants to taste every kind of learning, who goes to studying gladly and is insatiable for it, him we shall rightly call a philosopher, shall we not? {{{ 475d }}}

And Glaucon said, then you will end up with a great many strange people of this kind. For all the lovers of sights seem to me to be like this in their delight at learning by observation, and the lovers of hearing are among the strangest of all to count as philosophers, men who would not willingly go near a discussion or that kind of pastime, but who, as if they had hired out their ears to listen, run around to every chorus at the Dionysia, missing none, neither in the cities nor in the villages. Then shall we call all of these, and others who are eager learners of such things, {{{ 475e }}} along with the devotees of petty crafts, philosophers?

Not at all, I said, but similar to philosophers.

And the genuine ones, he said, whom do you mean?

The lovers of the sight of truth, I said.

That much is right, he said, but what exactly do you mean by it?

Not easily, I said, could I put it to anyone else, but I think you will agree with me on the following.

What is that?

Since the beautiful is opposite to the ugly, they are two. {{{ 476a }}}

How could they not be?

Then since they are two, is each of them also one?

That too.

And concerning the just and the unjust, the good and the bad, and all the forms as well, the same account holds: each is itself one, but because they appear everywhere in combination with actions, with bodies, and with one another, each of them appears to be many.

You are right, he said.

This, then, I said, is how I draw the division: on one side those you were just now calling lovers of sights, lovers of crafts, and practical men, {{{ 476b }}} and on the other, separately, those we are actually discussing, whom alone one could rightly call philosophers.

How, he said, do you mean?

The lovers of hearing and the lovers of sights, I said, embrace beautiful sounds, colors, and shapes, and everything crafted out of such things, but their minds are incapable of seeing and embracing the nature of the beautiful itself.

Yes, that is certainly how it is, he said.

But those who are able to go to the beautiful itself and see it in itself, would they not be rare? {{{ 476c }}}

Very rare indeed.

So the one who believes in beautiful things but neither believes in the beautiful itself nor, if someone leads him toward knowledge of it, is able to follow, does he seem to you to be living in a dream or awake? Consider it. Is not this just what dreaming is, whether asleep or awake, taking what is merely like a thing to be not like it but the very thing it resembles?

I, at any rate, he said, would say that such a person is dreaming.

But what of this? The one who, in contrast to this, believes there is something beautiful itself {{{ 476d }}} and is able to see both it and the things that share in it, and who believes neither that the things sharing in it are it, nor that it is the things sharing in it, does this person, in turn, seem to you to be living awake or in a dream?
