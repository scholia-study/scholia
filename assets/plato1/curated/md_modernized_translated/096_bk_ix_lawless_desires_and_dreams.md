## Lawless Desires, and Dreams

{{{ 571a }}} It remains, then, I said, to consider the tyrannical man himself — how he changes over from the democratic man, and once he has come to be, what sort of man he is and how he lives, wretched or happy.

Yes, he is the one still left, he said.

Do you know, then, I said, what I still find myself missing?

What is it?

It seems to me that we have not adequately distinguished the desires — what kinds there are and how many. And while this is left wanting, the inquiry we are conducting will {{{ 571b }}} be less clear.

Well then, he said, are we not still in good time?

Very much so — and do consider what I want us to see in them. It is this. Among the unnecessary pleasures and desires, some seem to me to be lawless — desires that are liable to arise in everyone, but which, checked by the laws and by the better desires with the help of reason, are in some men done away with altogether, or left few and weak, while {{{ 571c }}} in others they are stronger and more numerous.

Which ones do you mean, he asked?

The ones that wake in sleep, I said — whenever the rest of the soul is asleep, all that is rational and gentle and rules over the other part, but the beastly and wild part, gorged with food or drink, rears up and, having thrown off sleep, seeks to go and satisfy its own dispositions; you know that in such a state it dares to do everything, as though released and freed from all shame and practical wisdom. For it does not shrink from attempting, as it supposes, {{{ 571d }}} to have intercourse with its mother, or with anyone else at all — man, god, or beast — from committing any bloody murder, or from touching any food whatsoever; and in a word, it stops short at no folly and no shamelessness.

Most true, he said.

But when, I think, a man is in a healthy and moderate state with himself, and goes to sleep having roused the rational part of himself and feasted it on fine arguments and reflections, having {{{ 571e }}} arrived at communion with himself, and having given the appetitive part neither want nor excess, so that it may settle and not trouble the best part {{{ 572a }}} with either joy or pain, but leave it alone by itself, pure, to look about and reach out to perceive something it does not know — whether of the past, the present, or even the future — and likewise having calmed the spirited part, and not going to sleep with his temper stirred from having fallen into anger with someone, but having quieted those two parts and stirred up the third, in which practical wisdom arises, and so takes his rest — you know that in such a state he grasps truth most of all, and then the visions {{{ 572b }}} appearing in his dreams are least lawless.
