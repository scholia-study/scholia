## Astronomy and Harmonics

You are right, he said.

Then, I said, let us set down astronomy as a fourth study, assuming that the study now neglected will be there for us, if a city pursues it.

That is reasonable, he said. And the very point you rebuked me for just now, Socrates — praising astronomy crudely — I now praise it in the way you take it up {{{ 529a }}}; for it seems clear to everyone that this at least compels the soul to look upward and leads it from the things here to that place.

Perhaps, I said, it is clear to everyone except me; for it does not seem so to me.

But how so? So he asked.

The way those who lead people up toward philosophy handle it now makes the soul look very much downward.

What, he said, do you mean?

You seem to me, I said, to take no mean view in your own mind of what the study of things above is; for you are apt to suppose that if a person craned his neck to gaze at decorations on a ceiling and made out something there, {{{ 529b }}} he would be studying it with reason and not with his eyes. Perhaps, then, you think rightly, and I foolishly. For I, in turn, am unable to consider anything else as making the soul look upward except that study which concerns being and the invisible; and if someone, gaping upward or squinting downward, tries to learn any of the things perceived by the senses, I say he never learns it at all — for he has no knowledge {{{ 529c }}} of such things — and that his soul looks not upward but downward, even if he does his learning while swimming on his back on land or in the sea.

I get my due, he said; for you have rightly rebuked me. But how, then, were you saying astronomy ought to be learned, contrary to how they now learn it, if they are to learn it usefully for the purposes we speak of?

Like this, I said. These ornaments in the heaven, since they are wrought in what is visible, must be regarded, I think, as the finest {{{ 529d }}} and most exact of such things, yet they fall far short of the true ones — the motions by which the true speed and the true slowness, in true number and in every true figure, move in relation to one another and carry along the things within them, which are graspable by argument and understanding but not by sight — or do you think otherwise?

Not at all, he said.

Then, I said, the ornamentation of the heaven must be used as patterns for the sake of learning those true things, just as one would {{{ 529e }}} if one came upon diagrams drawn and finished with special care by Daedalus or some other craftsman or painter. For someone experienced in geometry, seeing such things, would surely think them finest in point of workmanship, but would think it ridiculous to examine them in earnest as if to grasp from them the truth {{{ 530a }}} about equals or doubles or some other proportion.

Why should it not be ridiculous? So he asked.

Do you not think, I said, that a true astronomer, when he looks at the motions of the stars, will feel the same thing? He will believe that the craftsman of the heaven composed it and the things within it as finely as such works could possibly be composed; but as for the proportion of night to day, of these to a month, of a month to a year, and of the other stars {{{ 530b }}} to these and to one another, do you not think he will find it strange for anyone to hold that these things, having body and being visible, come to be forever in the same way and vary in no way at all, and to seek by every means to grasp the truth about them?

It seems so to me at least, he said, now that I hear you.

Then, by means of problems, I said, we shall pursue astronomy just as we pursue geometry, and we shall let the things in the heaven be, if, by genuinely partaking of astronomy, we are to make useful {{{ 530c }}} the naturally sensible part of the soul, instead of useless.

Truly, he said, you are prescribing a task many times greater than astronomy as it is now practiced.

And I think, I said, that we shall prescribe the other subjects too in the same way, if we are to be of any use as lawgivers. But can you remind me of any of the appropriate studies?

I cannot, he said, not offhand like this.

Motion, however, I said, presents not one kind but several, {{{ 530d }}} as I suppose. Now perhaps some wise person will be able to name them all; but those evident even to us are two.

Which are they?

Besides this one, I said, its counterpart.

Which one?

It is likely, I said, that just as the eyes are fixed on astronomy, so the ears are fixed on harmonic motion, and that these branches of knowledge are in some way sisters to one another, as the Pythagoreans say and as we, Glaucon, agree. Or what do we say?

Just so, he said. {{{ 530e }}}

Then, since the task is a large one, I said, we shall inquire of them how they speak about these things, and whether there is anything else besides; but throughout all this we shall guard what is our own.

What is that?

That those we are rearing should never attempt to learn any of these subjects incompletely, falling short of the point to which everything must be carried, as we were just now saying about astronomy. Or do you not know that {{{ 531a }}} they do another such thing regarding harmonics as well? For measuring the audible concords and sounds against one another, they toil endlessly, just like the astronomers.

By the gods, he said, and ridiculously too — naming certain close-packed intervals and pressing their ears close, as if hunting for a voice from next door, some claiming still to hear a note in between and that this is the smallest interval by which one must measure, others disputing this and insisting the notes already sound alike — both sides {{{ 531b }}} setting their ears before their intellect.

You mean, I said, the good fellows who give the strings trouble and torture them, twisting them on the pegs; but so that the image does not grow too long, with the blows of the plectrum and the accusation, denial, and boasting of the strings, I break off the image and say that these are not the ones I mean, but those whom we said just now we would question about harmonics. For they do the same thing as those in {{{ 531c }}} astronomy; for they seek the numbers in these audible concords, but they do not rise to problems, to examine which numbers are concordant and which are not, and why each is so.

That is a marvelous thing you speak of, he said.

Useful indeed, I said, for the search after the beautiful and the good, but pursued in any other way, useless.

That is likely, he said.

And I think, I said, that the method of all these subjects we have gone through {{{ 531d }}}, if it arrives at their community and kinship with one another, and draws the conclusion how they are akin, carries us some way toward what we want, and the labor is not spent in vain; but if not, it is in vain.
