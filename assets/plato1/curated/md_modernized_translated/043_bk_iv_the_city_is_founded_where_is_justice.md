## The City Is Founded: Where Is Justice?

{{{ 427a }}} And indeed, he said, that is exactly what they do.

Well then, I said, concerning this kind of law and constitution I did not think the true lawgiver needed to trouble himself, in either a badly or a well-governed city — in the one because it is useless and achieves nothing more, in the other because some of these things anyone at all could discover, and others follow automatically from the practices already established. {{{ 427b }}}

What, then, he asked, would still be left to us of legislation?

And I said that nothing remained for us, but that to Apollo at Delphi belonged the greatest, finest, and first of the laws.

What sort of laws? So he asked.

The founding of temples, and sacrifices, and other forms of service to gods, spirits, and heroes; and, again, the tombs of the dead, and all that must be done in service to those below to keep them well-disposed toward us. For such things we ourselves do not understand, and in founding a city {{{ 427c }}} we will trust no one else, if we have any sense, nor will we employ any interpreter other than the ancestral one; for this god, seated [in the middle] of the earth upon the navel, is surely the ancestral interpreter of such matters for all mankind, and gives his guidance from there.

And you are quite right, he said; and so it must be done.

Well then, I said, the city would now be founded for you, son of {{{ 427d }}} Ariston; so examine within it, procuring sufficient light from somewhere — look yourself, and call in your brother too, and Polemarchus, and the rest — in case somehow we can see where justice might be, and where injustice, and how the two differ from each other, and which of them the man who means to be happy must possess, whether or not it escapes the notice of all gods and men.

You are talking nonsense, said Glaucon; for you promised to search for it, {{{ 427e }}} on the ground that it would be impious for you not to defend justice with all your power, in every way.

You remind me rightly, I said, and so it must indeed be done; but you must join in helping too.

Well, he said, we will do so.

I expect, then, I said, that we shall find it in this way. I believe our city, if indeed it has been rightly founded, is completely good.

It must be, he said.

Clearly then it is wise, and courageous, and moderate, and just.

Clearly.

Then whichever of these we find in it, will not the remainder be the one not yet found? {{{ 428a }}}

What else?

Just as, then, with some other set of four things, if we were looking for one of them in something or other, as soon as we recognized that one first, it would have been enough for us; but if we recognized the three others first, the thing sought would by that very fact have been recognized, for clearly it could be nothing other than what was left.

You are right, he said.

Then must we not search in the same way concerning these too, since they happen to be four?

Clearly.

And indeed it seems to me that {{{ 428b }}} wisdom is the first thing clearly visible in it; and something strange appears about it.

What? So he asked.

The city we have described seems to me to be truly wise; for it is well-advised, is it not?

Yes.

And indeed this very thing, good counsel, is clearly a kind of knowledge; for surely people do not counsel well through ignorance, but through knowledge.

Clearly.

But there are many kinds of knowledge of every sort in the city.

How could there not be?

Is it, then, because of the knowledge of carpenters that the city should be called wise and well-advised? {{{ 428c }}}

Not at all on account of that, he said — that would make it skilled in carpentry.

Then it is not on account of the knowledge concerning wooden furnishings, deliberating how it might best be arranged, that a city is to be called wise.

No, indeed.

What about this? Is it the knowledge concerning things made of bronze, or any other such knowledge?

Not that one either, he said.

Nor on account of the knowledge of raising crops from the earth — that would make it skilled in farming.

So it seems to me.

But what of this? I said, is there some knowledge, residing in some of the citizens of the city we have just founded, by which one deliberates not about something {{{ 428d }}} within the city, but about the city as a whole — how it might best conduct itself, both toward itself and toward other cities?

There is indeed.

Which one, I said, and among whom?

This one, he said — guardianship — and it belongs to these rulers whom we just now called complete guardians.

On account of this knowledge, then, what do you call the city?

Well-advised, he said, and truly wise.

Which, then, I said, do you think there will be more of in our city — smiths, {{{ 428e }}} or these true guardians?

Far more smiths, he said.

Then, I said, of all the others also who are called something because they possess some knowledge, would these not be the fewest of all?

By far.

By its smallest class and part, then, and by the knowledge within it that presides and rules, a city founded according to nature would be wise as a whole; and this, as it seems, is by nature {{{ 429a }}} the smallest class that comes to be — the one to which it belongs to share in this knowledge, which alone of all the other kinds of knowledge must be called wisdom.
