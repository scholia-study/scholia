## First Proof: The City and the Soul Compared

Yes indeed, he said — quite rightly.

Let us sum up, then, I said, the worst man. And he is, I take it, the man we described as in a dream — whoever proves to be such a man in waking reality.

Certainly.

So this is what he becomes — whoever, being most tyrannical by nature, gains sole rule, and the longer he lives as a tyrant, the more he becomes such a man.

Necessarily, said Glaucon, taking up the argument.

So, I said, will he who appears most wicked also appear {{{ 576c }}} most wretched? And will he who has been a tyrant for the longest time and most completely — having in truth become such most fully and for the longest time — also appear most wretched? But then, the many think many things.

Necessarily, he said — at any rate that much is so.

Then is it not the case, I said, that the tyrannical man would resemble the city ruled by a tyrant, and the democratic man the city ruled democratically, and so with the rest?

What else?

Then as one city is to another in virtue and happiness, so too is one man to another? {{{ 576d }}}

How could it not be so?

Then how does the tyrannized city compare in virtue to the one ruled by a king, such as we described at the outset?

Quite the opposite, he said — for the one is best and the other worst.

I will not ask, I said, which one you mean — it is clear. But do you judge happiness and wretchedness in the same way, or differently? And let us not be struck with astonishment, looking at the tyrant as one man on his own, nor at the few who stand around him, but, as we ought, let us go in and view the whole {{{ 576e }}} city, sinking down into every part of it and seeing it, and only then declare our opinion.

But you challenge us rightly, he said; and it is clear to everyone that there is no city more wretched than the one ruled by a tyrant, and none happier than the one ruled by a king.

So, I said, would I be right, in issuing this same {{{ 577a }}} challenge about the men as well, to demand that the judge of them be that man who is able, in his mind, to enter into a man's character and see through it, and who is not, like a child looking from outside, struck with amazement by the tyrannical display they put on for those outside, but sees through it clearly? So if I should think that we all ought to listen to that man — the one able to judge, who has lived under the same roof with him and has been present at his dealings within the household, seeing how he behaves toward each of his intimates, occasions on which {{{ 577b }}} he would most be seen stripped of his tragic costume, and again in the dangers of public life — and, having seen all this, we should bid him report how the tyrant stands, in happiness and wretchedness, compared to other men?

You would be quite right, he said, to make this challenge too.

Do you want us, then, I said, to pretend that we ourselves are among those capable of judging, and that we have already met such men, so that we may have someone to answer what we ask?

Certainly. {{{ 577c }}}

Come now, I said, consider it this way. Recalling the likeness between the city and the man, and so examining each point by point in turn, tell me the condition of each.

What sort of things? He asked.

First, I said, to speak of the city, will you call the tyrannized city free or enslaved?

As enslaved as a city can possibly be, he said.

And yet you do see in it masters and free men.

I do see them, he said — though that is a small thing; but as a whole, so to speak, even the most decent part of it is enslaved, dishonored and wretched. {{{ 577d }}}

If, then, I said, the man is like the city, must not the same order be present in him too, and his soul be full of much slavery and unfreedom, with the parts of it that were most decent enslaved, while a small part — the most base and maddest — rules as master?

Necessarily, he said.

What then? Will you say that such a soul is enslaved or free?

Enslaved, I should think, at any rate.

Then again, does not the city which is enslaved and tyrannized do least of all what it wishes?

Very much so. {{{ 577e }}}

Then the tyrannized soul too, to speak of the soul as a whole, will do least of all what it wishes; but, always dragged along by force under the gadfly's goad, it will be full of turmoil and regret.

How could it not be so?

And must the tyrannized city be rich or poor?

Poor. {{{ 578a }}}

Then the tyrannical soul too must always be needy and insatiable.

So it is, he said.

What then? Must not such a city, and such a man, be full of fear?

Very much so.

Do you think you will find more lamentations, groans, wailings and pains in any other city?

By no means.

And do you think that among men such things are found more in anyone else than in this tyrannical man, maddened by desires and erotic love?

How could they be? He replied.
