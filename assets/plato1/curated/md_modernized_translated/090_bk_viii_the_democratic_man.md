## The Democratic Man

Quite so, he said — noble indeed.

These things, then, I said, democracy would have, along with other traits akin to them, and it would be, it seems, a pleasant constitution, anarchic and many-colored, distributing a kind of equality alike to equals and unequals.

Yes indeed, he said, that is all familiar enough.

Consider then, I said, who such a man is privately. Or must we first examine, just as we examined the constitution, in what way he comes to be?

Yes, he said.

Is it not, then, like this? A son might be born to that thrifty and oligarchic man, {{{ 558d }}} raised, I suppose, under his father in his father's ways?

Why not?

This one too, by force, ruling over the pleasures within himself — as many as are wasteful but not money-making, which indeed are called unnecessary—

Clearly, he said.

Do you want, then, I said, so that we may not talk obscurely, that we first define the necessary desires and the unnecessary ones?

I do, he said.

Would not, then, those desires that we could not turn aside be rightly {{{ 558e }}} called necessary, and also those which, when fulfilled, benefit us? For our nature necessarily desires both of these. Or not?

Very much so. {{{ 559a }}}

Rightly, then, we shall apply the term necessary to them.

Rightly.

But what of these? Those that a man could rid himself of if he practiced from youth, and that do no good when present — and some indeed the opposite: if we said that all of them are unnecessary, would we not be speaking correctly?

Quite correctly, indeed.

Shall we then choose some example of each kind, so that we may grasp them in outline?

Yes, we should.

Is not, then, the desire to eat up to the point of health and good condition, {{{ 559b }}} and the desire for bread and relish themselves, a necessary one?

I think so.

The desire for bread, at any rate, is necessary in both respects — both in that it is beneficial and in that it is capable of ⟨not⟩ putting an end to life.

Yes.

But the desire for relish, if it provides some benefit toward good condition,

Quite so.

But what of the desire for things beyond these and for different foods or the like — a desire that, if disciplined and trained from youth, is capable of being got rid of by most people, and is harmful to the body, and harmful to the soul in respect of practical wisdom and moderation? Would it not, then, {{{ 559c }}} rightly be called unnecessary?

Most rightly, indeed.

Shall we not, then, say that these are wasteful, and those others money-making, because they are useful for one's work?

Why not?

Shall we say the same, then, about the pleasures of love and the rest?

Just so.

Was it not, then, the man we just now called a drone whom we meant — the one full of such pleasures and desires and {{{ 559d }}} ruled by the unnecessary ones — while the man ruled by the necessary ones we called thrifty and oligarchic?

Well, of course.

Let us say again, then, I said, how the democratic man comes to be from the oligarchic. It seems to me that, for the most part, it happens like this.

How?

Whenever a young man, raised as we just said, without education and thriftily, tastes the honey of the drones, and keeps company with fierce and clever creatures who can concoct pleasures of every kind, varied and diversely composed, there, you may suppose, {{{ 559e }}} is the beginning of his change … from the oligarchic element in himself to the democratic.

Quite necessarily, he said.

Is it not, then, just as the city changed when an alliance from outside came to the aid of one part — like helping like — that the young man too changes, when in turn a kind of desire from outside comes to the aid of the other part within him, one akin and alike to it?

Absolutely, indeed.

And if, I suppose, some counter-aid comes in alliance to the oligarchic element within him — from his father perhaps, or {{{ 560a }}} from his other relatives, admonishing and reproaching him — then faction and counter-faction and battle arise in him against himself.

Of course.

And sometimes, I suppose, the democratic element yields to the oligarchic, and some of the desires are destroyed while others are driven out, with a kind of shame arising in the young man's soul, and order is restored again.

Yes, that happens sometimes, he said.

But then, I suppose, other desires akin to those that had been driven out {{{ 560b }}} are secretly nourished, and through the father's ignorance of how to rear them, they become many and strong.

That is indeed apt to happen so, he said.

So they draw him toward the same company, and, meeting with him in secret, breed a multitude.

Of course.

At last, I suppose, they seize the citadel of the young man's soul, perceiving it empty of learning and fine pursuits and true arguments — which indeed are the best sentinels and guardians in the minds of men beloved by the gods. {{{ 560c }}}

Quite so, he said.

Then, I suppose, false and boastful arguments and opinions rush up in their place and seize that same region in such a man's soul.

Very much so, he said.

Does he not, then, come back again to those Lotus-eaters and openly dwell among them, and if some aid from his relatives comes to the thrifty part of his soul, those boastful arguments, having shut the gates of the royal wall within him, neither let the alliance itself pass, nor admit as ambassadors {{{ 560d }}} the words of older private persons, but themselves prevail in the fight, and, naming shame folly, they thrust it out disgraced, as an exile, and calling moderation cowardice and abusing it, they cast it out, and, persuading him that measure and orderly spending are boorishness and meanness, they drive them beyond the border, in the company of many useless desires?

Very much so.

And having thus emptied and purified {{{ 560e }}} the soul of the young man possessed and initiated by them with great rites, after this they now lead back insolence and anarchy and prodigality and shamelessness, resplendent, crowned, with a great chorus in attendance — praising and giving them pet names, calling insolence good breeding, anarchy freedom, prodigality {{{ 561a }}} magnificence, and shamelessness courage. Is it not in some such way, I said, that a young man, once reared among necessary desires, changes over to the liberation and release of unnecessary and useless pleasures?

Very much so, indeed, he said — quite plainly.

After this, I suppose, such a man lives spending money and toil and time no more on necessary pleasures than on unnecessary ones; but if he is fortunate and not carried too far into frenzy, and, becoming somewhat older, once the great {{{ 561b }}} tumult has passed, he admits back some part of what was driven out and does not give himself over wholly to the newcomers, he then lives establishing his pleasures on some kind of equal footing, always handing over his own rule to whichever one happens along, as if it had won it by lot, until it is satisfied, and then again to another, dishonoring none but nurturing all alike.

Quite so, indeed.

And, I said, not admitting a true account nor letting it into the fortress, if someone says that some pleasures belong to fine and good desires, {{{ 561c }}} and others to bad ones, and that the former should be cultivated and honored, the latter disciplined and enslaved; but in all these cases he shakes his head and says they are all alike and equally to be honored.

Yes indeed, he said — being so disposed, he does this.

And thus, I said, he also spends his daily life gratifying whatever desire comes along — now drinking and reveling to the flute, now drinking only water and reducing his flesh; {{{ 561d }}} now exercising, and sometimes idle and neglectful of everything, and now as if occupied with philosophy. Often, too, he engages in politics, and leaping up he says and does whatever comes into his head; and if ever he admires some men of war, he is carried in that direction, or if money-makers, then again toward that. And there is neither order nor necessity present in his life, but calling this life sweet and free and blessed, he lives by it throughout. {{{ 561e }}}

You have described completely, he said, the life of some egalitarian sort of man.

And I think too, I said, that this man is of every sort and full of the greatest number of characters, and — like that city — both fine and richly varied; many men and many women might well emulate his life, since it contains within it the greatest number of patterns of constitutions and ways of life.

For that indeed is what he is, he said.
