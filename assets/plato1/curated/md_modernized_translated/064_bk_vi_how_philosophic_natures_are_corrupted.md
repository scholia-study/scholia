## How Philosophic Natures Are Corrupted

Yes.

Have we not, then, gone through the cause of the uselessness of the decent?

Very much so.

Do you want us to go through, next, the necessity behind the wickedness of the many, and to try — {{{ 489e }}} if we can — to show that philosophy is not responsible for this either?

By all means.

Let us listen, then, and speak, recalling the point at which we went through the nature — what sort a man who is to be noble and good {{{ 490a }}} must of necessity be born with. And leading the way for him, if you recall, was truth first of all, which he had to pursue wholly and in every way, or else, being an impostor, have no part whatever in true philosophy.

Yes, that was how it was said.

Now, is not this one point sharply contrary to what is currently believed about him?

Very much so, he said.

Then shall we not be making a reasonable defense in saying that the one who is truly a lover of learning is by nature driven to strive toward what is, and would not {{{ 490b }}} linger over the many particular things that are merely believed to be, but would press on and not grow dull nor abandon his erotic love until he had laid hold of the nature of each thing itself with the part of the soul fit to grasp such a thing — and it is the kindred part that is fit for it — and, having drawn near to it and mingled with what truly is, having begotten intellect and truth, he would know and would truly live and be nourished, and only thus would his travail cease, but not before?

As reasonable as could be, he said.

What then? Will he have any part in loving falsehood, or, quite the opposite, in hating it? {{{ 490c }}}

Hating it, he said.

With truth leading, then, I do not suppose we would ever say that a chorus of evils follows in her train.

How could it?

But instead a sound and just character, followed also by moderation.

Correct, he said.

And indeed, what need is there to force myself back to the beginning and marshal the rest of the philosophic nature's chorus all over again? For you remember, I suppose, that courage, magnificence, quickness to learn, and memory turned out to belong to these natures; and with you taking us up on the point that, while everyone {{{ 490d }}} would be compelled to agree with what we say, still, if he let the arguments go and looked instead at the actual men the argument is about, he would say he sees some of them useless and most of them bad with every kind of badness — examining the cause of this slander, we have now come to just this point, why ever it is that the many are bad, and it is for this reason that we have again taken up the nature of true philosophers and defined it of necessity. {{{ 490e }}}

That is so, he said.

We must look, then, I said, at the ways this nature is corrupted — how it is destroyed in most who have it, while some small part escapes, the ones people call not wicked, exactly, but useless — and after that, {{{ 491a }}} in turn, at the natures that imitate it and install themselves in its pursuit: what sorts of souls they are that arrive at a pursuit for which they are unworthy and which is beyond them, and that, striking false notes in many ways, have fastened upon philosophy, everywhere and among all men, the reputation you speak of.

And which corruptions, he said, do you mean?

I will try, I said, to go through them for you, if I am able. Now this much, I think, everyone will grant us: that such a nature, possessing all the qualities we prescribed just now, if it is to become {{{ 491b }}} a philosopher in the full sense, is born among men only seldom, and only in a few cases. Or do you not think so?

Indeed I do.

Consider, then, in the case of these few, how many and how great are the ways of ruin.

What are they?

The most astonishing thing to hear of all is that each single one of the qualities we praised in that nature destroys the soul that possesses it, and tears it away from philosophy. I mean courage, moderation, and everything we went through.

Strange to hear, he said. {{{ 491c }}}

And besides these, I said, all the things called good also corrupt it and tear it away — beauty, wealth, bodily strength, powerful connections of kin in the city, and everything akin to these; for you grasp the type of thing I mean.

I do, he said; and I would gladly learn more precisely what you mean.

Grasp the matter correctly as a whole, then, I said, and it will appear quite clear to you, and what was said earlier about them will no longer seem strange.

How, then, he said, do you bid me proceed? {{{ 491d }}}

Concerning any seed, I said, or any growing thing, whether one of those in the earth or one of the animals, we know this — that when a thing fails to meet with the nourishment proper to it, or with its right season, or its right place, then the more vigorous it is, the more it falls short of what befits it; for I suppose what is bad stands more opposed to what is good than to what is not good.

How could it not?

It stands to reason, then, I think, that the best nature, when it lights on nurture more foreign to it, comes off worse than a poor one does.

It does. {{{ 491e }}}

Well then, Adeimantus, I said, shall we not say the same of souls — that those of the best natural gifts, meeting with bad upbringing, become exceptionally bad? Or do you suppose that great wrongdoing and sheer, unmixed wickedness arise from a poor nature rather than from a vigorous one ruined by its nurture, and that a feeble nature will ever be the cause of anything great, whether for good or for ill?

No, he said, but rather as you describe it.
