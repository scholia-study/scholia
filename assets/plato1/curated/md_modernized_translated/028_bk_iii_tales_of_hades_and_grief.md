## Tales of Hades, and Grief

{{{ 386a }}} Such, then, I said, are more or less the things about the gods that must and must not be heard, it seems, right from childhood, by those who are to honor the gods and their parents, and who are not to hold their friendship for one another lightly.

And I think, he said, that this seems right to us.

But what if they are to be courageous? Must we not tell them these things too, and such as will make them least afraid of death? {{{ 386b }}} Or do you think anyone could ever become courageous while holding this terror within himself?

By Zeus, he said, not I.

What then? Do you think that someone who believes the things in Hades exist and are terrible will be unafraid of death, and will choose death in battle over defeat and slavery?

Not at all.

It seems, then, that we must supervise those who undertake to tell these tales as well, and ask them not to disparage what is in Hades so flatly, but rather to praise it, since what they say would be neither true {{{ 386c }}} nor of any use to men who are going to fight.

We must indeed, he said.

We shall erase, then, I said, beginning from this verse, all such passages—
+ I would rather be a serf, laboring for hire for another,
+ a landless man with little to live on,
+ than be ruler over all the dead who have perished—
and this: {{{ 386d }}}
+ and the house would show itself to mortals and immortals,
+ dreadful, dank, the very thing the gods themselves detest—
and:
+ Ah me, so there is, even in the house of Hades,
+ a soul and a phantom, but no mind in it at all—
and this:
+ that he alone has understanding, but the others flit about as shadows—
and:
+ his soul, flying forth from his limbs, went down to Hades,
+ bewailing its fate, leaving behind manhood and youth—
{{{ 387a }}} and this:
+ … and the soul went beneath the earth, like smoke,
+ gibbering as it went …
and:
+ and as when bats, in the recess of a wondrous cave,
+ fly about shrieking, whenever one falls
+ from the cluster on the rock, and they cling to one another,
+ so these went together, shrieking.
{{{ 387b }}} All these things, and everything like them, we will beg Homer and the other poets not to take offense if we cut out — not on the grounds that they lack poetic quality or fail to delight most listeners, but rather, the finer the poetry, the less fit it is for the ears of boys and men who must live as free people, dreading slavery more than death.

Quite so.

Then all the terrible and frightening names connected with these things must also be discarded — Cocytus and Styx, {{{ 387c }}} and the dead below, and the bloodless ones, and everything else named in this fashion that is supposed to make everyone who hears it shudder. And perhaps that serves well enough for some other purpose; but on behalf of the guardians we are afraid that a shudder of this kind will make them more feverish and softer than they ought to be.

And we are right to fear it, he said.

Then must they be removed?

Yes.

And must the opposite pattern to these be both spoken and composed?

Clearly. {{{ 387d }}}

And shall we then remove the wailings and lamentations of men of note?

It is necessary, he said, if the earlier ones were too.

Consider, then, I said, whether we will be right to remove them or not. We do say that the decent man will not think it terrible that another decent man, who is in fact his comrade, has died.

Yes, we do say that.

Then he would not lament on behalf of that man, as though he had suffered something terrible.

Certainly not.

But surely we also say this, that such a man is above all self-sufficient in himself for living well, and that, unlike {{{ 387e }}} others, he least of all needs anyone else.

True, he said.

Then it is least terrible for him to be deprived of a son or a brother or possessions or anything else of that kind.

Least of all, indeed.

Then he will least of all lament, and will bear it as calmly as possible, whenever such a misfortune overtakes him.

Very much so.

Then we would rightly remove the dirges of famous men, and give them instead to women — and not even to worthy women — {{{ 388a }}} and to such men as are base, so that those whom we say we are raising to guard the country will find it repugnant to behave like them.

Rightly, he said.

Again, then, we shall ask Homer and the other poets not to portray Achilles, son of a goddess, as—
+ now lying on his side, and now again
+ on his back, and now on his face, …
and at other times springing upright and roaming distracted along {{{ 388b }}} the shore of the barren sea, or taking the sooty dust in both hands and pouring it down over his head, or weeping and wailing in every other way, and to every degree, that the poet gave him, or as Priam, near kin to the gods, imploring, and—
+ rolling in the dung,
+ calling out each man by name.
And still more than these we shall ask them not, at any rate, to make the gods lament and say— {{{ 388c }}}
+ Ah me, wretched, ah me, who bore the bravest of men to misfortune—
but if they portray the gods at all, let them not dare to imitate even the greatest of the gods so unlike himself as to make him say—
+ Ah me, he says, truly a dear man, pursued around the city,
+ I see with my eyes, and my heart grieves—
and—
+ Ah, ah, woe is me, that Sarpedon, dearest of men to me,
{{{ 388d }}}
+ is fated to be slain by Patroclus, son of Menoetius.
For if, dear Adeimantus, our young men were to hear such things told in earnest and not laugh at them as unworthy of being said, a man would hardly consider himself — mere human that he is — above such conduct, and reproach himself, should the impulse come to him to say or do anything of the kind; instead, feeling no shame and exercising no restraint, he would sing many dirges and lamentations over small misfortunes. {{{ 388e }}}

That is most true, he said.

But it must not be so, as our argument just now showed us; and we must be persuaded by it, until someone convinces us with a finer one.

No, it must not, indeed.

But surely they must not be lovers of laughter either. For pretty much whenever someone gives way to violent laughter, that condition looks for a violent change as well.

I think so, he said.

Then, if anyone portrays men of account overcome by {{{ 389a }}} laughter, it must not be accepted — and much less so, if it is gods.
