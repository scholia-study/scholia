## Adeimantus Objects: The Ship of State

{{{ 487b }}} And Adeimantus said, Socrates, no one could argue against what you say here. But in truth this is what happens each time to those who hear what you are now saying: through inexperience in asking and answering, they think that at each question they are led on a little by the argument, and that when the small steps are gathered together at the end of the discussion, a great error comes to light, contrary to what was said at first; and just as those unskilled at checkers are shut in by the skilled players and have no move {{{ 487c }}} left to make, so they too, in the end, are shut in and have nothing to say, defeated by this other kind of game, played not with counters but with words; and yet the truth is no more on that side for all this. I say this with an eye to the present case. For now someone might say that in argument he cannot contradict you at each separate point as it is asked, but that in fact he sees that those who set out for philosophy and do not, having taken it up for the sake of an education, drop it while still young, {{{ 487d }}} but linger at it longer, become for the most part quite odd, not to say thoroughly wicked, while even those who seem the most decent are nevertheless affected in just this way by the very pursuit you praise, and become useless to their cities.

And I, hearing this, said, Do you think, then, that those who say this are lying?

I do not know, he said, but I would gladly hear what you think.

Then you may hear that to me they seem to be speaking the truth. {{{ 487e }}}

How then, he said, can it be right to say that cities will not cease from evils until philosophers rule in them — the very men we agree are useless to them?

You are asking, I said, a question that needs an answer given through an image.

And you, he said, are not in the habit, I suppose, of speaking in images.

Very well, I said; are you mocking me, after landing me in an argument so {{{ 488a }}} hard to prove? Listen, then, to the image, so that you may see all the more how clumsily I make my comparisons. For so harsh is the treatment the most decent men have suffered at the hands of their cities, that no single other thing has suffered the like, but one must gather it together out of many things, making a comparison and speaking in their defense, just as painters do who mix together goat-stags and other such creatures in their paintings. For imagine something of this sort happening, whether concerning many ships or one: a shipowner who surpasses everyone on the ship {{{ 488b }}} in size and strength, but who is somewhat deaf and likewise sees only a little, and whose knowledge of seafaring is much the same; and sailors quarreling with one another over the steering, each thinking that he ought to steer, though he has never learned the craft, nor can point to a teacher of his own, nor to a time when he learned it; who, besides, claim that it cannot even be taught, and are ready to cut to pieces anyone who says it can; while they themselves are forever crowding around the {{{ 488c }}} shipowner himself, begging him and doing everything so that he will hand the helm over to them; and sometimes, when they fail to persuade him and others succeed instead, they either kill the others or throw them off the ship, and having bound the noble shipowner with mandrake, or with drink, or by some other means, they rule the ship, making use of what is aboard, and drinking and feasting they sail just as such men are likely to; and besides this they praise {{{ 488d }}} as a real seaman, a skilled pilot, a master of the ways of a ship, whoever is clever at lending a hand in getting them control, whether by persuading or by forcing the shipowner, while they blame as useless whoever is not of this sort, and have no notion at all of the true pilot — that he must of necessity attend to the year, the seasons, the sky, the stars, the winds, and everything that belongs to his craft, if he is truly to be fit to command a ship, while as for how he is to steer {{{ 488e }}} whether or not some wish it, they do not think one can acquire the craft and the practice of that at the same time as the pilot's craft. With such things happening aboard ships, do you not think that the man who is truly fit to pilot would in fact be called {{{ 489a }}} a stargazer, a babbler, and useless to them, by those sailing in ships equipped in this way?

Very much so, said Adeimantus.

I do not think, I said, that you need the image gone over point by point to see that it is like the disposition of cities toward true philosophers, but that you understand what I mean.

Very much so, he said.

First, then, take that man who wonders that philosophers are not honored in cities: teach him the image, and try to persuade him that it would be far more astonishing {{{ 489b }}} if they were.

I will teach him, he said.

And tell him also that you speak the truth, that the most decent of those engaged in philosophy are useless to the many; but bid him blame, for this uselessness, those who fail to make use of them, and not the decent men themselves. For it is not in the nature of things that a pilot should beg the sailors to be ruled by him, nor that the wise should go to the doors of the rich; whoever put it so cleverly was lying, and the truth stands thus by nature: whether a rich man or a poor man falls ill, it is necessary {{{ 489c }}} for him to go to the doors of physicians, and for everyone who needs to be ruled to go to the doors of the man able to rule, and not for the ruler — the one who is genuinely of some use — to beg the ruled to submit to his rule. But you will not go wrong in likening the political rulers of today to the sailors we were just describing, and those whom they call useless and sky-babblers to the true pilots.

Quite right, he said.

For these reasons, then, and among such people, it is not easy for the best pursuit to win a good reputation from those who pursue the opposite; but by far the greatest and strongest slander falls upon philosophy {{{ 489d }}} because of those who claim to pursue such things — the very men you say the accuser of philosophy has in mind, when he says that most of those who go in for it are thoroughly wicked, and the most decent of them useless, and I admitted that you spoke the truth. Was that not so?
