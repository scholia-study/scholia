## Timocracy: How It Arises

That, at least, he said, is how the viewing and the judging would reasonably come about.

Come then, I said, let us try to say in what way a timocracy might arise out of an aristocracy. Or is this much straightforward {{{ 545d }}} — that every constitution changes from within the very body that holds power, whenever faction arises within that same body; but while it is of one mind, even if very small, it is impossible for it to be moved?

Yes, that is how it is.

How then, I said, Glaucon, will our city be set in motion, and in what way will the auxiliaries and the rulers fall into strife with one another and with themselves? Or would you like us, like Homer, to pray the Muses to tell us how {{{ 545e }}} strife first broke out, and shall we say that they, treating us as children to be played with and teased, and pretending to be in earnest, address us in a lofty, tragic style?

How? {{{ 546a }}}

Something like this. It is difficult, indeed, for a city so composed to be moved; but since everything that has come into being is subject to decay, not even a structure of this kind will remain for all time, but will be dissolved. And the dissolution is this: not only for plants that grow in the earth, but also for creatures that live upon it, there come to be fruitfulness and unfruitfulness of soul and of body alike, whenever the revolutions bring round for each the circuits of their cycles — short circuits for the short-lived, and opposite ones for the opposite kind. But as for the good breeding and the barrenness of your race — though those you have trained as leaders of the city {{{ 546b }}} are wise — they will be no better at hitting the mark by reasoning joined with observation, but these will slip past them, and they will at some time beget children when they ought not. For the divine begotten kind there is a period which a perfect number encompasses, but for the human kind there is one in which, first, increases capable of exercising power and subject to it — taking three intervals and four terms of things that make like and unlike, and that increase and decrease — rendered all things speakable {{{ 546c }}} and rational in relation to one another; of which the base in the ratio of four to three, yoked with the five, when increased three times, yields two harmonies: the one equal an equal number of times, taken a hundred times as many; the other of equal length one way but oblong — a hundred of the numbers from the rational diagonals of the five, each falling short by one, and of the irrational diagonals, two, and a hundred cubes of the three. And this whole number is geometrical, and has authority over things of this kind — better and worse births {{{ 546d }}} — and when your guardians, in ignorance of it, join brides to bridegrooms out of due season, the children born will be neither well-natured nor fortunate; of these the elder rulers will appoint the best, but nonetheless, being unworthy, once they in turn have come into their fathers' power, they, though guardians, will first begin to neglect us, holding the things of music in less honor than they ought, and second the things of gymnastic, so that your young will become more unmusical. And from these there will be established rulers not at all fit to guard, {{{ 546e }}} when it comes to testing Hesiod's races and those among {{{ 547a }}} you — the golden, the silver, the bronze, and the iron; and when iron is mixed together with silver, and bronze with gold, there will arise unlikeness and inharmonious inequality, which, wherever they arise, always breed war and enmity. It is of this stock, one must say, that strife always is, wherever it may arise.

And we shall say that they answer rightly, he said.

Yes indeed, I said, it must be so, since they are Muses. {{{ 547b }}}

What then, he said, do the Muses say next?

When strife arose, I said, the two kinds then pulled each its own way: the iron and the bronze toward money-making and the acquisition of land and houses and gold and silver, while the other two, the golden and the silver — being not poor but naturally rich — led their souls toward virtue and the ancient order; but as they used force on one another and pulled against each other, they agreed on a middle course: having divided up the land and the houses, {{{ 547c }}} to make them private property, and those whom before they had guarded as free friends and providers, they now enslaved and held as dependents and servants, while they themselves took charge of war and of guarding them.

It seems to me, he said, that this is how the transition comes about.

Then, I said, would this constitution not be somewhere between an aristocracy and an oligarchy?

Certainly.

It will change, then, in this way; but once it has changed, how will it be governed? Or {{{ 547d }}} is it clear that, being in between, it will in some ways imitate the former constitution, in others oligarchy, and will also have something peculiar to itself?

So it is, he said.

Then in honoring its rulers, in its fighting part's abstaining from farming and from handicrafts and the rest of money-making, in having common meals established, and in attending to gymnastic and the contests of war — in all such things will it not imitate the former constitution?

Yes. {{{ 547e }}}

But in fearing to bring the wise into office, since such men as it now has are no longer simple and unswerving but mixed, and in inclining instead toward the spirited and simpler sort, those naturally suited more to war than to {{{ 548a }}} peace, and in holding in honor the tricks and contrivances of war, and in spending its whole time at war — in these ways it will in turn have much that is peculiar to itself alone?

Yes.

And men of this sort, I said, will be desirers of money, like those in oligarchies, fiercely honoring gold and silver in secret, since they possess treasure-chambers and private hoards where they can store and hide them, and, again, walled dwellings — simply private nests — in which, {{{ 548b }}} spending on women and on whomever else they chose, they would lay out a great deal.

Very true, he said.

And so they will also be miserly with money, since they honor it and do not acquire it openly, yet eager to spend the wealth of others to satisfy desire, secretly reaping their pleasures like children running away from their father, the law, educated not by persuasion but by force, because they have neglected the true Muse, the one that goes with {{{ 548c }}} reason and philosophy, and have honored gymnastic above music as the elder of the two.

Altogether, he said, you are describing a constitution mixed of bad and good.

For it is mixed, I said; but one thing alone stands out most clearly in it, since the spirited part rules — love of victory and love of honor.

Very much so, he said.

Well then, I said, this constitution, having thus come about, would be of such a kind that, sketching the outline of a constitution in words, {{{ 548d }}} one would not work it out with precision, since it is enough to see even from the sketch both the most just man and the most unjust, while it would be an impossibly long task to go through all constitutions and all characters, leaving nothing out.
