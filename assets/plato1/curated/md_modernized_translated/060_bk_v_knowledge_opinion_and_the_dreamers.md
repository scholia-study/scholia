## Knowledge, Opinion, and the Dreamers

Very much so, he said, wide awake.

Then should we not rightly say that the thought of this one, since he knows, is knowledge, and that of the other, since he opines, is opinion?

Certainly.

What then, if this man is angry with us — the one we say opines but does not know — and disputes that what we say is true? {{{ 476e }}} Shall we have anything to comfort him with and persuade him gently, concealing that he is not well?

We must indeed, he said.

Come now, consider what we shall say to him. Or would you rather we inquire of him in this way, telling him that if he knows something we do not grudge it him at all, but would gladly see him knowing something. But tell us this: does the one who knows know something or nothing? So answer me on his behalf.

I will answer, he said, that he knows something.

Something that is, or something that is not? {{{ 477a }}}

Something that is; for how could a thing that is not be known at all?

Do we then have this adequately — even if we should look at it from many sides — that what completely is, is completely knowable, and what in no way is, is in every way unknowable?

Most adequately.

Well then; if something is such as both to be and not to be, would it not lie between what purely is and what in turn in no way is?

Between them.

Then knowledge was set over what is, and ignorance of necessity over what is not, while over this thing between, something between {{{ 477b }}} ignorance and knowledge must be sought, if indeed anything of that kind happens to exist?

Certainly.

Do we then say that opinion is something?

How could we not?

A different power from knowledge, or the same one?

A different one.

Opinion, then, is set over one thing, and knowledge over another, each according to its own power.

So it is.

Then is knowledge by nature set over what is, to know what is, as it stands? — Or rather, it seems to me, we should first distinguish it this way.

How so? {{{ 477c }}}

We shall say that powers are a certain kind among the things that are, by which we ourselves are able to do what we are able to do, and everything else too is able to do whatever it is able to do — I mean, for instance, that sight and hearing are among the powers — if indeed you follow the kind of thing I mean to say.

But I do follow it, he said.

Listen then to what appears to me about them. For in the case of a power, I see neither any color nor shape nor anything of that sort, such as I see in many other things, by looking to which I distinguish for myself, in some cases, that this is one thing and that another; but in the case of a power {{{ 477d }}} I look only to this — what it is set over and what it accomplishes — and it is by this that I have called each of them a power, and the one that is set over the same thing and accomplishes the same thing I call the same power, while the one that is set over a different thing and accomplishes a different thing I call a different power. And you? How do you do it?

Just so, he said.

Come here again then, I said, my good man. Do you say that knowledge itself is some kind of power, or under what kind do you place it?

Under that, he said, the strongest of all powers. {{{ 477e }}}

And opinion — shall we assign it to a power, or to some other kind?

In no way, he said; for that by which we are able to opine is nothing other than opinion.

But yet a little while ago you agreed that knowledge and opinion are not the same.

How could anyone with any sense, he said, take what is infallible to be the same as what is not infallible?

Well said, I replied, and it is clear that opinion is agreed by us to be {{{ 478a }}} different from knowledge.

Different.

Each of them, then, being a different power, is by nature set over something different?

Necessarily.

Knowledge, surely, is set over what is — to know what is, as it stands?

Yes.

And opinion, we say, is to opine?

Yes.

Does it opine the very same thing that knowledge knows? And will the same thing be both knowable and opinable? Or is that impossible?

Impossible, he said, from what we have agreed; if indeed a different power is by nature set over a different thing, and both are powers, opinion {{{ 478b }}} and knowledge, and each is different, as we say, then from these it is not possible for the knowable and the opinable to be the same.

Then if what is, is knowable, would not the opinable be something other than what is?

Something other.

Does it, then, opine what is not? Or is it impossible even to opine what is not? Consider it. Does not the one who opines direct his opinion toward something? Or is it possible, in turn, to opine, yet to opine nothing?

Impossible.

But the one who opines opines some one thing?

Yes.

But surely what is not would most correctly be called {{{ 478c }}} not some one thing, but nothing?

Certainly.

And to what is not, did we not of necessity assign ignorance, and to what is, knowledge?

Correctly, he said.

Then it opines neither what is nor what is not?

No, indeed.

Then opinion would be neither ignorance nor knowledge?

It would seem not.

Is it, then, outside these, exceeding either knowledge in clarity or ignorance in obscurity?

Neither.

But does opinion, I said, appear to you darker than knowledge, but brighter than ignorance?

Yes, and much so, he said. {{{ 478d }}}

And does it lie within both of them?

Yes.

Opinion, then, would be between the two of them.

Quite so.

Did we not say earlier that, if something should appear to be, as it were, at once both being and not being, such a thing would lie between what purely is and what altogether is not, and that neither knowledge nor ignorance would be set over it, but rather what appears between ignorance and knowledge?

Correctly.

And now has not something appeared between these two, which indeed we call opinion?

It has appeared. {{{ 478e }}}

It would remain for us, then, it seems, to find that thing which partakes of both — of being and of not being — and which could not correctly be called purely either, so that, if it appears, we may justly call it opinable, assigning the extremes to the extremes and what is between to what is between. Or is it not so?

So it is.

These things, then, being laid down, let that good fellow speak and answer me, I shall say — {{{ 479a }}} the one who believes that there is no beautiful itself, nor any form of beauty itself always existing the same in the same way, but who holds that there are many beautiful things — that lover of sights who in no way tolerates it if anyone says that the beautiful is one, and the just, and the rest in the same way. For of these many beautiful things, my good man, we shall say, is there one that will not appear ugly? And of the just things, one that will not appear unjust? And of the holy things, one that will not appear unholy? {{{ 479b }}}

No, he said, but it is necessary that these things appear in some way both beautiful and ugly, and likewise for all the others you ask about.

And what of the many things that are double? Do they appear any less half than double?

Not at all.

And with great and small, light and heavy — will the things we call by these names be addressed by them any more than by their opposites?

No, he said, but each will always have a share of both.

Is each of the many things, then, any more what someone might say it is than it is not?

It resembles, he said, those double-meaning riddles at feasts, {{{ 479c }}} and the children's riddle about the eunuch — concerning his throw at the bat, with what and on what they hint he struck it — for these things too have a double meaning, and it is not possible to conceive firmly of any of them as either being or not being, nor as both, nor as neither.

Do you have, then, I said, any use to make of them, or any better place to put them than the space between being and not being? For surely they will appear neither darker than what is not, so as to be more not-being, {{{ 479d }}} nor brighter than what is, so as to be more being.

Most true, he said.

We have found, then, it seems, that the many conventions of the many, concerning the beautiful and the rest, roll about somewhere between what is not and what purely is.

We have found it.

And we agreed beforehand that, if any such thing should appear, it must be called opinable, not knowable — this wanderer between the two, caught by the power between.

We have agreed. {{{ 479e }}}

Then those who look upon the many beautiful things, but do not see the beautiful itself, nor are able to follow another who leads them to it, and who see the many just things, but not the just itself, and so with everything else — these, we shall say, opine everything, but know nothing of what they opine.

Necessarily, he said.

And what, in turn, of those who look upon each thing itself, and upon what is always the same in the same way? Do they not know, rather than opine?

This too is necessary.

Shall we not say, then, that these embrace and love the things over which there is knowledge, {{{ 480a }}} and those others the things over which there is opinion? Or do we not remember that we said these latter love and gaze upon beautiful sounds and colors and things of that sort, but cannot bear to accept that the beautiful itself is anything at all?

We remember.

Shall we not, then, be doing something out of tune if we call them lovers of opinion rather than philosophers? And will they be very angry with us, if we speak in this way?

Not if they are persuaded by me, he said; for it is not right to be angry at the truth.

Then those who embrace each thing itself that is must be called philosophers, and not lovers of opinion?

By all means.
