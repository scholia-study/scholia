## All One Family: Kinship, Unity, and the Guardians' Happiness

Quite right, he said.

This, then, Glaucon, is the community of women and children for your city's guardians — this, and of this kind; but that it follows from the rest of the constitution, and is by far the best, we must next establish from the argument. Or how shall we proceed? {{{ 462a }}}

Yes, by Zeus, he said.

Is not this, then, the starting point of our agreement — that we ask ourselves what is the greatest good we can name for the ordering of a city, at which the lawgiver must aim in laying down the laws, and what is the greatest evil, and then examine whether what we have just described fits the footprint of the good and fails to fit that of the evil?

Most of all, he said.

Do we have, then, any greater evil for a city than that which {{{ 462b }}} tears it apart and makes it many instead of one? Or a greater good than that which binds it together and makes it one?

We do not.

Then does not a community of pleasure and pain bind a city together, when, as far as possible, all the citizens alike rejoice and grieve over the same things coming to be and passing away?

Certainly, he said.

And is it not a private ownership of such feelings that dissolves it, when some are deeply pained and others greatly pleased at the same {{{ 462c }}} experiences befalling the city and those within it?

Why not?

Does this, then, arise from the fact that the citizens do not all utter, together and in the same way, such words as these in the city — mine and not mine? And the same about what belongs to another?

Quite so.

Then is not that city best governed in which the most people say this — mine and not mine — about the same thing in the same way?

Very much so.

And is it not the city that comes closest to being like one human being? For instance, whenever one of our fingers is struck, the whole community stretched throughout the body toward the soul, drawn into a single ordering under its ruling part, {{{ 462d }}} perceives this, and the whole feels the pain together with the part that suffers; and so we say that the man's finger hurts — and the same account holds for anything else belonging to the man, both for the pain of a part that suffers and for the pleasure of a part that finds relief?

Yes, the same, he said; and as for what you are asking, it is the best-governed city that dwells nearest to being of that kind.

Then I think, when any one of the citizens suffers anything, whether good {{{ 462e }}} or bad, such a city will say above all that the one suffering belongs to itself, and the whole of it will either rejoice together or grieve together.

That is necessary, he said — at least for one that is well governed.

It would be time, then, I said, for us to return to our own city, and examine in it the points on which the argument has agreed, to see whether it possesses them most fully, or some other city more so.

We must indeed, he said. {{{ 463a }}}

What then? In other cities too, presumably, there are rulers and a people; and are there also in this one?

There are.

Will all of these address one another as citizens?

How could they not?

But besides citizens, what does the people in the other cities call their rulers?

In most, masters; in the democratically governed ones, this very name, rulers.

And what about the people in our city? Besides citizens, what does it say the rulers are? {{{ 463b }}}

Saviors and helpers, he said.

And what do these call the people?

Paymasters and providers of their sustenance.

And what do the rulers in the other cities call their peoples?

Slaves, he said.

And what do the rulers call one another?

Fellow rulers, he said.

And ours?

Fellow guardians.

Can you say, then, of the rulers in the other cities, whether any of them can address one of his fellow rulers as one of his own, and another as a stranger?

Yes, and many.

Then does he not both think of and speak of the one who is his own as belonging to himself, {{{ 463c }}} and of the outsider as not belonging to him?

Just so.

And what about your guardians? Is there any of them who could think of or address one of his fellow guardians as a stranger?

Not at all, he said; for whomever he meets, he will think he is meeting either a brother, or a sister, or a father, or a mother, or a son, or a daughter, or the offspring or ancestors of these.

That is excellently said, I said, but tell me this too — will you make it law for them that only the names are their own, or {{{ 463d }}} that they must also perform all the actions in accordance with the names, toward their fathers, whatever the law requires concerning fathers, in the way of respect and care and the duty of children to be obedient to their parents, or else it will go worse for him, before both gods and men, on the ground that he is acting neither piously nor justly, if he does anything other than this? Will these be the sayings, or others, that all the citizens will chant straightway into the children's ears, concerning the fathers whom someone points out to them as theirs, and concerning their other kin? {{{ 463e }}}

These, he said; for it would be ridiculous if names of kinship were uttered only through the mouth, without deeds to match.

Then in this city, more than in any other, when someone fares well or badly, all will speak in unison the word we mentioned just now — that mine is faring well, or that mine is faring badly.

Very true again, he said. {{{ 464a }}}

Then did we not say that, along with this belief and this word, pleasures and pains follow in common?

And we said so rightly.

Then will not our citizens share above all in the very thing they will call mine? And sharing in this, will they not in this way have the greatest community of pain and pleasure?

Very much so.

Is not the cause of this, then, in addition to the rest of our arrangement, the community of women and children among the guardians?

Yes, above all, he said. {{{ 464b }}}

But we agreed that this was the greatest good for a city, comparing a well-governed city to a body in how it relates to one of its own parts in respect of pain and pleasure.

And we agreed rightly, he said.

Then the community of children and women among our helpers has been shown to be the cause of the greatest good for the city.

Very much so, he said.

And indeed this agrees with what we said before too; for we said, I believe, that these must have neither private houses, nor land, nor {{{ 464c }}} any possession, but that, receiving sustenance from the rest as wages for their guarding, they must all spend it in common, if they are truly to be guardians.

Rightly, he said.

Is it not, then, as I say, that what was said before, together with what is now being said, makes them even more truly guardians, and prevents them from tearing the city apart by calling mine not the same thing but each a different thing — one man dragging off to his own house whatever he can acquire apart from the rest, {{{ 464d }}} another into his own, which is a different one, with a different wife and different children, producing pleasures and pains that are private because their objects are private, but rather, by a single belief about what belongs to them, all straining toward the same thing, being as far as possible alike in feeling pain and pleasure together?

Quite so, he said.

What then? Will not lawsuits and accusations against one another virtually vanish from among them, because they possess nothing private except the body, while everything else is held in common? Hence it follows {{{ 464e }}} that they will be free from faction, at least over everything that sets men at odds through the possession of money or of children and kin?

It is quite inevitable, he said, that they will be rid of them.

And moreover there could rightly be no lawsuits among them either for violence or for assault; for we shall say, I think, that it is honorable and just for age-mates to defend themselves against age-mates, laying it down as a necessity in the care of their bodies.

Rightly, he said. {{{ 465a }}}

For this law has this to be said for it too, I said — if anyone is ever angry at another, by satisfying his anger in that way he would be less likely to go on to greater faction.

Quite so.

The elder, indeed, will be charged with ruling and chastising all the younger.

Clearly.

And moreover, as is likely, a younger person will never attempt to use force against an elder, or to strike him, unless the rulers command it. Nor, I think, will he dishonor him in any other way; for two guardians are sufficient to restrain him — fear and reverence: reverence, which keeps him from laying hands on {{{ 465b }}} a parent, and fear, because the others will come to the aid of the one who suffers, some as his sons, others as his brothers, others as his fathers.

Yes, that is how it turns out, he said.

Then in every way, as a result of the laws, will the men live in peace with one another?

A great peace, certainly.

And indeed, with these men not engaged in faction among themselves, there is no danger that the rest of the city will ever be divided, either against them or against itself.

No, indeed not.

As for the very smallest of the evils, from which they would be freed, I hesitate {{{ 465c }}} even to mention them, because of their unseemliness — the flattery of the rich by the poor, and the difficulties and pains that men undergo in raising children and making money on account of the necessary sustenance of household servants, at one time borrowing, at another disowning debts, at another procuring funds by any means whatever and depositing them with their wives and servants, handing them over to be managed — and how much, my friend, and what sort of thing they suffer over these matters is plain enough, and ignoble, and not worth speaking of. {{{ 465d }}}

Yes, obvious, he said, even to a blind man.

So they will be free from all these things, and they will live a life more blessed than that most blessed life which Olympic victors live.

How so?

Those men are counted happy for only a small part of what belongs to these. For the victory of these men is finer, and their sustenance from the public is more complete. For the victory they win is the preservation of the whole city, and they and their children are crowned with sustenance and everything else that life requires, and they receive prizes {{{ 465e }}} from their own city while they live, and when they die they share in a burial worthy of them.

Yes, very fine ones, he said.

Do you remember, then, I said, that earlier some argument — I do not recall whose — rebuked us for not making the guardians {{{ 466a }}} happy, since, though it was open to them to have everything the citizens have, they would have nothing? And we, I believe, said that we would consider this later, if it should happen to come up, but that for now we would make the guardians guardians, and the city as happy as we could, rather than looking to one class within it and molding that alone to be happy?

I remember, he said.

What then? Now, does the life of our helpers, since it appears far finer and better than that of the Olympic victors, {{{ 466b }}} in any way appear inferior to the life of cobblers, or of other craftsmen, or of farmers?

It does not seem so to me, he said.

But indeed, what I said there it is right to say here too — that if the guardian sets about becoming happy in such a way that he is no longer even a guardian, and a life so moderate and secure, and, as we say, best, will not satisfy him, but a foolish and adolescent opinion about happiness falls upon him and drives {{{ 466c }}} him, through his power, to appropriate everything in the city for himself, he will come to know that Hesiod was truly wise in saying that the half is somehow more than the whole.

If he takes me as his counselor, he said, he will stick to this life.

Then do you agree, I said, with the community of the women with the men that we have gone through, concerning education, and children, and the guarding of the other citizens — that whether remaining in the city or going out to war, they must guard together and {{{ 466d }}} hunt together like dogs, and share in everything, in every way, as far as possible, and that by doing these things they will do what is best, and not act against the nature of the female in relation to the male, according to which the two of them are naturally fitted to share with one another?
