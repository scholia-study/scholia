## Thrasymachus' Speech: The Shepherd and the Flock

{{{ 343a }}} So when we had come this far in the argument, and it was plain to everyone that the account of justice had swung right around into its opposite, Thrasymachus, instead of answering, said, Tell me, Socrates, do you have a nurse?

What do you mean? I said — shouldn't you be answering rather than asking things like that?

Because she lets you go about sniveling, he said, and doesn't wipe your nose when you need it — you, who thanks to her don't even know a sheep from a shepherd.

Why exactly? I said. {{{ 343b }}}

Because you think that shepherds and cowherds look to the good of the sheep or the cattle, and that in fattening them and caring for them they have anything in view but the good of their masters and their own; and you suppose, too, that the rulers in cities — those who truly rule — regard their subjects in some other light than one regards sheep, and that night and day they have anything in mind but {{{ 343c }}} where their own profit is to come from. And you are so far off, concerning the just and justice and the unjust and injustice, that you do not know that justice and the just are really someone else's good — the advantage of the stronger, that is, of the ruler — while for the one who obeys and serves it is a harm that is properly his own, whereas injustice is the opposite, and rules over those who are truly naive and just, and the ruled do what serves the advantage of that stronger party, and by serving {{{ 343d }}} him they make him happy, but themselves not in the least. You must consider it this way, Socrates, you utter innocent: the just man everywhere comes off with less than the unjust. First, in their contracts with one another, wherever such a man enters into partnership with such another, you would nowhere find, when the partnership is dissolved, the just man coming away with more than the unjust, but with less; then in their dealings with the city, whenever there are levies, the just man contributes more from an equal estate and the unjust man less, and whenever there are distributions, {{{ 343e }}} the one gains nothing and the other gains a great deal. Indeed, whenever each of them holds some office, the just man, even if he suffers no other penalty, finds his household affairs the worse for neglect, while he gains nothing from the public purse because he is just, and besides this he becomes hateful to his relatives and acquaintances when he is unwilling to serve them against justice; whereas for the unjust man everything is the opposite of this. I mean, {{{ 344a }}} the man I was just now speaking of, the one able to get the greater share on a grand scale — consider him, then, if you want to judge how much more it profits a person privately to be unjust rather than just. You will learn this most easily of all if you turn to the most complete injustice, the kind that makes the man who commits it happiest of all, and those who suffer it — who would themselves be unwilling to commit it — most wretched of all. This is tyranny, which does not take what belongs to others little by little, by stealth and by force, but {{{ 344b }}} wholesale — sacred property and profane, private and public alike; and when someone, in any one part of this, commits injustice and does not go undetected, he is punished and bears the greatest reproach (for those who do wrong in these particular ways are called temple-robbers, kidnappers, burglars, swindlers, and thieves), but when someone, besides the citizens' possessions, has enslaved and subjugated the citizens themselves too, instead of these shameful names he is called {{{ 344c }}} happy and blessed, not only by his fellow citizens but by everyone else who learns that he has committed injustice entire — for those who reproach injustice do so not because they are afraid of doing injustice but because they are afraid of suffering it. So it is, Socrates, that injustice, when it comes on a large enough scale, is stronger, freer, and more masterful than justice; and, just as I said from the start, the just turns out to be the advantage of the stronger, while the unjust is what is profitable and to one's own advantage.
