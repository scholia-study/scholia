## Why Musical Training Matters: Grace, and the Right Love

{{{ 400d }}} But surely, of good rhythm and bad rhythm, the one follows and resembles fine speech, and the other follows its opposite, and likewise with good harmony and disharmony, if indeed rhythm and harmony follow speech, as was just said, rather than speech following them.

But surely, he said, these must follow speech.

And what of the manner of diction, I said, and speech itself? Does it not follow the character of the soul?

How could it not?

And the rest follow diction?

Yes.

Fine speech, then, and good harmony, and good grace, and good rhythm {{{ 400e }}} follow good nature — not the folly that we flatteringly nickname [as good nature], but the understanding truly and finely established in its character.

Absolutely, he said.

Must not the young then pursue these everywhere, if they are to do their own work?

They must indeed pursue them. {{{ 401a }}}

Painting, I suppose, is full of these, and every craft of that kind, and so is weaving, and embroidery, and building, and in turn all the working of other implements, and further the nature of bodies and of other growing things — for in all of these there is grace or the lack of it. And ungracefulness, and bad rhythm, and disharmony are kin to bad speech and bad character, while their opposites are kin to, and likenesses of, the opposite — a moderate and good character.

Absolutely, he said. {{{ 401b }}}

Must we then oversee only the poets, and compel them to embody the image of good character in their poems or else not compose among us, or must we oversee the other craftsmen too and prevent them from embodying this ill character — licentious, illiberal, and graceless — in images of living things, or in buildings, or in anything else that is crafted, and must the one who is unable to do so not be allowed to practice his craft among us, so that our {{{ 401c }}} guardians, being reared among images of vice as if in bad pasture, cropping and grazing a little each day from many sources, should not unknowingly gather it together into one great evil in their own soul; but rather we must seek out those craftsmen gifted enough to track down the nature of the fine and the graceful, so that our young, living as if in a healthy place, may profit from everything, from wherever, out of fine works, something should strike their sight or hearing, like a breeze bringing health from {{{ 401d }}} wholesome regions, and may be led, unaware, from childhood on, into likeness and friendship and harmony with fine reason?

Yes, he said, for they would be reared most finely that way.

Now is it not for these reasons, I said, Glaucon, that education in music is most sovereign — that rhythm and harmony sink deepest into the inside of the soul and take the strongest hold on it, bringing grace with them, and make a person {{{ 401e }}} graceful if he is reared correctly, and if not, the opposite? And is it not also that the one reared there as he should be would perceive most keenly whatever is left out and not finely crafted or not finely grown, and, rightly repelled by them, would praise the fine things instead and, delighting in them and taking them into his soul, would be nourished by them and {{{ 402a }}} become fine and good, while he would rightly blame and hate the shameful things even while still young, before he is able to grasp the reason for it, and when reason comes, he who is thus reared would welcome it, recognizing it above all through its kinship?

At any rate it seems so to me, he said — that for reasons like these, education lies in music.

So then, I said, just as with letters, we were satisfied about them at the point when the elements — few as they are — no longer escaped our notice, appearing throughout everything in which they occur, and we did not disregard them whether in something small or {{{ 402b }}} great, thinking there was no need to attend to them, but everywhere we were eager to distinguish them, on the ground that we would not be literate before we could do this—

True.

And is it not also true that we would not recognize images of letters, if they should appear somewhere in water or in mirrors, before we knew the letters themselves — but that this belongs to the same craft and the same practice?

Absolutely.

Then is not this what I mean, by the gods — that in just this way we shall not be musical either, neither we ourselves nor those we say we must educate as our {{{ 402c }}} guardians, until we recognize the kinds of moderation and courage and liberality and magnificence, and everything akin to these, and in turn their opposites, circulating everywhere, and perceive them present in whatever they are present in, both themselves and their images, and neither in small things nor in great ones disregard them, but believe this to belong to the same craft and the same practice?

Quite necessarily, he said. {{{ 402d }}}

So then, I said, when fine character in the soul coincides in someone with an outward form that agrees and chimes with it, both sharing in the same pattern — would that not be the finest sight for anyone able to behold it?

Very much so.

And surely the finest is also the most lovable?

How could it not be?

It is such people above all, then, that the musical person would love; but if someone were out of harmony, he would not love him.

He would not, he said, if the lack were in the soul; but if it were something in the body, he would put up with it, so as to be willing {{{ 402e }}} to welcome him.

I understand, I said — that you have, or have had, a favorite of this sort, and I grant the point. But tell me this: is there any partnership between moderation and excessive pleasure?

And how could there be? It drives one out of one's mind, he said, no less than pain does?

But with virtue in general? {{{ 403a }}}

Not at all.

And what about this? With insolence and licentiousness?

Most of all.

Can you name a greater or keener pleasure than that of sex?

I cannot, he said, nor a madder one either.

But is it not the nature of the right kind of love to love what is orderly and fine, moderately and musically?

Very much so, he said.

Then nothing mad, or akin to licentiousness, must be brought near the right kind of love?

Nothing must be brought near it. {{{ 403b }}}

Then this pleasure must not be brought near it either, nor may lover and beloved, if they love and are loved rightly, share in it together?

No indeed, by Zeus, he said, Socrates, it must not be brought near.

So then, it seems, you will lay down as law in the city we are founding that a lover may kiss, and be with, and touch his favorite as a father would his son, for the sake of what is fine, if he persuades him, but that otherwise he must associate with the one he is eager for in such a way that he never seems to go further than {{{ 403c }}} this; and if he does, he will be liable to the reproach of a lack of culture and of taste.
