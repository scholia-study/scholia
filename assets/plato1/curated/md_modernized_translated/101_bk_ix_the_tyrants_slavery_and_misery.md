## The Tyrant's Slavery and Misery

{{{ 578b }}} It is with an eye to all this, I think, and to other things of the same kind, that you judged that city the most wretched of cities—

Is that not right? He said.

Very much so, I said. But about the tyrannical man now, in turn — what do you say, looking to these same things?

By far the most wretched of all the rest, he said.

There, I said, you are no longer right.

How so? He said.

This, I said, is not yet the man who is such in the highest degree.

But who then?

This one will perhaps still seem to you more wretched than he.

Which one? {{{ 578c }}}

Whoever, I said, being tyrannical in nature does not live out his life as a private citizen, but is unlucky enough that some misfortune furnishes him with the means of becoming an actual tyrant.

I gather, he said, from what has already been said, that you are speaking the truth.

Yes, I said, but we should not merely suppose such things, but examine them very carefully by just such reasoning; for our inquiry concerns the greatest matter of all, the good life and the bad.

Quite rightly, he said.

Consider, then, whether I am saying anything to the purpose. For it seems to me we must reflect {{{ 578d }}} on him by examining the following.

From what?

From each one of the private men — all those who are rich in their cities and own many slaves. For these men have at least this much in common with tyrants, ruling over many; but the tyrant's numbers are different.

Yes, they are different.

Do you know, then, that these men live free of fear and are not afraid of their household slaves?

For what would they be afraid of?

Nothing, I said; but do you see the reason?

Yes, because the whole city comes to the aid of each private citizen. {{{ 578e }}}

You put it well, I said. But what then? If some god should lift a single man — one who owns fifty slaves or even more — out of the city, together with his wife and children, and set him down in a desolate place with the rest of his property and his household slaves, where none of the free would be there to help him, in what sort of fear, and how much of it, do you suppose he would come to be, on account of himself and his children and his wife, lest they be destroyed by the slaves?

In every kind of fear, he said, for my part. {{{ 579a }}}

Would he not then be forced already to flatter some of his own slaves and to promise them a great deal, and to free them though nothing required it, and would he not be revealed as a flatterer of his own servants?

He would be under every compulsion, he said, or else be destroyed.

But what, I said, if the god should also settle around him in a circle many other neighbors, who would not tolerate it if anyone claimed the right to be master over another, but if they caught anyone doing such a thing anywhere, would punish him with the most extreme penalties? {{{ 579b }}}

He would, he said, I think, be even more completely beset by every trouble, guarded all around by nothing but enemies.

Is the tyrant, then, not bound in just such a prison, being by nature such as we have described, full of every sort of fear and erotic love; and though his soul is ravenous, he alone of those in the city may not travel abroad anywhere, nor see the sights the other free men long to see, but lives shut away in his house for the most part like a woman, {{{ 579c }}} envying even the other citizens if one of them travels abroad and sees something good?

Quite so indeed, he said.

Does not the man reap even more of such troubles who governs himself badly — the one you just now judged most wretched, the tyrannical man — in that he does not live out his life as a private citizen, but is forced by some turn of fortune to become an actual tyrant and, unable to rule himself, undertakes to rule others as well, just as if a man with a sick body, one that has no mastery of itself, were forced to live not in private but contending {{{ 579d }}} against other bodies and fighting?

What you say, he said, is altogether most apt and most true, Socrates.

Is not this condition, then, my dear Glaucon, I said, wretched through and through, and does the man who plays the tyrant live a life still harder than the man you judged to have the hardest life of all?

Quite so, he said.

So it is in truth, even if it does not seem so to some, that the man who is really a tyrant is really a slave, reduced to the greatest fawning and servility {{{ 579e }}} and a flatterer of the basest men, and that, since he satisfies his desires not in the least, he appears, to anyone able to view the whole of his soul, most in want of very many things and truly poor, and full of fear throughout the whole of his life, full of convulsions and pains, if indeed he resembles the condition of the city he rules. And he does resemble it; is that not so?

Very much so, he said. {{{ 580a }}}

Shall we not, then, in addition to this, further assign to the man what we said before, that of necessity he is — and on account of his rule becomes even more than before — envious, faithless, unjust, friendless, impious, a host and nurse of every vice, and that as a result of all this he is above all unlucky himself, and moreover makes those near him such as well?

No one, he said, who has any sense will contradict you.

Come now, tell me, I said — just as a judge who weighs everything {{{ 580b }}} renders his verdict, so you too declare who is first in happiness in your opinion and who second, and judge the rest, five in number, in order: kingly, timocratic, oligarchic, democratic, tyrannical.

But the judgment is easy, he said. For just as they came on, so I judge them, as though they were choruses, by virtue and vice and by happiness and its opposite.

Shall we hire a herald, then, I said, or shall I myself proclaim that the son of Ariston judged the best and most just man to be happiest {{{ 580c }}}, and that this man is the most kingly and reigns as king over himself, while the worst and most unjust man is the most wretched, and that this in turn happens to be whoever, being most tyrannical, plays the tyrant most of all over himself and over the city?

Let it be proclaimed by you, he said.

Shall I add to the proclamation, then, I said — whether or not they escape the notice of all men and gods in being such?

Proclaim it, he said.

Very well then, I said; this then would be one proof for us, {{{ 580d }}} but look now at this second one, to see whether there seems to be anything in it.
