## Polemarchus Interrupts: The Community of Women and Children

{{{ 449a }}} Such a city, then, and such a constitution I call good and right, and the man who is like it — but the others, if this one is right, I call bad and mistaken, both in the government of cities and in the framing of a private person's character of soul, since they fall under four kinds of badness.

What are these, then? He asked.

And I was going on to describe the constitutions that came next, as they each seemed to me to pass over out of one another — {{{ 449b }}} but Polemarchus (he was sitting a little farther off than Adeimantus) reached out his hand, took hold of his cloak from above at the shoulder, drew him toward himself, and, leaning forward, bent over and said something, of which we caught nothing else but this: shall we let him off, then, he said, or what are we to do?

Not in the least, said Adeimantus, already speaking up loudly.

And I asked, What exactly is it that you are not letting go?

You, he said. {{{ 449c }}}

Why exactly, I said, is that?

You seem to us, he said, to be shirking — stealing a whole kind of thing out of the argument, and not the smallest one, so that you would not have to go through it, and thinking you could slip it past us by treating it lightly, as if it were obvious to everyone that, when it comes to women and children, friends hold everything in common.

And is it not correct, I said, Adeimantus?

Yes, he said. But this rightness, like everything else, needs some account of what the manner of the community is; for there could be many kinds of it. {{{ 449d }}} So do not leave out which manner you mean; for we have long been waiting, thinking that somewhere you would mention the begetting of children — how they will be begotten — and, once born, how they will be raised, and this whole community of women and children that you speak of; for we think it matters a great deal — indeed everything — to the constitution, whether it comes about rightly or not. So now, since you are taking up another constitution before adequately settling these matters, we have resolved on this {{{ 450a }}} — which you heard — that you are not to be released until you have gone through all of it, just as you did the rest.

Count me too, then, said Glaucon, as a partner in this vote.

Have no doubt of it, said Thrasymachus — consider this settled by all of us, Socrates.

What a thing you have done, I said, by taking hold of me. What a discussion you are stirring up all over again, as if from the beginning, about the constitution — one I, for my part, was glad to think I had already finished, content if someone would let it stand as accepted, just as it was then stated. By now calling me back to these things, {{{ 450b }}} you do not know what a swarm of arguments you are rousing — one I saw and let go at the time, for fear it should cause a great deal of trouble.

What? Thrasymachus said — do you think these men have come here now to smelt gold, rather than to listen to arguments?

Yes, I said, to a moderate amount of them, at least.

But the proper measure for listening to arguments like these, Socrates, said Glaucon, is a whole lifetime, at least for men of sense. But never mind our part; as for what we are asking, do not by any means grow weary of setting it out {{{ 450c }}} in whatever way seems best to you — what the community of children and women will be for us, the guardians, and the rearing of them while they are still young, that is, the begetting and the education that takes place in the time between, which indeed seems to be the most laborious part. So try to say in what manner it must come about.

It is not easy, my fortunate friend, I said, to go through it; for it holds many grounds for disbelief — even more than the things we went through before. For it would be doubted that such things are even possible, and, even if they were to come about as fully as could be, that they would be best — on that score too there will be disbelief. {{{ 450d }}} That, then, is why there is some hesitation about even touching on them, for fear the account should seem to be no more than a wish, my dear friend.

Do not hesitate at all, he said — those who are going to listen are neither unreasonable, nor distrustful, nor ill-disposed.

And I said, My good man, is it perhaps because you want to encourage me that you say this?

I do, he said.

Then you are doing exactly the opposite, I said. For if I trusted myself to know what I am saying, the encouragement would be well placed; for among sensible people and friends, to speak the truth about the greatest and {{{ 450e }}} dearest matters, when one knows it, is safe and emboldening, whereas to make one's arguments while doubting and still searching, which is indeed {{{ 451a }}} what I am doing, is fearful and precarious — not that I fear incurring laughter, for that at least would be childish — but that, having slipped from the truth, I may drag down not only myself but my friends along with me, and come to grief in the very matters where one ought least of all to slip. But I bow down to Adrasteia, Glaucon, on account of what I am about to say; for I believe it a lesser wrong to become, unwillingly, the killer of someone than to become a deceiver about fine and good and just customs. So it is better to run this risk among enemies than {{{ 451b }}} among friends, and that is why you do well to encourage me.

And Glaucon, laughing, said, But Socrates, if we come to any harm from the argument, we acquit you, as one acquitted of murder, and declare you clean and no deceiver of us. So take courage and speak.

Well, but even so, I said, the one who is acquitted there is also counted clean, as the law says — and presumably, if there, then here too.

Speak, then, he said, on that account at least.

Then I must now say, I said, in reverse order, what perhaps {{{ 451c }}} ought to have been said then in its proper place; but perhaps it would be right to do it this way — once the drama of the men has been played out fully, to go through in turn that of the women, especially since you challenge me to it.
