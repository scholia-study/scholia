## The Charm of Poetry, and the Old Quarrel

Quite so.

Yet we have not so far brought against it the gravest charge. For that it is capable of corrupting even decent people — apart from a very few — is surely altogether dreadful.

Why would it not, if indeed it does this?

Listen and consider. For surely, when the best of us listen to Homer or one of the other tragic poets imitating {{{ 605d }}} one of the heroes in grief and drawing out a long speech in his lamentations, or singing and beating their breasts, you know that we feel delight and, giving ourselves over, follow along in sympathy, and earnestly praise as a good poet whoever affects us most in this way.

I know it well — how could I not?

But when a grief of our own comes to one of us, do you notice, in turn, that we pride ourselves on the opposite — if we are able to keep {{{ 605e }}} quiet and endure it — since this is a man's part, while that which we praised before is a woman's?

I do, he said.

Then is it a fine thing, I said, that this praise holds — that in seeing a man of a kind one would not think worthy to be oneself, but would be ashamed to be, one feels no disgust but takes delight and praises him?

No, by Zeus, he said — it does not seem reasonable. {{{ 606a }}}

Yes, I said — if you were to look at it from that angle.

In what way?

If you were to reflect that the part which is then forcibly held in check amid our own private misfortunes, and hungers to weep and lament fully and have its fill of it, being by nature the sort of thing that desires these things, is the very part that is filled and gladdened by the poets, while the part of us that is by nature best, since it has not been adequately trained by reason or by habit, relaxes its guard over this {{{ 606b }}} tearful part — seeing that it is watching another's sufferings, and that there is no shame to itself in praising and pitying another man who, though he claims to be good, grieves when he ought not — but instead reckons that it gains something by this, namely the pleasure, and would not consent to be deprived of it by holding the whole poem in contempt. For I think that few people reason that the enjoyment taken from others' griefs necessarily carries over into one's own; for once one has nourished the pitying impulse to be strong on those occasions, it is not easy to restrain it in one's own sufferings. {{{ 606c }}}

Very true, he said.

Is not the same account true also of the comic? That is, if you yourself would be ashamed to play the clown, but in comic imitation, or even hearing it privately, you take great delight in it and do not hate it as low, are you not doing the very same thing you did in the case of pity? For the impulse in yourself that wants to play the clown, which you were holding back by reason for fear of a reputation for buffoonery, you now let loose again; and by giving it free rein there, you have often, without realizing it, been carried away in your own affairs to the point of becoming a comedian.

Very much so, he said. {{{ 606d }}}

And indeed the same holds for sexual desire, and spirit, and all the appetitive things, and the painful and pleasant things in the soul, which we say accompany us in every action — that poetic imitation produces such effects in us; for it nourishes and waters these things when they ought to be left to wither, and sets them up as rulers over us, when they ought to be ruled if we are to become better and happier instead of worse and more wretched.

I cannot say otherwise, he said. {{{ 606e }}}

So then, Glaucon, I said, whenever you meet admirers of Homer who say that this poet has educated Greece, and that for the management and education of human affairs he is worth taking up and learning from, and that one should order one's whole life {{{ 607a }}} by this poet and live so, you must love and welcome them as the best men they are capable of being, and grant that Homer is the most poetic of poets and the first of the tragedians, but you must know that only so much of poetry as consists of hymns to the gods and praises of good men is to be admitted into the city; for if you admit the sweetened Muse, in lyric or epic verse, pleasure and pain will reign as kings in your city instead of law and the argument that has always, by common consent, been held to be best.

Very true, he said. {{{ 607b }}}

Let this, then, I said, stand as our defense concerning poetry, now that we have brought it back to mind — that it was reasonable of us to send it away from the city then, being the sort of thing it is; for the argument compelled us. But let us say to it, so that it does not condemn us for some harshness and boorishness, that there is an old quarrel between philosophy and poetry; for indeed there is "that yelping bitch, barking at her master," and "great amid the empty chatter of fools," {{{ 607c }}} and "the mob of the over-wise holding sway," and "the subtle thinkers who are, for all that, paupers," and countless other signs of this ancient opposition between the two. Nevertheless, let it be said that we, at least, would gladly welcome it back if pleasure-seeking poetry and imitation could offer any argument that it ought to have a place in a well-ordered city, since we are indeed conscious in ourselves of being charmed by it; but it is not holy to betray what seems to be true. Is it not so, my friend — are you not charmed by it too, {{{ 607d }}} and especially when you view it through Homer?

Very much indeed.

Then is it not just that it should return in this way, having offered its defense in lyric verse or some other meter?

Quite so.

And we might indeed grant, too, to its champions — those who are not themselves poets, but lovers of poetry — to speak on its behalf without meter, arguing that it is not only pleasant but also beneficial to constitutions and to human life; and we will listen favorably. {{{ 607e }}} For we shall surely profit, if it turns out to be not only pleasant but also beneficial.

How, he said, could we fail to profit?

But if it cannot, my dear friend and companion, then — just as those who have once fallen in erotic love with someone, if they come to believe that the erotic love is not beneficial, hold themselves back from it by force, though reluctantly — so too shall we, because of the erotic love for such poetry that has grown in us through the upbringing of {{{ 608a }}} noble constitutions, be well disposed toward its proving to be as good and as true as possible; but as long as it is not able to make its defense, we shall listen to it while chanting to ourselves, as a charm, this very account we are giving, guarding against falling back again into that erotic love of childhood, the erotic love of the many. We shall sing, then, that such poetry is not to be taken seriously, as though it grasped truth and were a serious thing, but that whoever listens to it must be on {{{ 608b }}} his guard, fearing for the constitution within himself, and must hold to exactly what we have said about poetry.

I agree entirely, he said.

For the contest is great, I said, dear Glaucon — great, and greater than it seems — the contest of becoming good or bad; so that it is not worth a man's while, lifted up by honor or by money or by any office, or indeed by poetry, to neglect justice and the rest of virtue.

I agree with you, he said, on the basis of what we have gone through; and I think anyone else would too.
