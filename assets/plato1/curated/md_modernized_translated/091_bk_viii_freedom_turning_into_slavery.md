## Freedom Turning Into Slavery

{{{ 562a }}} What then? Is this man to be ranked by us under democracy, as one who would rightly be called democratic?

Let him be so ranked, he said.

Then, I said, it remains for us to discuss the finest constitution and the finest man of all — tyranny and the tyrant.

Quite so, he said.

Come then, my dear friend, in what way does tyranny come about? That it changes over from democracy is fairly clear.

Clear.

Is it not, then, in some sense the same way that democracy {{{ 562b }}} comes from oligarchy, and tyranny from democracy?

How so?

The good they set before themselves, I said, and because of which oligarchy was established — this was [excessive] wealth; was it not? —

Yes.

Then insatiable desire for wealth, and neglect of everything else for the sake of money-making, destroyed it.

True, he said.

Is it not then also the insatiable desire for that which democracy defines as good that destroys it in turn?

And what do you say it defines as good?

Freedom, I said. For that, I suppose, is what you would hear said in a {{{ 562c }}} city under democracy — that it possesses this as its finest thing, and that for this reason it alone is worth living in for anyone who is free by nature.

That saying is certainly repeated often, he said.

Is it not then, I said, just as I was about to say a moment ago, that the insatiable desire for this and neglect of everything else changes this constitution too, and prepares it to need a tyranny?

In what way? He asked.

Whenever, I suppose, a city under democracy, thirsting for freedom, {{{ 562d }}} happens to have bad cupbearers presiding over it, and becomes drunk on unmixed wine beyond what is fitting, then, if its rulers are not very gentle and do not give it freedom in abundance, it punishes them, accusing them of being vile and oligarchic.

They certainly do that, he said.

And it heaps abuse, I said, on those who are obedient to the rulers, as willing slaves and nothing at all, while it praises and honors, both privately and publicly, rulers who are like the ruled and ruled who are like the rulers. Is it not necessary that in such a {{{ 562e }}} city freedom go to every extreme?

How could it not?

And that this anarchy, my friend, I said, sinks down into private households, and ends by working its way into the very animals.

What do we mean, he asked, by a thing like that?

For example, I said, a father grows accustomed to becoming like his child, and fears his sons, while a son becomes like his father, and feels neither shame nor fear before his parents, so that he may be free; a resident alien becomes equal to the {{{ 563a }}} citizen, and the citizen to the resident alien, and likewise the foreigner.

Yes, that is indeed how it happens, he said.

These things happen, I said, and other small things like them as well; a teacher in such a place fears his pupils and fawns on them, while pupils make light of their teachers, and likewise of their attendants; and in general the young set themselves up as equal to their elders and compete with them, in word and in deed, while the old come down to the level of the young and fill themselves with {{{ 563b }}} wit and playfulness, imitating the young, so that they may not seem disagreeable or overbearing.

Very much so, he said.

But the extreme of the people's freedom, my friend, I said, as much of it as occurs in such a city, is when the bought men and the bought women are no less free than those who bought them. And how great the equality and freedom is that arises between women toward men and men toward women — we very nearly forgot to mention it. {{{ 563c }}}

Then shall we not say, he said, in the words of Aeschylus, whatever has now come to our lips?

Quite so, I said — and that is indeed what I mean too; for no one without experience of it would believe how much freer the animals kept by men are here than elsewhere. For quite literally, according to the proverb, the dogs become just like their mistresses, and so too do the horses and donkeys, accustomed to walking about in a thoroughly free and dignified manner, barging into anyone they meet in the streets who does not get out of the way, and everything else likewise becomes {{{ 563d }}} full of freedom.

You are telling my own dream back to me, he said; for I myself, when walking out to the country, often experience just that.

And the sum of all these things gathered together, I said, do you notice how tender it makes the soul of the citizens, so that if anyone brings any form of slavery near them at all, they grow indignant and cannot bear it? For in the end, I suppose you know, they pay no heed even to the laws, written or unwritten, so that in no way {{{ 563e }}} at all may anyone be their master.

I know it very well, he said.

This, then, my friend, I said, is the beginning, so fine and full of youthful vigor, from which tyranny grows, as it seems to me.

Full of youthful vigor indeed, he said; but what comes after this?

The same thing, I said, as the disease that arose in oligarchy and destroyed it — this same disease, arising in this constitution too, greater and stronger because of its license, enslaves democracy. And in truth, doing anything to excess tends to produce a great change into its opposite, in seasons and in {{{ 564a }}} plants and in bodies, and indeed, not least of all, in constitutions.

That is likely, he said.

For excessive freedom seems to change into nothing other than excessive slavery, both for the private man and for the city.

That is indeed likely.

It is reasonable, then, I said, that tyranny is established from no other constitution than democracy — from the most extreme freedom, I suppose, comes the greatest and most savage slavery.

Yes, that stands to reason, he said.

But I do not think, I said, that is what you were asking, but rather what {{{ 564b }}} the disease is that grows in oligarchy and, arising the same in democracy, enslaves it.

True, he said.

Well, I said, I meant that class of idle and extravagant men — the boldest of them leading, the less bold following — whom indeed we compare to drones, some equipped with stings, others without.

And rightly so, he said.

These two, then, I said, disturb every constitution when they arise in it, just as phlegm and bile disturb the body; against which indeed {{{ 564c }}} the good physician and lawgiver of a city must guard well in advance, no less than a wise beekeeper — above all so that they may not arise at all, but if they do arise, so that they may be cut out, comb and all, as quickly as possible.

Yes, by Zeus, he said, absolutely.

Let us then take it up this way, I said, so that we may see more clearly what we want.

How?

Let us divide a city under democracy, in our discussion, into three parts, {{{ 564d }}} just as indeed it actually is. For one such class, I suppose, grows up in it because of its license, no less than in the city under oligarchy.

So it is.

But it is much sharper here than there.

How so?

There, because it has no honor but is driven away from office, it becomes untrained and lacking in vigor; but in democracy this class is, I suppose, for the most part what stands at its head, apart from a few, and the sharpest part of it speaks and acts, while the rest, settling around the speakers' platforms, buzzes and will not tolerate anyone {{{ 564e }}} saying otherwise, so that in such a constitution everything is managed by this class, apart from a few exceptions.

Very much so, he said.

Then another such class is always separated off from the multitude.

What sort?

When everyone, I suppose, is engaged in money-making, those who are most orderly by nature generally become the wealthiest.

Likely.

It is from these, I suppose, that the most honey for the drones is taken, and the most easily got.

How indeed, he said, could anyone extract honey from those who have little?

Such rich men, I suppose, are called the pasture of the drones.

Just about, he said.
