## Third Proof: Pleasure, Pain, and the State Between

{{{ 583b }}} So then, these would be two victories in a row, the just man twice victorious over the unjust; but for the third — in Olympic fashion, to the Savior and to Olympian Zeus — consider that the pleasure of everyone but the man of practical wisdom is not even wholly true, nor pure, but is a kind of shadow-painting, as I think I have heard from one of the wise. And yet this would be the greatest and most decisive of the falls.

Very much so — but what do you mean? {{{ 583c }}}

I will find it out this way, I said — searching it out while you answer.

Ask, then, he said.

Tell me then, I said — do we not say that pain is the opposite of pleasure?

Very much so.

And is there not also something which is neither rejoicing nor being pained?

There is indeed.

Is it not something between these two, lying in the middle — a kind of stillness of the soul in regard to these matters? Or is that not how you describe it?

Just so, he said.

Do you remember, then, I said, the talk of sick people — what they say whenever they are ill?

What things?

That nothing, after all, is more pleasant than being healthy, but it had {{{ 583d }}} escaped their notice, before they fell ill, that this was the most pleasant thing of all.

I remember, he said.

And do you also hear those gripped by some intense pain saying that nothing is more pleasant than the ceasing of pain?

I hear it.

And in many other such cases too, I think, you notice people falling into this condition, in which, whenever they are in pain, they praise not being in pain, and the stillness that comes with it, as the most pleasant thing — not rejoicing itself.

For that, he said, is what then becomes pleasant, perhaps, and welcome — stillness. {{{ 583e }}}

And so when someone ceases rejoicing, I said, the stillness that follows the pleasure will be painful.

Perhaps, he said.

So the thing we just now said was between the two — the stillness — will sometimes be both of them, both pain and pleasure.

So it seems.

But is it even possible for what is neither to become both?

It does not seem so to me.

And surely what is pleasant, arising in the soul, and what is painful, are both a kind of motion — or are they not?

Yes. {{{ 584a }}}

But did not what is neither painful nor pleasant just now appear to be stillness — and in the middle of these two?

It did appear so.

How then can it be correct to consider not being in pain as pleasant, or not rejoicing as distressing?

Not at all.

This, then, is not really so, I said, but at such a moment the stillness merely appears pleasant beside what is painful and painful beside what is pleasant, and none of these appearances is sound in relation to the truth of pleasure, but rather a kind of sorcery.

So the argument indicates, at any rate, he said. {{{ 584b }}}

Look, then, I said, at pleasures that do not arise from pains, so that you may not too readily suppose, for the moment, that this is how things naturally stand — that pleasure is simply the cessation of pain, and pain the cessation of pleasure.

Where, then, he said, and what sort do you mean?

There are many others, I said, but especially, if you care to consider them, the pleasures of smell. For these, without any prior pain, suddenly become immeasurably great, and once they cease, they leave no pain behind at all.

Most true, he said. {{{ 584c }}}

Let us not, then, be persuaded that release from pain is pure pleasure, or release from pleasure pure pain.

No, let us not.

But nevertheless, I said, the pleasures that pass through the body to reach the soul, the ones we call by that name — most of them, and the greatest — are of this kind: certain releases from pains.

They are, indeed.

And do not the anticipatory pleasures and pains too, which arise out of expectation before such things actually come, stand in the same way?

In the same way. {{{ 584d }}}

Do you know, then, I said, what sort of pleasures these are, and what they most resemble?

What? He said.

Do you think, I said, that there is something in nature that is up, something that is down, and something in the middle?

I do.

Do you think, then, that someone being carried from below toward the middle would suppose anything other than that he is being carried upward? And that, standing in the middle and looking back at the place he has been carried from, he would suppose he was anywhere else than in the upper region — never having seen the truly upper region?

By Zeus, he said, I do not think such a man could suppose otherwise. {{{ 584e }}}

But if he were carried back again, I said, would he not think he was being carried downward, and think truly?

How could he not?

Would he not undergo all this because he has no experience of what is truly up, and in the middle, and down?

Clearly, indeed.
