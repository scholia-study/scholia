## What the Rulers Must Guard: Education as the One Great Thing

No, by Zeus, he said.

Then this, I said, would be the finest boundary for our rulers — how large the city must be made, and, once it has reached that size, how much territory it should mark off, letting the rest go.

What boundary? he asked.

I think, I said, this: let it grow up to the point where, as it grows, it is willing to remain one city, and no further. {{{ 423c }}}

And nicely put, too, he said.

Then this, in turn, is another command we shall lay on the guardians — to guard in every way that the city be neither small nor merely seem great, but be some one city, sufficient in size.

And perhaps, he said, it is a slight command we shall be laying on them.

And even slighter than that, I said, is this one, which we also mentioned earlier when we said it was necessary — if a poor child is born among the guardians, that he be sent off to the others, {{{ 423d }}} but if a worthy one is born among the others, that he be sent to the guardians. This was meant to show that the other citizens too, each one, must be directed toward that single task for which he is naturally fitted, so that each, practicing his own one thing, may become not many but one, and so the whole city too may grow to be one and not many.

Yes, he said, this one is a smaller matter than that.

It is not, my good Adeimantus, I said, as one might suppose, that the commands we are laying on them are many and great; {{{ 423e }}} all of them are slight, provided they guard the one great thing, as the saying has it — or rather, instead of great, the one sufficient thing.

What is this? So he asked.

Education, I said, and upbringing: for if men, being well educated, become reasonable, they will easily discern all these matters — and the others too, which we are now passing over: the possession of wives, marriages, and the begetting of children — namely, that {{{ 424a }}} according to the proverb, all these things must be made as far as possible common among friends.

That would come about most rightly, he said.

Moreover, I said, a constitution, once it has made a good start, goes on growing like a circle: for sound upbringing and education, when preserved, produce good natures, and in turn good natures, laying hold of such education, grow up even better than those before them, both for other things and for {{{ 424b }}} begetting, as also among the other animals.

Likely so, he said.

To put this briefly, then: the overseers of the city must hold fast to this — so that its corruption does not escape their notice, but that they guard it above all else, not innovating in gymnastic or in music against the established order, but guarding it as best they can — taking alarm whenever someone says that
+ … men give more heed to that song
+ which hovers newest among the singers,
{{{ 424c }}} for fear that someone may suppose the poet means not new songs but a new manner of song, and may praise it. One must neither praise such a thing nor accept it. For one must be wary of changing to a new kind of music, as putting the whole at risk; nowhere are the modes of music moved without the greatest laws of the city being moved too, as Damon says, and I for one am persuaded.

Then count me too, said Adeimantus, among the persuaded. {{{ 424d }}}

So it seems, I said, the guardians must build their watch-post somewhere here, in music.

Lawlessness, at any rate, he said, easily slips in unnoticed.

Yes, I said, as if it were part of play, and as if it were doing no harm.

For indeed, he said, it does nothing else but settle in by degrees and quietly flow into people's characters and pursuits; and from there it issues, grown greater, into their dealings with one another; and from these dealings it goes on {{{ 424e }}} to the laws and constitutions, Socrates, with a great deal of insolence, until at last it overturns everything, private and public alike.

Well then, I said, is that how it is?

So it seems to me, he said.

Then, as we were saying from the beginning, I said, must not our children from the very start take part in more lawful play, since, if their play becomes lawless, and the children with it, it is impossible for law-abiding and serious {{{ 425a }}} men to grow up from them?

How could it be otherwise? So he said.

So then, whenever children, having begun their play well, receive good order through music, the opposite of what happened to those others follows them into everything and grows with them, setting right whatever in the city had gone wrong before.

True indeed, he said.

And so these people, I said, rediscover even the small things that pass for matters of custom — all of which those before them had let go.

Which ⟨ones⟩? {{{ 425b }}}

Things like these: the silence that is fitting for the young before their elders; how they take their places at table, and their rising to give way; care for parents; and haircuts, dress, and footwear, and the whole fashioning of the body, and all the other such things. Or do you not think so?

I do.

But to legislate about these things, I think, is naive; for such things neither come about nor would they last through being enacted in speech and writing.

How could they?

At any rate, Adeimantus, I said, it is likely that whichever way one's education {{{ 425c }}} sets out, what follows will be of the same kind. Or does not like always call forth like?

Of course.

And in the end, I think, we would say that it turns out to be one complete and full-grown result, either good or else the opposite.

Why not indeed? So he said.

For my part, then, I said, it is because of these things that I would no longer attempt to legislate about such matters.

Reasonably so, he said.

But what, in the name of the gods, I said, about these matters of the marketplace — the contracts that people make with one another there, {{{ 425d }}} and, if you like, contracts among craftsmen, and slander, and assault, and the filing of suits, and the appointment of jurors, and whatever dues, whether their collection or their assessment, are needed in the markets or the harbors, or, generally, any market-regulations or city-regulations or harbor-regulations or anything else of the kind — shall we dare to legislate anything about these?

No, he said, it is not fitting to dictate to men who are fine and good; {{{ 425e }}} most of these things, whatever needs to be enacted, they will easily discover for themselves.

Yes, my friend, I said, provided a god grants them the preservation of the laws we went through before.

And if not, he said, they will spend their lives always laying down and revising many such things, thinking they will hit upon the best.

You mean, I said, that such people will live like the sick, who, from lack of restraint, are unwilling to give up their bad regimen.

Quite so. {{{ 426a }}}

And indeed, they carry on quite charmingly; for being doctored, they accomplish nothing, except that they make their illnesses more complicated and worse, and are always hoping, if someone recommends a remedy, that by it they will become well.

Yes indeed, he said, such are the experiences of those who are sick in this way.

And what of this? I said: is not this charming in them too — that they regard as their worst enemy the man who tells them the truth, because, until he stops getting drunk and stuffing himself and indulging in sex and lazing about, {{{ 426b }}} neither drugs, nor cauterizing, nor surgery, nor incantations, nor amulets, nor anything else of that kind will do him any good?

Not very charming, he said, for there is no charm in being angry at one who speaks well.

You are not, it seems, I said, an admirer of such men.

No, by Zeus, indeed not.

Then neither will you praise a city, if, as we were just saying, it acts this way as a whole. Or do they not seem to you to be doing the very same thing as those cities which, being badly governed, proclaim {{{ 426c }}} to their citizens that no one is to disturb the established order of the city as a whole, on pain of death for whoever does so — while whoever most agreeably serves them as they govern in this way, and indulges them, fawning on them and anticipating their wishes, and is clever at satisfying them, this man will be accounted a good man, and wise in great matters, and will be honored by them?

Just the same thing, indeed, he said, they seem to me to be doing, and I do not praise it in the least. {{{ 426d }}}

But what, in turn, of those who are willing and eager to serve such cities? Do you not admire their courage and their easy readiness?

I do, he said, except for those who have been deceived by them, and think that in truth they are statesmen, because they are praised by the many.

What do you mean? Have you no sympathy, I said, for these men? Or do you think it possible for a man who does not know how to measure, when many other {{{ 426e }}} such men tell him that he is four cubits tall, not to believe this about himself?

No, not that, at least, he said.

Then do not be harsh with them; for such people are, I dare say, the most charming of all — legislating the sort of things we just went through, and revising them, always thinking they will find some final limit to the wrongdoing in contracts, and to the things I was just speaking of, unaware that they are, in truth, cutting at a Hydra.
