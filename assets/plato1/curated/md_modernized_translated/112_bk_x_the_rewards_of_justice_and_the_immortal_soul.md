## The Rewards of Justice, and the Immortal Soul

{{{ 608c }}} And yet, I said, we have not gone through the greatest rewards of virtue and the prizes set before it.

You are speaking of an inconceivable magnitude, he said, if there are other things greater than what has been said.

But what, I said, could become great in so short a time? For this whole span, from childhood to old age, would surely be a short one, set against all time.

Nothing, certainly, he said.

What then? Do you think that, in the case of an immortal thing, one need be in earnest only for so much {{{ 608d }}} time as this, and not for all time?

I think so, he said — but what do you mean by this?

Have you not perceived, I said, that our soul is immortal and never perishes?

And he, glancing at me in astonishment, said: by Zeus, not I — but can you say this?

Unless I am doing wrong, I said. And I think you can too — it is nothing difficult.

Not for me either, he said — but I should be glad to hear this easy thing from you.

You shall hear, I said.

Just speak, he said.

Is there something you call good, I said, and something evil?

I do, he said. {{{ 608e }}}

Do you think of them, then, as I do?

In what way?

That the evil is everything that destroys and corrupts, and the good is everything that preserves and benefits.

I do, he said.

But what of this? Do you say there is some evil and some good peculiar to each thing? For instance, ophthalmia {{{ 609a }}} for eyes, and disease for the whole body; blight for grain, rot for wood, rust for bronze and iron — and, as I say, for practically everything, some inborn evil and disease?

I do, he said.

Then whenever one of these attaches itself to something, does it not make the thing it attaches to bad, and in the end dissolve and destroy the whole of it?

How could it be otherwise?

So each thing's inborn evil, its own badness, destroys it — or, if this does not destroy it, then nothing else could ever still {{{ 609b }}} corrupt it. For the good would never destroy anything, nor again would that which is neither bad nor good.

How could it? He said.

If, then, we find something among existing things that has an evil making it bad, but this evil is unable to dissolve and destroy it, shall we not already know that for a thing so constituted there was no destruction?

That is likely, he said.

What then? Is there not, I said, something that makes the soul itself bad?

Very much so, he said — all those we just went through: injustice, {{{ 609c }}} licentiousness, cowardice, and ignorance.

Does any of these, then, dissolve and destroy it? And take care that we not be deceived into supposing that the unjust and foolish man, when caught in wrongdoing, has then been destroyed by his injustice, injustice being a badness of the soul. But consider it this way — just as the body's own badness, being disease, wastes and destroys it, and brings it to the point of not even being a body, so too all the things we just mentioned, {{{ 609d }}} through their own peculiar evil, which corrupts them by settling in and being present within them, arrive at not-being — is that not so?

Yes.

Come then, consider the soul too in the same way. Do injustice and the rest of vice, being in the soul, corrupt and wither it by their presence and by settling in it, until they lead it to death and separate it from the body?

Certainly not that, he said.

But surely this at least is unreasonable, I said — that a thing should be destroyed by another's badness and not by its own.

Unreasonable. {{{ 609e }}}

For consider, Glaucon, I said, that we do not think the body need perish even from the badness of the food itself — whether that badness is staleness, or rottenness, or whatever it may be; but if the badness of the food produces in the body a badness of body, we shall say that the body perished, on account of the food, by its own evil, which is a disease; whereas, the badness of {{{ 610a }}} food being one thing and the body another, we shall never allow that the body is corrupted by a foreign evil that has not produced in it its own inborn evil.

Again you speak most correctly, he said.

By the same reasoning, then, I said, unless a badness of body produces in the soul a badness of soul, let us never allow that the soul is destroyed by a foreign evil apart from its own badness — one thing by the evil of another.

That is reasonable, he said.

Let us, then, either refute this and show that we are not speaking rightly, or, {{{ 610b }}} as long as it remains unrefuted, let us never say that, because of fever, or some other disease, or slaughter, or even if someone should cut the whole body into the smallest possible pieces, the soul is on that account any more destroyed, until someone demonstrates that these sufferings of the body make the soul itself more unjust and more unholy; and when a foreign evil arises in one thing but each thing's own evil does not arise within it, {{{ 610c }}} let us allow no one to say that either the soul or anything else is destroyed.

But surely, he said, no one will ever show this — that the souls of the dying become more unjust because of death.

But if someone, I said, dares to meet this argument head-on and say that the dying man becomes worse and more unjust, so as not to be forced to admit that souls are immortal, we shall, I suppose, insist that, if the one who says this speaks truly, injustice is deadly to the one who has it, like a disease, and that those who catch it die by {{{ 610d }}} this very thing, which kills by its own nature — some very quickly, others more slowly — and not, as now, that the unjust die on this account at the hands of others who inflict the penalty.

By Zeus, he said, injustice will not, then, appear altogether terrible, if it is to be deadly to the one who has it — for that would be a release from evils — but rather, I think, it will appear quite the opposite: killing {{{ 610e }}} others, if that is even possible, while making the one who has it very much alive, and, besides being alive, sleepless as well; so far off, it seems, has it pitched its tent from being deadly.

Well put, I said. For since its own peculiar badness and its own peculiar evil are not enough to kill and destroy the soul, an evil appointed for the destruction of something else will hardly destroy the soul, or anything else, except that for which it is appointed.

Hardly, he said, as is likely.

So then, since it is destroyed by no evil at all, neither {{{ 611a }}} its own nor another's, it is clear that it must always exist; and if it always exists, it is immortal.
