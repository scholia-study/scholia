## Wealth, Poverty, and the Right Size of a City

Well, he said, I think you put that well.

Then, I said, will you think I am speaking reasonably about its counterpart too?

What exactly do you mean? {{{ 421d }}}

Consider in turn whether the following things ruin the other craftsmen too, so that they become bad as well.

What things do you mean?

Wealth, I said, and poverty.

How so?

Like this. Once a potter grows rich, do you think he will still want to attend to his craft?

Not at all, he said.

Will he not become more idle and careless than he was before?

Much more so.

So does he not become a worse potter?

That too, he said, by a great deal.

And what is more, unable through poverty to supply himself with tools or anything else his craft needs, he will produce poorer work {{{ 421e }}} and will teach his sons, or whoever else he teaches, to be worse craftsmen.

How could he not?

So under the influence of both, poverty and wealth, the works of the crafts grow worse, and so do the craftsmen themselves.

So it appears.

Then it seems we have found other things for the guardians, which must be guarded against in every way, so that these never slip unnoticed into the city.

What things are these? {{{ 422a }}}

Wealth, I said, and poverty — the one breeding luxury, idleness, and a taste for novelty, the other breeding meanness and wrongdoing, on top of that same taste for novelty.

Certainly, he said. But consider this too, Socrates: how our city will be able to wage war when it possesses no money — especially if it is forced to fight against a city that is both large and wealthy.

Clearly, I said, against one such city it will be harder, {{{ 422b }}} but against two it will be easier.

What do you mean? So he asked.

First of all, I said, if there is fighting to be done, will they not be fighting rich men while being themselves athletes of war?

Yes, that much is so, he said.

What then, I said, Adeimantus? Would it not seem to you that one boxer, prepared as well as possible for the task, could easily fight two men who are not boxers at all, but rich and fat?

Not at the same time, perhaps, he said.

Not even if he were free, I said, to give ground, and then, {{{ 422c }}} whenever the foremost man came at him, to turn and strike him, doing this again and again in the sun and the stifling heat? Would such a man not overpower even more opponents of that kind?

No doubt, he said, there would be nothing surprising in that.

But do you not think that the rich have more knowledge and experience of boxing than of warfare?

I do, he said.

So our athletes will probably fight easily against men twice or three times their own number.

I will grant you that, he said; for I think you are right. {{{ 422d }}}

What if we sent an embassy to another city and had it speak the truth, saying: "We ourselves make no use of gold or silver — we are not permitted to, though you are — so join the fighting on our side, and keep for yourselves what belongs to the others"? Do you think anyone who heard this would choose to fight against dogs, hard and lean, rather than, with the dogs as allies, fight against sheep, fat and soft?

It does not seem so to me. But, he said, if the wealth of all the other cities were gathered together {{{ 422e }}} into one city, watch that it not bring danger to the one that has no wealth.

You are fortunate, I said, to think that any city other than the kind we have been founding deserves to be called a city at all.

Why, what else should I call it? He said.

The others, I said, we must call by a grander name; for each of them is a great many cities, not a city — the sort of thing riddle-tellers say. Any one of them, {{{ 423a }}} whatever else it may be, is at least two cities at war with each other, one of the poor, the other of the rich; and within each of these there are very many more, and if you approach any of them as one, you will be entirely wrong, but if you approach it as many, and hand over the money, the power, or even the persons of one group to the other, you will always have many allies and few enemies. And as long as your city is governed with moderation, in the way we have just arranged, it will be the greatest city — not in reputation, I mean, but truly the greatest, even if it has only a thousand men to fight for it; for a single city that great you will not easily find, either among the Greeks or among the barbarians, {{{ 423b }}} though you will find many that seem to be, many times the size of that one. Or do you think otherwise?
