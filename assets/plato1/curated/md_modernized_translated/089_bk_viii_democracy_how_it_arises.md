## Democracy: How It Arises

Not at all, he said.

Democracy, then, it seems, we must consider next — in what way it comes about, and what character it has once it has come about, so that once we know the character of such a man too, we may bring him forward for judgment.

At any rate, he said, we would be going on consistently with ourselves.

Then, I said, does it not change from oligarchy into democracy in some such way as this, through insatiable desire for the good set before it — the need to become as rich as possible?

How so? {{{ 555c }}}

Since the rulers in it, I suppose, rule because they possess much, they are unwilling to restrain by law those of the young who turn unruly, so as to bar them from spending and squandering what is theirs, in order that by buying up such men's property and lending money against it they may grow still richer and more honored.

More than anything.

Then is this not already evident in a city — that to honor wealth and at the same time adequately cultivate moderation among the citizens {{{ 555d }}} is impossible, but one must neglect the one or the other?

Reasonably clear, he said.

By being negligent in oligarchies, then, and allowing licentiousness, they have sometimes forced men not ignoble by birth to become poor.

Very much so.

These men, I suppose, sit in the city fitted with stings and fully armed, some owing debts, some stripped of civic rights, some both, hating and plotting against those who have acquired their property, and against everyone else, {{{ 555e }}} longing for revolution.

That is so.

But the moneymakers, going about with their heads down and seeming not even to see these men, wound whoever among the rest gives way, injecting their money into him, and by collecting manifold interest — offspring, as it were, of the parent sum {{{ 556a }}} — they breed a great swarm of drones and beggars in the city.

How, he said, could it not be great?

And they are unwilling, I said, to quench this evil as it blazes up, either in that way — by restraining a man from turning his own property wherever he pleases — or in this way, by which such troubles are resolved under a different law.

Under what law, then?

The one that ranks second after that other law, and that compels the citizens to care for virtue. For if one were to require that most voluntary contracts be entered into at one's own risk, {{{ 556b }}} people would do business less shamelessly in the city, and fewer of the evils we just now mentioned would spring up in it.

Yes, and by far, he said.

But now, I said, because of all this, the rulers treat those they rule in the city in that way; but as for themselves and their own children — do they not make the young extravagant and averse to toil, both in body and {{{ 556c }}} in soul, too soft to hold out against pleasures and pains, and idle?

Of course.

And do they not, apart from money-making, neglect everything else themselves, and take no more care for virtue than the poor do?

No, they do not.

So disposed, then, whenever the rulers and the ruled come together with one another — whether on journeys along the roads, or in some other joint undertaking, or at festivals or on campaigns, or as shipmates or fellow soldiers, or even watching one another in the midst of dangers themselves {{{ 556d }}} — the poor are in that setting by no means despised by the rich; but often a lean, sunburnt poor man, standing in battle next to a rich man reared in the shade, carrying much flesh that is not his own, sees him full of gasping breath and helplessness — do you not think he concludes that such men are rich thanks to the cowardice of his own sort, and passes the word to another, whenever they meet in private, that these men are ours {{{ 556e }}} — for they are nothing?

I for one know well, he said, that they do that.

Then, just as a sickly body needs only a slight push from outside to fall ill, and sometimes even without any outside cause is at war with itself, does not a city disposed in the same way also, from some slight pretext, fall sick and fight against itself when one party brings in an alliance from an oligarchically governed city, or the other from a democratically governed one, and sometimes even without any outside cause falls into civil strife? {{{ 557a }}}

Very much so indeed.

Democracy, I suppose, comes about when the poor, having won, kill some of the other party, banish others, and give the rest an equal share in the constitution and in the offices — and for the most part the offices in it are assigned by lot.

Yes, he said, that is the establishment of democracy, whether it comes about by arms, or whether the other party withdraws through fear.

In what manner, then, I said, do these people live? And what sort {{{ 557b }}} of constitution is this one, in turn? For it is clear that such a man will turn out to be some sort of democratic man.

Clearly, he said.

Then, first of all, are they not free, and does not the city become full of freedom and free speech, and is there not license in it to do whatever one wants?

So it is said, he said.

And where there is license, clearly each man would arrange his own private way of life within it, whatever pleased him.

Clearly. {{{ 557c }}}

It is in this constitution above all, I suppose, that people of every sort would spring up.

How could it not be so?

It looks, I said, as though this is the most beautiful of the constitutions; just as a many-colored cloak is spangled with every flower, so this one, spangled with characters of every sort, would appear the most beautiful. And perhaps, I said, many people, just as children and women do when looking at colorful things, would judge this one too to be the most beautiful.

Very much so, he said. {{{ 557d }}}

And it is indeed, my dear fellow, I said, the right place to look for a constitution.

Why so?

Because it holds every kind of constitution on account of its license; and it seems that anyone wishing to found a city — which is exactly what we were just doing — must, on coming to a democratically governed city, pick out whichever pattern suits him, as if he had arrived at a general store of constitutions, {{{ 557e }}} and, having picked it out, settle his city on that model.

At any rate, he said, he would probably not be at a loss for models.

And the fact, I said, that there is no compulsion to rule in this city, even if you are competent to rule, nor again to be ruled, if you do not wish to be, nor to make war when the others are making war, nor to keep the peace when the others are keeping it, if you have no desire for peace, and again, even if some law bars you from ruling or judging, that there is nothing to stop you all the same {{{ 558a }}} from ruling and judging, should the fancy take you — is not such a way of life divinely pleasant, for the moment at least?

Perhaps, he said, at least for that moment.

And what of this? Is not the mild manner of some who have been sentenced charming? Or have you not yet seen how, in such a constitution, when [men] have been condemned to death or exile, they none the less remain and move about in the midst of things, [and] with no one caring or even noticing, each of them struts about like a hero?

Yes, and many of them, he said. {{{ 558b }}}

And then its forgivingness, and its utter want of pettiness, and its contempt for what we said so solemnly when we were founding our city — that unless a man had a surpassing nature he would never become good, not unless from boyhood he played amid fine things and practiced all such pursuits — how magnificently it tramples all of that underfoot, caring nothing for what pursuits a man comes from when he enters politics, but honoring him {{{ 558c }}} so long as he merely says he is well disposed toward the multitude?
