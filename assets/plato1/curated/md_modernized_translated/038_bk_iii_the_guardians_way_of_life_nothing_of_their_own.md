## The Guardians' Way of Life: Nothing of Their Own

{{{ 415d }}} Not in the least, he said — not these men themselves; but perhaps their sons, and those who come after, and the rest of mankind in later ages.

But even this, I said, would be a good thing, tending to make them care more for the city and for one another — for I think I roughly understand what you mean.

Well, this will go wherever the report may carry it; but let us arm these earthborn men and lead them forth, with their rulers going in front. And when they arrive, let them look out over the city for the best place to make camp, {{{ 415e }}} from which they could best hold in check anyone within who was unwilling to obey the laws, and ward off anyone from without, if an enemy should come against the flock like a wolf; and once they have made camp and offered the sacrifices that are due, let them prepare their beds. Or how else?

Just so, he said.

Beds, then, of the sort that would keep out the winter cold and be adequate for the summer heat?

How could they not be? For you seem to me to be speaking of dwellings, he said.

Yes, I said, dwellings fit for soldiers, but not for money-makers. {{{ 416a }}}

And how, he said, do you mean this differs from that?

I will try to tell you, I said. For surely it is the most terrible and shameful thing of all for shepherds to raise dogs as helpers for their flocks of such a kind, and in such a fashion, that through indiscipline or hunger or some other bad habit the dogs themselves should turn to doing harm to the sheep, and become like wolves instead of dogs.

Terrible, he said — how could it not be? {{{ 416b }}}

Must we not guard in every way against our auxiliaries treating the citizens in any such way — since they are the stronger — and so coming to resemble savage masters instead of friendly allies?

We must guard against it, he said.

Would they not be furnished with the greatest safeguard, if they are truly well educated?

But surely they are, he said.

And I said: this point is not worth insisting on too strongly, dear Glaucon — but what we said just now is worth insisting on, that they need {{{ 416c }}} to receive the right education, whatever that may be, if they are going to have the most important thing for being gentle both to themselves and to those under their guard.

And rightly so, he said.

Besides this education, then, anyone with sense would say that their dwellings and the rest of their property must be furnished for them in such a way as neither to stop them {{{ 416d }}} from being the best guardians they can be, nor to incite them to do wrong to the rest of the citizens.

And he will say so truly.

Consider, then, I said, whether they must live and dwell in some such way as this, if they are going to be guardians of that kind: first, that no one should possess any private property at all, unless it is altogether necessary; next, that no one should have any dwelling or storeroom of the sort that not everyone who wishes may enter; that as for provisions — whatever moderate and {{{ 416e }}} brave men who are athletes of war require — they should agree on an amount and receive it from the other citizens as wages for their guardianship, enough that there is neither anything left over at the end of the year nor anything lacking; that they should go to common meals and live together as if they were encamped; and that as for gold and silver, they should be told that they always have the divine kind in their souls as a gift from the gods, and need nothing more of the human kind, and that it is not holy to defile the possession of that kind by mixing it with the possession of mortal gold, since many unholy things have come about because of the {{{ 417a }}} currency ordinary people use, while theirs remains undefiled; but that they alone among the people of the city are not permitted to handle or touch gold and silver, nor to come under the same roof with it, nor to wear it as an ornament, nor to drink from silver or gold. And living in this way they would be safe themselves and would keep the city safe; but whenever they acquire private land and houses and money of their own, they will become householders and farmers instead of guardians, {{{ 417b }}} and hostile masters instead of allies of the other citizens, and hating and being hated, plotting and being plotted against, they will pass their whole life fearing the enemies within far more, and more constantly, than those outside, and racing at that point already very close to their own ruin and that of the rest of the city as well. For all these reasons, then, I said, shall we say that the guardians must be provided for in this way as regards housing and everything else, and shall we lay this down as law, or not?

By all means, said Glaucon.
