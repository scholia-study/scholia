## Adeimantus: What Fathers and Poets Praise

{{{ 362d }}} When Glaucon had said this, I for my part had it in mind to say something in reply, but his brother Adeimantus said, You surely do not think, Socrates, that enough has been said about the argument?

Why, what else is there? I said.

The very point that most needed making has not been made, he replied.

Well then, I said, as the saying goes, let brother stand by brother — so you too, if he is leaving anything out, come to his aid. And yet what he has said is enough to throw me to the ground and leave me unable to come to justice's aid. {{{ 362e }}}

And he said, You are talking nonsense — but listen to this as well. For we must also go through the opposite arguments to the ones he gave — the ones that praise justice and censure injustice — so that what I take Glaucon to want may become clearer. Fathers, too, tell their sons, and so does everyone {{{ 363a }}} who has someone in his care, that one must be just — not praising justice itself but the good reputation that comes from it, so that the person who is thought to be just may gain from that reputation offices and marriages and everything else Glaucon just went through, all coming from being well-regarded for being just. But these people carry the business of reputations further still. For by bringing in the good reputation that comes from the gods, they have no end of good things to say — the things they claim the gods give to the pious; just as the noble Hesiod and Homer say — the one that the gods make oaks {{{ 363b }}} bear acorns at their tops for the just, and bees in their middles, and that the wool-bearing sheep, he says, are weighed down with fleece, and much else besides, of the same kind. The other poet says much the same — for he speaks of someone as
+ … or of a faultless king, one who fears the gods
+ and upholds just judgments, so the dark earth bears
{{{ 363c }}}
+ wheat and barley, and the trees hang heavy with fruit,
+ and the flocks bring forth their young unfailingly, and the sea yields fish.
Musaeus and his son grant the just, from the gods, goods still headier than these — for leading them down to Hades in their account, and laying them on couches, and setting up a banquet of the pious, crowned with garlands, they have them spend all the rest of time {{{ 363d }}} already drunk, taking everlasting drunkenness to be virtue's finest reward. Others stretch the rewards from the gods out even longer than these — for they say that children's children, and a whole line descending after, are left behind to the man who is pious and true to his oaths. With this and other such things they praise justice — as for the unholy and unjust, on the other hand, they bury them in a kind of mud in Hades and force them to carry water in a sieve, and while they are {{{ 363e }}} still living they bring them into ill repute as well — the very punishments Glaucon recounted for the just man who is thought unjust — these are what they say about the unjust, and they have nothing else to add. So much, then, for the praise and the blame given to each.

Besides this, Socrates, consider another kind of talk about justice and injustice, spoken both privately {{{ 364a }}} and by the poets. For with one voice they all chant that moderation and justice are fine, yet hard and toilsome, while licentiousness and injustice are pleasant and easy to get, shameful only by opinion and by law — they say that unjust acts pay better than just ones, for the most part, and they are quite ready to call the wicked happy and to honor them, both in public and in private, if they are rich or hold some other kind of power, while dishonoring and looking down on anyone who happens to be weak {{{ 364b }}} or poor, even while admitting that such people are better than the others. Of all these claims, the ones about the gods and virtue are the most astonishing — that the gods themselves assign misfortune and a bad life to many good people, and the opposite lot to their opposites. Begging priests and seers go to the doors of the rich and persuade them that they possess a power, procured from the gods through sacrifices and incantations, to heal with pleasures and festivities any wrongdoing {{{ 364c }}} committed by that person or by his ancestors, and that if someone wishes to harm an enemy, for a small fee he will injure the just and the unjust alike, by certain spells and bindings, since they persuade the gods, as they say, to serve them. For all these claims they bring in poets as witnesses, some on the subject of vice, granting that it comes easily, as:
+ Wickedness can be taken up even in droves
{{{ 364d }}}
+ with ease — smooth is the road, and it lies very near;
+ but before virtue the gods have set the sweat of toil
and some road that is long and rough and steep; while others call Homer to witness that the gods can be led astray by men, since he too said—
+ the gods themselves can be entreated as well,
+ and with sacrifices and gentle vows,
{{{ 364e }}}
+ with libation and with savor, men turn them aside
+ by entreaty, whenever someone oversteps and errs.
They also produce a whole din of books by Musaeus and Orpheus, offspring of the Moon and the Muses, as they say, and perform rites according to them, persuading not only private people but whole cities that there are indeed releases and purifications from wrongdoing, through sacrifices and {{{ 365a }}} pleasurable games, both for those still alive and for those who have died — rites they call initiations, which free us from the evils of the other world, while terrible things await those who have not sacrificed.

All this, he said, dear Socrates — all these many things said about virtue and vice, and the honor men and gods hold them in — what do we suppose it does to the souls of the young who hear it, those of good natural parts, able to light on everything that is said, as a bird might, and reckon up from it what sort of person {{{ 365b }}} one should be, and what path to take, to get through life in the best way possible? For, reasoning from what is likely, he might well say to himself, in Pindar's words, that very question — shall I climb the higher wall by justice, or by crooked deceit, and fence myself round and live my life that way? For what people say is that if I am just but do not also seem so, there is no benefit in it for me, only plain hardship and loss, while for the unjust man who has secured himself a reputation for justice, a marvelous life is said to be in store. {{{ 365c }}} Well then, since seeming, as the wise make clear to me, overpowers even truth itself and is the master of happiness, I must turn wholly to that — I must draw round myself, as a front porch and a facade, a mere outline-sketch of virtue, and trail behind me the fox of that most cunning Archilochus, wily and full of shifts. But, someone will say, it is not easy always to escape notice while being bad. Well, we will reply, {{{ 365d }}} nothing that is great is easy either — but still, if we mean to be happy, this is the path we must take, following where the tracks of the argument lead. For the sake of escaping notice, we will form secret societies and clubs, and there are teachers of persuasion who impart the wisdom of public speaking and of the courts, with which we will win some things by persuasion and take others by force, so as to get the advantage and not pay the penalty. But then it is impossible either to escape the gods' notice or to force them. Well then, if they do not exist, or if they have no concern at all for human affairs, why should {{{ 365e }}} we concern ourselves with escaping notice either? But if they do exist and do care, we know or have heard of them from nowhere else but the laws and the poets who traced out their lineages, and these very same sources say that the gods are of a kind to be led astray, won over by sacrifices, gentle vows, and votive offerings — and we must trust these sources on both counts, or on neither. If then we must trust them, we should do wrong and sacrifice {{{ 366a }}} out of the proceeds of our wrongdoing. For if we are just, we will only go unpunished by the gods, while pushing away the profits that come from injustice; but if we are unjust, we will make our profit, and by entreating them as we overstep and err, we will win them over and come away unpunished. Ah, but in Hades we will pay the penalty for whatever wrongs we commit here, either we ourselves or our children's children. But, my friend, our reasoner will say, the initiation rites in turn have great power, and so do the gods who release us, as the {{{ 366b }}} greatest cities say, and the children of the gods who became poets and prophets of the gods, and who reveal that these things are so.

On what reasoning, then, would we still choose justice over the greatest injustice, when, if we acquire that injustice along with a counterfeit air of decency, we will fare according to our wishes both with gods and with men, in life and in death, as the argument of the many and of the eminent alike puts it? So from everything that has been said, {{{ 366c }}} what device is left, Socrates, for anyone with some power of soul, or of wealth, or of body, or of birth, to be willing to honor justice, rather than laugh when he hears it praised? For indeed, if anyone can show that what we have said is false, and has come to know well enough that justice is best, he surely has a great deal of forgiveness for the unjust, and feels no anger at them, but knows that unless someone, by a divine nature, is disgusted by doing wrong, or has gained knowledge {{{ 366d }}} and so holds back from it, no one else is just of his own will — rather, people condemn wrongdoing out of cowardice, or old age, or some other weakness, simply because they are unable to do it. And that this is so is plain — for the first of these people to come into power is the first to do wrong, to whatever extent he is able. And the cause of all this is nothing other than the very thing this whole argument — both his and mine — started from, in speaking to you, Socrates: that, my remarkable friend, of all {{{ 366e }}} of you who claim to be praisers of justice, starting from the heroes of old, all whose words have come down to us, down to the people of today, no one has ever blamed injustice or praised justice for anything other than the reputations, honors, and gifts that come from them, while what each of the two itself does, by its own power, dwelling in the soul of the one who has it and escaping the notice of gods and men alike, no one has ever adequately worked through, in poetry or in private discourse, to show that the one is the greatest of all the evils a soul holds within itself, and justice the greatest good. {{{ 367a }}} For if this had been said by all of you from the beginning, and you had persuaded us from childhood on, we would not now be guarding one another against wrongdoing, but each of us would be his own best guard, afraid that in doing wrong he would be living with the greatest evil as his housemate.

This, Socrates, and perhaps even more than this, is what Thrasymachus or someone else might say on behalf of justice and injustice, turning their respective powers inside out in a crude way, or so it seems to me at least. But I — for I have no need to {{{ 367b }}} hide anything from you — speak as forcefully as I can, because I am eager to hear the opposite from you. So do not merely show us in your argument that justice is stronger than injustice, but show what each of them does, in its own right, to the one who has it, that makes the one bad and the other good — and take away their reputations, just as Glaucon urged. For if you do not take away the true reputations from both sides, and instead add the false ones, we will say that you are praising, not justice, but the seeming of it, and censuring, not being {{{ 367c }}} unjust, but seeming so, and that you are urging us to escape notice while being unjust, and agreeing with Thrasymachus that justice is really someone else's good, the advantage of the stronger, and that injustice is advantageous and profitable to oneself, but disadvantageous to the weaker. Since, then, you agreed that justice is among the greatest goods — the sort worth acquiring both for the sake of what comes from them and, far more, for their own sake, such as seeing, {{{ 367d }}} hearing, being wise, and indeed being healthy, and whatever other goods are fruitful by their own nature and not merely by reputation — praise this very thing in justice, how it, by itself, benefits the one who has it, while injustice harms him, and leave rewards and reputations for others to praise, since I would accept it from others when they praise justice and blame injustice in that way, extolling and abusing the reputations and rewards attached to them, but not from you, unless you yourself bid me to, because you have spent your whole life {{{ 367e }}} examining nothing else but this. So do not merely show us in your argument that justice is stronger than injustice, but also what each of them does, in its own right, to the one who has it — whether or not it escapes the notice of gods and men — that makes the one good and the other bad.
