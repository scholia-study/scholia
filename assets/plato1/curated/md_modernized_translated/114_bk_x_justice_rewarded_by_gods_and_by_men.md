## Justice Rewarded, by Gods and by Men

Well then, I said, we settled the other matters in our argument, {{{ 612b }}} and did not praise justice for its wages or its repute, as you said Hesiod and Homer did, but found justice itself, for the soul itself, best, and that one must do just things whether one has the ring of Gyges or not, and along with such a ring the cap of Hades too?

Most true, he said.

Is it not now, then, I said, Glaucon, free of reproach to give back, besides those things, the wages also to justice and to {{{ 612c }}} the rest of virtue — as many and as great as it provides to the soul from men and from gods, both while the man still lives and after he has died?

Certainly, he said.

Will you, then, give me back what you borrowed in the argument?

What in particular?

I granted to you that the just man should seem unjust and the unjust man just; for you asked that, even if it were not possible for this to escape the notice of both gods and men, it must nevertheless be granted for the sake of the argument, so that justice itself might be judged {{{ 612d }}} against injustice itself. Or do you not remember?

I would indeed be committing an injustice, he said, if I did not.

Since, then, I said, they have been judged, I now claim back, on behalf of justice, the reputation it actually has from both gods and men, and ask that we agree it is thought of in this way, so that it too may carry off the prizes of victory, gaining from its reputation what it gives to those who possess it, since it has been shown to give the goods that come from actually being just, and not to deceive those who truly take hold of it. {{{ 612e }}}

What you ask is just, he said.

Then, I said, first you will grant me this — that the gods, at least, are not unaware what sort each of the two is?

We will grant it, he said.

And if they do not escape notice, the one would be dear to the gods, the other hateful to them, just as we agreed also at the beginning.

That is so.

And shall we not agree that for the man dear to the gods, whatever comes to him {{{ 613a }}} from the gods, all comes about in the best way possible, unless some necessary evil awaited him from an earlier fault?

Certainly.

This, then, is how one must think about the just man — whether he falls into poverty or into sicknesses or into any other of the things thought bad — that for him these things will end in some good, whether he is living or even after he has died. For the man who is willing to make it his earnest purpose to become just and, by practicing {{{ 613b }}} virtue, to be like a god so far as a man can, is surely never neglected by the gods.

It stands to reason, he said, that such a man should not be neglected by one like him.

Must we not, then, think the opposite of this concerning the unjust man?

Very much so.

Such, then, would be the prizes of victory from the gods for the just man.

So it seems to me, at any rate, he said.

But what, I said, of the rewards from men? Is it not so, if we must state the truth of the matter? Do not the clever and unjust act just like those runners who run well on the way out but not on the way back? At first they spring off sharply, but in the end {{{ 613c }}} they become ridiculous, their ears drooping on their shoulders, and they run off without a crown, while those who are true runners, once they reach the finish, take the prizes and are crowned. Is this not, for the most part, what happens with the just as well? Toward the end of each action and each association and of life itself, are they not held in good repute and do they not carry off the prizes from men?

Very much so.

Will you bear with me, then, if I say about these men the very things you yourself said {{{ 613d }}} about the unjust? For I will say that the just, once they have grown old, hold office in their own city if they wish to, marry from wherever they wish, and give their daughters in marriage to whomever they want; and everything that you said about the unjust, I now say about these men. And again, about the unjust — that most of them, even when they go undetected while young, are caught at the end of the race and become ridiculous, and, growing old, are wretchedly abused by strangers {{{ 613e }}} and citizens alike, scourged, and suffering what you yourself called crude, and you spoke truly — and then they will be racked and burned — take it that you have heard from me too all those things they suffer. But see whether you can bear what I am saying.

Quite so, he said; for what you say is just.

Such, then, I said, would be the prizes and wages and gifts that come to the just man while he lives, from both gods {{{ 614a }}} and men, in addition to those goods which justice itself provided.

Very much so, he said — fine and secure things.

These things, then, I said, are nothing either in number or in magnitude compared to those that await each of the two after he has died; and we must hear about them, so that each may fully receive what our argument owes him to hear.
