## The Second Pattern: God Does Not Change or Deceive

{{{ 380d }}} But what, then, is this second pattern? Do you think the god is a sorcerer — one who, as if by design, might appear now in one shape, now in another, at one moment truly becoming different [and] changing his own form into many shapes, at another merely deceiving us and making us think such things about him — or is he simple, and of all things least likely to step outside his own form?

I cannot say, he said, at least not right now.

And what of this? Isn't it necessary, if something departs at all from its own {{{ 380e }}} form, that it be changed either by itself or by another?

It is necessary.

Now then, isn't it true that things in the best condition are least altered and moved by another? Take a body affected by food, drink, and exertion, or any plant affected by heat, wind, and such things — isn't the healthiest and strongest {{{ 381a }}} least altered?

How could it not be?

And the soul — wouldn't the most courageous and most sensible soul be least disturbed and altered by anything from outside?

Yes.

And surely, by the same reasoning, all composite things too — furniture, buildings, and clothing — when well made and in good condition, are least altered by time and other such effects.

This is so. {{{ 381b }}}

Whatever is in good condition, whether by nature, by craft, or by both, admits the least change from another.

So it seems.

But surely the god, and everything belonging to the god, is in every way in the best condition.

How could it not be?

In that respect, then, the god would least of all take on many shapes.

Least of all, indeed.

But might he change and alter himself?

Clearly, he said, if he is altered at all.

Does he, then, change himself into something better and more beautiful, or into something worse and uglier than himself? {{{ 381c }}}

It must be for the worse, he said, if he is altered at all — for surely we won't say that the god is lacking in beauty or virtue.

That is exactly right, I said. And if that's how things stand, does it seem to you, Adeimantus, that anyone — god or man — would willingly make himself worse in any way at all?

Impossible, he said.

It is impossible, then, I said, for a god too to be willing to alter himself; rather, as it seems, being most beautiful and best so far as possible, each of them remains forever, simply, in his own form.

That seems to me an absolute necessity, he said. {{{ 381d }}}

Then let none of the poets, I said, my good friend, tell us that—
+ … gods, like strangers from foreign lands,
+ taking on every shape, go about the cities;
nor let anyone speak falsely of Proteus and Thetis either, nor bring Hera on, in tragedies or in any other poems, transformed into a priestess out collecting alms—
+ for the life-giving children of Inachus, the Argive river;
{{{ 381e }}} nor let them tell us many other such falsehoods. Nor, in turn, let mothers, taken in by these stories, frighten their children with them — telling the tales badly, as though there really are gods who go about at night disguised as every sort of stranger — so that they may not at once blaspheme against the gods and make their children more cowardly.

No indeed, he said.

But is it, I said, that the gods themselves are the sort not to change, yet make us believe they appear as every sort of thing, deceiving and bewitching us?

Perhaps, he said. {{{ 382a }}}

But what of this? I said — would a god be willing to lie, presenting a false appearance in word or in deed?

I don't know, he said.

Don't you know, I said, that the true lie — if one may put it that way — is hated by all gods and men alike?

What, he said, do you mean?

Like this, I said: no one willingly consents to be deceived in the most sovereign part of himself, about the most important things, but fears more than anything to have that falsehood lodged there.

I still don't understand, even now, he said. {{{ 382b }}}

You think I mean something solemn, I said — but what I mean is this: that of all things, everyone would least willingly accept being lied to and deceived and made ignorant, in the soul, about the things that are, and having and holding falsehood there, and it is there that they hate it most.

Very much so, he said.

And surely what I was speaking of just now would most correctly be called the true lie — the ignorance in the soul of the one who has been deceived — since the lie in words is a kind of imitation of that condition in the soul, an image of it that comes into being afterward, and not {{{ 382c }}} an entirely unmixed falsehood. Or is it not so?

Certainly.

So the lie that really is a lie is hated not only by gods but by men as well.

So it seems to me.

But what of the [lie] in words? When and for whom is it useful, such that it doesn't deserve to be hated? Isn't it useful, like a medicine, against enemies, and among those called friends, whenever, through madness or some folly, they attempt to do something bad, for the sake of turning them away from it? And in the myth-telling we were just now {{{ 382d }}} discussing, don't we make it useful in the same way, because we don't know how the truth stands about ancient matters, by making the falsehood resemble the truth as closely as possible?

Yes indeed, he said, that's how it is.

So on which of these grounds, then, is falsehood useful to the god? Would he lie, through not knowing about ancient things, by fashioning a likeness?

That would be ridiculous, he said.

So there is no lying poet in a god.

I don't think so.

But would he lie out of fear of his enemies? {{{ 382e }}}

Far from it.

But because of the folly or madness of his kin?

But none of the foolish and the mad is dear to the gods, he said.

So there is no reason for which a god would lie.

There is not.

So everything divine and everything godlike is altogether free of falsehood.

Absolutely, he said.

So the god is entirely simple and truthful, in deed as in word, and he neither changes himself nor deceives others, whether by apparitions, by words, or by the sending of signs, either in waking life or in dreams. {{{ 383a }}}

That's how it seems to me too, he said, now that you put it this way.

So you agree, I said, that this is the second pattern by which one must speak and compose about the gods — that they are neither sorcerers who change themselves, nor do they lead us astray with falsehoods, in word or in deed?

I agree.

So although we praise Homer for much, we will not praise this — the sending of the dream to Agamemnon by Zeus — nor this of Aeschylus, when Thetis says that Apollo, singing at her own {{{ 383b }}} wedding, foretold her fortune in children:
+ lives free of sickness, and long in their years,
+ and speaking of my fortunes as altogether dear to the gods,
+ he raised a paean of good omen, cheering my heart.
+ And I hoped that the divine mouth of Phoebus was free of falsehood,
+ brimming with the craft of prophecy;
+ but he — the very one singing, the very one present at the feast,
+ the very one who spoke these words — is himself the one who killed
+ my son —
{{{ 383c }}} whenever anyone says such things about the gods, we will be angry and refuse them a chorus, and we will not allow the teachers to make use of them in educating the young, if our guardians are to become god-fearing and godlike, so far as that is possible for a human being.

I agree entirely, he said, with these patterns, and I would treat them as laws.
