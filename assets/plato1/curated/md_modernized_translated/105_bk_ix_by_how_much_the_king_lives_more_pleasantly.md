## By How Much the King Lives More Pleasantly

Very much so.

And the kingly and orderly desires least?

Yes.

Then I think the tyrant will stand furthest from true pleasure, the pleasure proper to him, and the king least of all.

Necessarily.

And so the tyrant, I said, will live most unpleasantly, and the king most pleasantly.

It certainly must.

Do you know, then, I said, by how much more unpleasantly a tyrant lives than a king?

Only if you tell me, he said.

There being three kinds of pleasure, as it seems — one genuine and two {{{ 587c }}} spurious — the tyrant, having overleapt even the spurious ones into the region beyond, in flight from law and reason, lives with certain slavish pleasures that serve as his bodyguard; and by how much he falls short it is not at all easy to say, except perhaps in this way.

How? He said.

The tyrant was, I suppose, third in remove from the oligarchic man; for the democratic man was between them.

Yes.

Then would he not also dwell with a pleasure that is a third-removed image of the truth, counting from him, if what was said before is true?

That is so.

And the oligarchic man, in turn, is third from the kingly man, if we {{{ 587d }}} count the aristocratic and the kingly as one.

Yes, for he is third.

Then, I said, the tyrant is removed from true pleasure by a number: the triple of the triple.

So it appears.

Then, as it seems, I said, the image of tyrannical pleasure would be a plane figure, according to the number of its length.

Quite so.

But by the square and the third increase, it becomes clear how great a distance he comes to stand removed.

Clear, he said, to the rational part, at least.

Then if someone, turning it around, should say by how much the king stands removed {{{ 587e }}} from the tyrant in the truth of pleasure, he will find, once the multiplication is carried through, that he lives seven hundred and twenty-nine times more pleasantly, and the tyrant more distressingly by this same distance.

You have poured down on us, he said, an overwhelming calculation of the difference {{{ 588a }}} between the two men, the just and the unjust, with respect to pleasure and pain.

And indeed, I said, a true number too, and a fitting one for lives, if days and nights and months and years do indeed belong to them.

Why, of course they do, he said.

Then if the good and just man surpasses the bad and unjust man by so much in pleasure, by how immeasurably greater a margin will he surpass him in grace of life, and in beauty, and in virtue?

By an immeasurable margin indeed, by Zeus, he said.
