## Moderation as Agreement About Who Should Rule

Certainly.

How then might we find justice, so that we need trouble ourselves no further about moderation?

As for me, he said, I neither know, nor would I wish justice to appear first, if that means we are to look no further into moderation; but if you would do me a favor, look into this before that. {{{ 430e }}}

Well then, I said, I do wish to, if I am doing nothing unjust.

Look, then, he said.

It must be looked into, I said; and as far as one can see from here, it resembles a kind of concord and harmony more than the things before it.

How so?

Moderation, I said, is a kind of order, and a mastery of certain pleasures and desires, as people say when they declare someone master of himself — I do not know quite how — and other such things are spoken of as traces of it. Is that not so?

Most certainly, he said.

Now is not being master of oneself a ridiculous thing to say? For the one who is master of himself would presumably also be weaker than himself, and the one who is weaker would be master; {{{ 431a }}} for the same person is meant by all these terms.

Why not?

But, I said, this phrase seems to me to mean that within the man himself, in his soul, there is a better part and a worse; and whenever the better is by nature in control of the worse, this is called being master of himself — it is certainly meant as praise — but whenever, through bad upbringing or bad company, the better part, being the smaller, {{{ 431b }}} is overpowered by the greater mass of the worse, this condition is blamed as a reproach, and the one so disposed is called weaker than himself and unrestrained.

It does seem so, he said.

Look, then, I said, toward our new city, and you will find the one of these two present in it; for you will rightly say it is master of itself, if indeed that in which the better rules over the worse must be called moderate and master of itself.

I am looking, he said, and what you say is true.

And moreover, one would find the many and varied desires and {{{ 431c }}} pleasures and pains chiefly in children and women and household slaves, and, among those called free, in the great inferior mass of them.

Certainly.

Whereas the simple and measured desires, which are guided by reasoning together with intelligence and correct opinion, you will meet with only in a few — those best by nature and best raised.

True, he said.

Do you not see this present in your city as well, and the desires of the many and {{{ 431d }}} base there mastered by the desires and the practical wisdom found among the fewer and more decent?

I do, he said.

If then any city must be called master of its pleasures and desires and of itself, this one must be so addressed.

Absolutely, he said.

Is it not, then, also moderate in view of all this?

Very much so, he said.

Moreover, if in any other city the same opinion is held by the {{{ 431e }}} rulers and the ruled about who ought to rule, this too would be present in that city. Or do you not think so?

Very much so indeed, he said.

In which of the two classes of citizens, then, will you say moderation resides, when they are so disposed? In the rulers, or in the ruled?

In both, I suppose, he said.

Do you see, then, I said, that we were reasonably divining just now that moderation resembles a kind of harmony?

In what way?

Because, unlike courage and wisdom, each of which, residing in one {{{ 432a }}} part, made the city wise or courageous respectively, this virtue does not work that way, but is literally stretched through the whole, running through all the parts and making the weakest and the strongest and those in between all sing the same tune — whether by practical wisdom, if you like, or by strength, or by numbers, or by wealth, or by anything else of that kind — so that we would most correctly say that this concord is moderation: an agreement, in accordance with nature, between the worse and the better as to which of the two should rule, both in the city and in each individual.
