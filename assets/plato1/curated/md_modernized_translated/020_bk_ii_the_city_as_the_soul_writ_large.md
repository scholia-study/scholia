## The City as the Soul Writ Large

And when I heard this, I had always admired the character of Glaucon and Adeimantus, but just then {{{ 368a }}} I was quite delighted and said that Glaucon's admirer had not composed the opening of his elegiac verses about you badly, sons of that famous man, when, after your fine showing in the fight at Megara, he wrote—
+ Sons of Ariston, godlike stock of a famous man—
This seems to me well said, my friends; for something quite divine must have happened to you, if, with such powers of speech on its behalf, you are still not persuaded that injustice is better than justice. You seem to me {{{ 368b }}} not really convinced — I judge this from the rest of your character, since going by the arguments alone I would distrust you — but my trust in you keeps growing, and so does my bewilderment about what to do with you. For I have no idea how to give help — I think myself incapable of it, and my proof is that you did not accept what I said against Thrasymachus, when I thought I was showing that justice is better than injustice — yet neither do I know how to refuse to help; for I am afraid it would not even be pious, when present while justice is being maligned, to {{{ 368c }}} give up and not defend her while I am still breathing and able to speak. So it is best that I help her as I am able.

So Glaucon and the others begged me in every way to help and not to drop the argument, but to search out thoroughly what each of the two really is, and where the truth lies about their benefit. So I said what occurred to me: that the inquiry we were undertaking was no trivial matter but called for sharp eyesight, or so it {{{ 368d }}} seemed to me. Since we are not clever, then, I think, I said, we should make our search for it the way we would if someone had ordered men whose sight was not sharp to read small letters from a distance, and then one of them noticed that the same letters stood written somewhere else as well, larger and on a larger surface — it would seem a godsend, I think, to read those first and only then examine the smaller ones, to see whether they really are the same.

Certainly, said Adeimantus; but what do you see of that sort, {{{ 368e }}} Socrates, in our inquiry about the just?

I will tell you, I said. We say that justice belongs to a single man, and also, I suppose, to a whole city?

Certainly, he said.

Then is a city not bigger than a single man?

Bigger, he said.

Perhaps, then, there would be more justice in the bigger thing, and it would be easier to make out. So if you are willing, let us first look for what kind of thing it is in {{{ 369a }}} cities, and then examine it in the same way in each single man, looking for the resemblance of the greater in the form of the lesser.

Well, that seems to me a good way to put it, he said.

Then, I said, if in our discussion we were to watch a city coming into being, would we also see its justice and its injustice coming into being?

Perhaps, he said.

Then, once it has come into being, is there hope of seeing what we are looking for more easily?
