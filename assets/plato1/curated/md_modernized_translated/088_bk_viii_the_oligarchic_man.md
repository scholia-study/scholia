## The Oligarchic Man

{{{ 553a }}} Let this constitution too, then, be taken as completed, I said — the one they call oligarchy, which has its rulers chosen by property assessment; and let us next examine the man who resembles it, both how he comes to be and what sort of man he is once he has come to be.

Certainly, he said.

Is it not chiefly in this way, then, that he changes from that timocratic man into an oligarchic one?

How so?

It is when a son is born to him, and at first he emulates his father and follows his footsteps, but then sees him suddenly {{{ 553b }}} strike against the city as against a hidden reef, and spill out both his property and himself, after serving as general or holding some other great office, and then falling into court, [harmed] by false accusers, and being put to death or exiled or stripped of his rights, losing his whole estate.

That's likely, he said.

Having seen this, my friend, and suffered it, and lost what he had, in fear, I think, he at once thrusts headlong down from the throne {{{ 553c }}} in his own soul both the love of honor and that spirited part, and humbled by poverty he turns to money-making, and little by little, living meanly and scraping and toiling, he gathers up wealth. Don't you think that such a man then enthrones on that throne the appetitive part, the money-loving part, and makes it the great king within himself, girding it about with tiara and collar and scimitar?

I do, he said. {{{ 553d }}}

And the rational part, I think, and the spirited part he seats on the ground, this side and that, beneath it, and enslaves them: the one he allows to reckon and consider nothing else but how a little money may become more, and the other, in turn, to admire and honor nothing but wealth and the wealthy, and to be ambitious for nothing but the acquisition of money and whatever else contributes to that.

There is no other change, he said, so swift and so forceful, from an honor-loving young man into a money-loving one. {{{ 553e }}}

Is this man, then, I said, oligarchic?

At least his transformation is from a man resembling the constitution out of which oligarchy arose.

Let us examine, then, whether he would be like it. {{{ 554a }}}

Let us do so.

Then first, would he not be like it in valuing money above everything?

How could he not?

And surely too in being frugal and industrious, satisfying only his necessary desires, refusing to spend on anything else, and enslaving his other desires as futile.

Certainly.

A squalid sort of man, I said, who makes a surplus out of everything, a hoarder — the very sort the crowd praises {{{ 554b }}} — or would this not be the man who resembles such a constitution?

So it seems to me, at least, he said — for money, at any rate, is held in the highest honor both by the city and by such a man.

That is because, I suppose, I said, such a man has paid no attention to education.

I don't think so, he said — for he would not have set up a blind man as leader of the chorus and ⟨honored⟩ him most of all.

Good, I said. But consider this: shall we not say that drone-like desires spring up in him on account of his lack of education — {{{ 554c }}} some of them beggarly, some villainous — held down by force by his carefulness in other things?

Very much so, he said.

Do you know, then, I said, where you should look to observe their villainy?

Where? He asked.

To their guardianships over orphans, and to any similar situation that comes their way, giving them ample opportunity to act unjustly.

True.

So isn't it clear from this that in his other dealings, where he enjoys a good reputation for seeming just, {{{ 554d }}} he restrains, by force, with some decent part of himself, other bad desires that are present in him — not persuading them that wrongdoing is not better, nor taming them by argument, but by necessity and fear, trembling for the rest of his property?

Yes, quite so, he said.

And by Zeus, my friend, I said, you will find in most of them, whenever they need to spend what belongs to others, desires akin to the drone's present within them.

Very much so indeed, he said.

Such a man, then, would not be free of faction within himself, nor single but somehow double, though for the most part {{{ 554e }}} he would have his better desires holding down his worse ones.

That is so.

For this reason, I think, such a man would be more decent-seeming than many others; yet true virtue, belonging to a soul that is unified and in harmony with itself, would escape him by a long way.

So it seems to me.

And what's more, as a private competitor in the city, the thrifty man {{{ 555a }}} is a poor competitor for a victory or any other of the fine things men are ambitious for, unwilling to spend money for the sake of reputation and such contests, afraid to rouse his spendthrift desires and call on them as allies in the struggle for victory; fighting oligarchically with just a few of his own resources, he is mostly defeated, and grows rich.

Very much so, he said.

Do we still doubt, then, I said, that the thrifty, money-making man is to be ranked, by resemblance, {{{ 555b }}} with the oligarchically governed city?
