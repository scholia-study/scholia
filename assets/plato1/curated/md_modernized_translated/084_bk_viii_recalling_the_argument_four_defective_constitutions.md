## Recalling the Argument: Four Defective Constitutions

{{{ 543a }}} Well then — these things have been agreed, Glaucon: that the city which is to be governed at its very best will have the women in common, the children in common, and all their education in common, and likewise the pursuits common in both war and peace, and that its kings will be those who have proved best in philosophy and in warfare.

It has been agreed, he said. {{{ 543b }}}

And moreover we agreed to this too — that when the rulers are established, they will lead the soldiers and settle them in dwellings such as we described earlier, dwellings with nothing private belonging to anyone, but common to all; and besides such dwellings, we also agreed, if you remember, what sort of possessions they would have.

I do remember, he said, that we thought none of them should possess anything that the others now possess, but rather, as athletes of war {{{ 543c }}} and guardians, receiving from the others their keep for the year as wages for their guarding, they must care for themselves and for the rest of the city.

You are right, I said. But come, since we have finished this, let us recall from where we turned aside to arrive here, so that we may go back the same way.

Not difficult, he said. For much as now, you were speaking as one who had finished the account of the city, saying that you set down as good the sort of city you had then described, {{{ 543d }}} and the man resembling it, and this despite having, as it seems, still finer {{{ 544a }}} things yet to say of city and man. But at any rate you went on to say that the other constitutions were mistaken, if this one is right. And of the remaining constitutions, you said, as I recall, there are four kinds, about which it would be worth both giving an account and seeing their faults, and the men who resemble them in turn, so that, having seen them all and agreed on the best and worst man, we might examine whether the best is happiest and the worst most wretched, or whether it stands otherwise — and when I asked which {{{ 544b }}} four constitutions you meant, at that point Polemarchus and Adeimantus broke in, and so you, taking up the argument, have arrived here.

You have remembered it exactly right, I said.

Well then, offer the same hold again, like a wrestler, and when I ask the same question, try to say what you were then about to say.

If I can, I said.

And indeed, he said, I myself am eager to hear which four constitutions you meant. {{{ 544c }}}

You will hear it without difficulty, I said. For those I mean, the ones that actually have names, are: the one praised by the many, this Cretan-and-Laconian one; and second, and praised second, the one called oligarchy, a constitution full of many evils; and the one differing from this and coming next in order, democracy; and then, of course, that noble thing tyranny, differing from all of these, the fourth and last disease of a city. Or do you have some other form of constitution, one that lies in {{{ 544d }}} some distinct kind? For dynasties, and purchased kingships, and certain other constitutions of that sort, lie somewhere between these, and one could find them no fewer among barbarians than among Greeks.

Many odd ones do get talked of, at any rate, he said.

Do you know, then, I said, that there must also be as many kinds of human character as there are of constitutions? Or do you think that constitutions come from some oak or rock, rather than {{{ 544e }}} from the characters found in the cities, which, tipping the scale as it were, draw everything else along with them?

Not I, certainly, he said — from nowhere else but there.

Then if the constitutions of cities are five, the soul's arrangements in private individuals would also be five.

What else could it be?

The man resembling aristocracy, then, we have already discussed — the one we rightly call good and just. {{{ 545a }}}

We have.

Must we, then, after this, go through the worse men — the contentious and honor-loving man corresponding to the Laconian constitution, and again the oligarchic, the democratic, and the tyrannical — so that, having seen the most unjust and set him against the most just, our inquiry may be complete as to how unmixed justice stands against unmixed injustice with respect to the happiness or wretchedness of the one who has it, so that we may either, persuaded by Thrasymachus, {{{ 545b }}} pursue injustice, or, by the argument now coming to light, pursue justice?

By all means, he said, that is what we must do.

Shall we, then, just as we began by examining the characters in the constitutions before examining them in private individuals, since that is clearer, so now too first examine the honor-loving constitution — for I have no other name commonly given to it; it must be called either timocracy or timarchy — and in relation to it examine the corresponding {{{ 545c }}} man, then oligarchy and the oligarchic man, and again, turning our gaze to democracy, observe the democratic man, and fourth, having come to a city under tyranny and seen it, and looking in turn at the tyrannical soul, try to become adequate judges of what we set out to determine?
