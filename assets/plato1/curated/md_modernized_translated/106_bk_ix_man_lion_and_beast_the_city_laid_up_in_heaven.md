## Man, Lion, and Beast: The City Laid Up in Heaven

{{{ 588b }}} Well then, I said, since we have come this far in the argument, let us take up again the first things that were said, on account of which we have come here. It was said, I believe, that acting unjustly profits the man who is completely unjust yet reputed to be just; or was that not how it was said?

Just so.

Now then, I said, let us discuss this with him, since we have agreed on the power each of them has, both doing injustice and doing just things.

How? He said.

By fashioning in speech an image of the soul, so that the one who says those things may know what sort of thing he was saying. {{{ 588c }}}

What sort? He said.

One of that sort, I said, like the natures that are told of in old myths as having come to be — that of the Chimera and Scylla and Cerberus — and a good many others too are said to have grown together, many forms into one.

So they are said to be, he said.

Mold, then, one form of a beast, many-colored and many-headed, having heads of tame animals in a circle and of wild ones too, and capable of changing and of growing all of these out of itself. {{{ 588d }}}

That is the task of a skillful modeler, he said; still, since speech is more easily molded than wax and such things, let it be molded.

Now mold one further form, that of a lion, and one of a man; and let the first be by far the largest, and the second next in size.

These are easier, he said, and they have been molded.

Now join the three of them into one, so that they somehow grow together with one another.

They have been joined, he said.

Now mold around them, on the outside, the image of one being, that of a man, so that to whoever cannot see what is within but sees only the {{{ 588e }}} outer shell, it appears to be a single living creature, a man.

It has been molded around them, he said.

Let us say, then, to the man who claims that it profits this person to act unjustly, while acting justly does not benefit him, that he is saying nothing other than that it profits him to feast the beast of every kind and make it strong, and the lion too and what surrounds the lion, but to starve the {{{ 589a }}} man and make him weak, so that he is dragged wherever either of them leads him, and to accustom neither to the other, nor make them friends, but to let them bite each other and, fighting, devour one another.

Yes indeed, he said, that is what the one who praises injustice would say.

Then would not the one who declares that just things profit say that one must act and speak in the way from which the man within the {{{ 589b }}} man will be most in command, and will care for the many-headed creature as a farmer does — nurturing and taming the tame parts, but preventing the wild ones from growing — having made the lion's nature his ally, and caring for all of them together, having made them friends with one another and with himself — will he not nurture it in this way?

Yes, that is exactly what the one who praises justice says.

In every way, then, the one who extols just things would speak the {{{ 589c }}} truth, while the one who extols unjust things would be lying. For considering it in terms of pleasure, good repute, and benefit alike, the one who praises the just tells the truth, while the one who blames it does so without any soundness or knowledge of what he is blaming.

It does not seem so to me, he said, not in the least.

Let us persuade him gently, then — for he does not err willingly — by asking: My good man, would we not say that the customary distinctions of noble and shameful have arisen on account of things such as these: the noble being what places the beastlike parts of our nature {{{ 589d }}} under the man — or rather, perhaps, under the divine — while the shameful is what enslaves the tame to the wild? He will agree — or how else?

If he is persuaded by me, he said.

Is there, then, I said, by this argument, anyone for whom it profits to take gold unjustly, if some such thing as this results — that in taking the gold he at the same time enslaves the best part of himself {{{ 589e }}} to the most depraved? Or, if by taking gold he were enslaving a son or a daughter, and to wild and evil men at that, it would not profit him even to take a very great deal for it; but if he enslaves what is most divine in himself to what is most godless and most defiled, and pities nothing, is he not then wretched, and does he not accept gold as a bribe for a far more terrible {{{ 590a }}} ruin than Eriphyle did when she took the necklace in exchange for her husband's life?

Far more indeed, said Glaucon; for I will answer you on his behalf.

Then do you not think that licentiousness, too, has long been blamed for reasons such as these — because in a person of that kind the terrible thing, that great, many-formed creature, is let loose beyond what it should be?

Clearly, he said.

And are not self-will and ill temper blamed whenever the lion-like {{{ 590b }}} and the snake-like grow and are strained out of harmony?

Certainly.

And are not luxury and softness blamed for the slackening and relaxing of this same part, when it produces cowardice in it?

Of course.

And are not flattery and meanness blamed whenever someone subjects this same thing, the spirited part, to the unruly beast, and for the sake of money and that beast's insatiable greed accustoms it, mistreated from youth onward, to become an ape instead of a lion? {{{ 590c }}}

Very much so, he said.

And why do you think that the vulgar trades and manual crafts bring reproach? Or shall we say it is for any reason other than this — that someone has by nature a weak form of the best part, so that he cannot rule the creatures within him but must serve them, and can learn only how to flatter them?

So it seems, he said.

Then, so that a person of this sort too may be ruled by something like what rules the best man, do we not say that he must be a slave to that best man {{{ 590d }}}, who also has the divine element ruling within himself — not supposing that he must be ruled to the slave's own harm, as Thrasymachus supposed of the ruled, but on the ground that it is better for everyone to be ruled by what is divine and practically wise, best of all having it as his own within himself, but failing that, set over him from outside — so that as far as possible we may all be alike and friends, governed by the same thing?

And rightly so, he said. {{{ 590e }}}

And the law too shows, I said, that it wants something of this sort, being an ally to everyone in the city; and so does the governance of children — not letting them be free — until we establish in them, as in a city, a constitution, and, having cultivated the best part {{{ 591a }}} in them with the corresponding part in us, set up in its place within them a guardian and ruler like it, and then, indeed, we release them as free.

Yes, it shows this, he said.

In what way, then, and by what argument shall we say, Glaucon, that it profits one to act unjustly, or to be licentious, or to do anything shameful — actions from which he will become more corrupt, even if he acquires more money or some other power?

In no way, he said.

But in what way does it profit one who acts unjustly to escape notice and not pay the penalty? {{{ 591b }}} Or is it not true that the one who escapes notice becomes even more corrupt, while for the one who does not escape notice and is punished, the bestial part is lulled to sleep and tamed, and the tame part is set free, and the whole soul, settling into its best nature, acquires a more honorable condition, gaining moderation and justice together with practical wisdom, than a body does in gaining strength and beauty together with health — more honorable by as much as soul is more honorable than body?

Certainly, he said. {{{ 591c }}}

Then will not the man who has understanding live his whole life directing all that is his toward this end — honoring, first of all, the studies that will render his soul of this kind, and holding the rest in low regard?

Clearly, he said.

Next, I said, as for the condition and nourishment of the body, he will not live devoted to it so as to surrender himself to bestial and irrational pleasure, nor will he even look to health, nor give first place to becoming strong or healthy or handsome, unless he is also going to {{{ 591d }}} become moderate as a result — but he will always be seen tuning the harmony in his body for the sake of the concord in his soul.

Certainly, he said, if he is indeed to be truly musical.

Then will he not also maintain order and concord, I said, in the acquisition of wealth? And, not dazzled by what the many count as happiness, will he swell the bulk of his possessions without limit, and so have troubles without end?

I do not think so, he said. {{{ 591e }}}

But rather, I said, keeping his eye on the constitution within himself, and guarding against anything there being disturbed by too much or too little wealth, he will, steering himself in this way, add to and spend from his property so far as he is able.

Quite so, he said. {{{ 592a }}}

And moreover, as for honors too, keeping his eye on the same thing, he will willingly share in and taste those he believes will make him better, but those that would undo his existing condition he will avoid, both in private and in public life.

Then he will not be willing, he said, to engage in politics — at any rate, if he cares about this.

By the dog, I said, in his own city he certainly will, though perhaps not in his native land, unless some divine stroke of fortune happens to befall it.

I understand, he said; you mean the city we have just been founding in our discussion, the one laid up in words, since I do not think {{{ 592b }}} it exists anywhere on earth.

But, I said, perhaps a pattern is laid up in heaven for the one who wishes to see it, and, seeing it, to found himself by it. It makes no difference whether it exists anywhere or ever will; for he would order his actions by this city alone, and by no other.

That is likely, he said.
