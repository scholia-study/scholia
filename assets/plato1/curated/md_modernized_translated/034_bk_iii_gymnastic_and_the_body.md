## Gymnastic and the Body

So it is, he said.

Well then, I said, does our discussion of music seem to you, too, to have reached its end? It has certainly ended where it ought to end — for musical training, I think, must end in the erotic love of the beautiful.

I agree, he said.

After music, then, our young men are to be reared on gymnastic.

Of course.

In this too they must certainly be reared with care, from childhood {{{ 403d }}} throughout their life. It is somehow, I think, like this — consider it yourself too. For it does not seem to me that a good body, by its own virtue, makes a soul good, but on the contrary that a good soul, by its own virtue, renders the body as good as it can possibly be — so how does it seem to you?

So it seems to me too, he said.

Then, if we cared for the mind well enough and handed over to it the precise working-out of matters concerning the body, while we ourselves merely sketched the {{{ 403e }}} outlines, so as not to talk at excessive length, would we not be acting rightly?

Certainly.

We have already said that they must abstain from drunkenness; for anyone at all, sooner than a guardian, may get drunk and not know where in the world he is.

Yes, he said, for it would be ridiculous for the guardian himself to need a guardian.

But what about food? These men, after all, are athletes competing in the greatest of contests. Or is that not so?

Yes. {{{ 404a }}}

Then would the condition of these ordinary athletes be fitting for them?

Perhaps.

But, I said, that condition is somehow sleepy, and precarious for health. Or do you not see that these athletes sleep their life away, and that if they depart even slightly from their prescribed regimen, they fall gravely and violently ill?

I do.

Our warrior athletes, I said, need some more refined kind of training, since they must be wakeful as watchdogs, and see and hear as keenly as possible, and, amid the many changes they undergo on campaign — of water {{{ 404b }}} and other food, of scorching heat and winter cold — must not be liable to break down in health.

So it seems to me.

Then would the best gymnastic be a kind of sister to the simple music we described a little earlier?

What do you mean?

A gymnastic that is simple and decent, I suppose, and especially the kind fit for war.

How so?

One could learn such things from Homer too, I said. For you know that, on campaign, in the feasts of the heroes he never feeds them on fish, even though they are camped by the {{{ 404c }}} sea on the Hellespont, nor on boiled meats but only roasted, which are surely the most convenient for soldiers; for pretty much everywhere, so to speak, it is easier to make use of fire itself than to carry pots and pans around.

Quite so.

Nor, I think, does Homer ever once mention seasonings. Or is this something even other athletes know — that a body meant to be in good condition must abstain from all such things?

And rightly so, he said — they know it, and they abstain. {{{ 404d }}}

Then, my friend, since these things seem right to you, you evidently do not commend a Syracusan table and the variety of Sicilian relishes.

I do not think I do.

Then you also disapprove of a Corinthian girl as a friend for men who are to be in good bodily condition.

Absolutely.

And the supposed delights of Attic pastries as well?

Necessarily.

For I think that if we compare this whole manner of eating and living to song and melody composed in every {{{ 404e }}} mode and every rhythm, we would be comparing it rightly.

Of course.

And there variety bred licentiousness, while here it breeds disease; whereas simplicity, in music, breeds moderation in souls, and in gymnastic, health in bodies?

Most true, he said. {{{ 405a }}}

And when licentiousness and disease multiply in a city, do not law courts and clinics open up in great numbers, and advocacy and medicine give themselves airs, when even many free men come to take them very seriously indeed?

How could it be otherwise?

Could you find any greater proof of a city's bad and shameful education than this — that it needs first-rate doctors and judges, not only for the common sort and the manual laborers, but even for those who claim to have been raised in the manner of free men? {{{ 405b }}} Or does it not seem shameful, and a great proof of a lack of education, to be forced to use justice imported from others, as though from masters and arbiters, for want of any of one's own?

The most shameful thing of all, he said.

Does it seem to you, I said, that there is something even more shameful than this — when a man not only wears away the greater part of his life in the courts, prosecuting and defending, but even, from sheer want of taste, is persuaded to preen himself on this very thing, as one clever {{{ 405c }}} at wrongdoing and able to twist and turn every way, to slip through every loophole and wriggle free, so as to escape justice, and all this for the sake of small and worthless matters, not knowing how much finer and better it would be to arrange his life so as never to need a drowsy judge at all?

No, he said, this is even more shameful than that.

And needing medicine, I said, not on account of wounds or the occasional seasonal illness that befalls one, but because, {{{ 405d }}} through idleness and the sort of regimen we have described, filling oneself up like a stagnant pond with fluids and vapors, and forcing the clever sons of Asclepius to fasten names like flatulence and catarrh onto diseases — does that not seem shameful?

Very much so, he said; these truly are strange and newfangled names for diseases.

Such names, I said, did not exist, I think, in the time of Asclepius. {{{ 405e }}} And I infer this because his sons, at Troy, when Eurypylus was wounded and given Pramnian wine with a good deal of barley meal {{{ 406a }}} sprinkled on it, and grated cheese besides — which are surely thought to cause phlegm — did not reproach the woman who gave it to him to drink, nor did they find fault with Patroclus, who was treating him.

And yet, he said, that is certainly a strange drink to give a man in his condition.

Not so strange, I said, if you consider that the sons of Asclepius, so they say, did not practice this nursemaid style of medicine we have now until Herodicus came along; but Herodicus, being a trainer, and then becoming sickly, mixed gymnastic {{{ 406b }}} with medicine, and wore down himself first and foremost, and many others afterward.

How so? He asked.

By making his death a long one, I said. For, tracking a disease that was fatal, he was not able, I think, to cure himself, but lived out his life treating himself to the neglect of everything else, tormented if he departed at all from his accustomed regimen, and, dying hard thanks to his own cleverness, arrived at old age.

A fine reward, he said, he won from his craft.
