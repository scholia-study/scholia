## Two Portraits: The Perfectly Unjust and the Perfectly Just Man

{{{ 360e }}} Now, as for the judgment itself about the life of the men we are speaking of — if we set the most just man and the most unjust man apart from one another, we will be able to judge correctly; otherwise, we will not. What, then, is this separation? This — let us take nothing away, either from the unjust man's injustice or from the just man's justice, but set each of them, in full, into his own pursuit. First, then, let the unjust man act as skilled craftsmen do — a master pilot or doctor, say, perceives what is impossible in his craft and what is possible, {{{ 361a }}} undertakes the one while leaving the other alone, and further, if he does happen to slip somewhere, is able to set it right — so let the unjust man too, in pursuing his wrongdoings correctly, escape notice, if he is to be unjust in the extreme. The one who is caught must be reckoned a poor hand at it; for the extreme of injustice is to seem just without being so. We must, then, grant the completely unjust man the most complete injustice, and not take anything away, but allow that, while doing the greatest wrongs, he has secured for himself the greatest possible reputation {{{ 361b }}} for justice — that if he does happen to slip in something, he is able to set it right; that he is a good enough speaker to win people over if any of his wrongdoing comes to light; and that he can use force wherever force is needed, through courage and strength, and through his supply of friends and wealth. Having made him such, let us in turn set the just man beside him in our discussion — a simple, noble man who, in Aeschylus's phrase, wishes not to seem but to be good. The seeming, then, must be taken away. For if he is thought to be {{{ 361c }}} just, he will have honors and gifts because he is thought to be such a man; then it would be unclear whether he is such a man for the sake of justice, or for the sake of the gifts and honors. He must, then, be stripped of everything except justice, and made the very opposite of the man before him — while doing no wrong, let him have the reputation of being unjust in the extreme, so that his justice may be tested by his not softening under ill repute and what comes from it; instead let him go unchanged until {{{ 361d }}} death, seeming unjust throughout his life while in fact being just, so that both men, having each come to the furthest extreme — one of justice, the other of injustice — may be judged as to which of the two is happier.

Goodness, my dear Glaucon, I said, how vigorously you scour each of the two men clean, like a statue, for the judging.

As best I can, he said. Now that the two of them are such, I think it is no longer difficult to go on in our account and describe what sort of life awaits {{{ 361e }}} each of them. It must be said, then — and even if it is said somewhat crudely, do not suppose it is I who say it, Socrates, but those who praise injustice ahead of justice. They will say this: that the just man, disposed in this way, will be whipped, racked on the wheel, bound in chains, {{{ 362a }}} have his eyes burned out, and finally, after suffering every evil, be impaled, and will come to know that one must wish not to be just but to seem so. So the saying of Aeschylus, it turns out, would be far more rightly spoken of the unjust man. For in truth they will say that the unjust man, since he pursues a business that holds fast to truth and does not live with an eye to reputation, wishes not to seem unjust but to be so,
+ reaping a deep furrow through his mind,
{{{ 362b }}}
+ from which prudent counsels spring,
first ruling in the city because he is thought to be just, then marrying from wherever he wishes and giving his daughters in marriage to whomever he wishes, entering into contracts and partnerships with whomever he likes, and besides all this, profiting by feeling no distaste at doing wrong; then, going into contests both private and public, coming out ahead and getting the better of his enemies, and by getting the better of them growing rich and {{{ 362c }}} benefiting his friends and harming his enemies, offering sacrifices to the gods and dedicating offerings amply and splendidly, and serving the gods — and whichever men he wishes — far better than the just man does, so that it is more fitting, in all likelihood, that he rather than the just man be dear to the gods. So they say, Socrates, that life is arranged better for the unjust man than for the just, both by gods and by men.
