## Modes and Rhythms

{{{ 398c }}} Well then, I said, what remains after this is the subject of the manner of song and of melodies?

Clearly.

Then would not anyone by now find for himself what we must say about them, what sort they should be, if indeed we are going to be in accord with what has already been said?

And Glaucon, laughing, said, Well now, Socrates, I am in danger of being outside the whole business — I am not well enough equipped at present to work out just what sort of things we ought to say; I do have my suspicions, though.

At any rate, I said, you are well enough equipped to say {{{ 398d }}} this much first — that a melody is composed of three things, words and harmony and rhythm.

Yes, he said, that much.

Then so far as it consists of words, surely it does not differ at all from unsung words in needing to be spoken in the same patterns we described just now, and in the same way?

True, he said.

And indeed harmony and rhythm must follow the words.

How could they not?

But surely we said that our words have no need at all of dirges and lamentations.

No indeed. {{{ 398e }}}

Which harmonies, then, are suited to dirges? Tell me — you are the musical one.

Mixolydian, he said, and Syntonolydian, and some such.

Then must these, I said, be removed? They are useless even for women who are meant to be decent, let alone for men.

Certainly.

And indeed drunkenness is most unbecoming for guardians, and softness, and idleness.

How could they not be?

Which of the harmonies, then, are soft and suited to drinking parties?

Ionian, he said, and Lydian too — some of them are called relaxed. {{{ 399a }}}

Then, my friend, will you make any use of these for warlike men?

Not at all, he said — but it looks as though Dorian and Phrygian are what is left to you.

I do not know the harmonies myself, I said, but leave that harmony which would fittingly imitate the tones and accents of a brave man engaged in warlike action and in every violent undertaking, who meets failure, or goes toward wounds or {{{ 399b }}} death, or falls into some other misfortune, and who in all these circumstances wards off fortune steadily and with endurance; and leave another, in turn, for one engaged in a peaceful and unforced but voluntary action — persuading someone of something and making a request, whether of a god by prayer or of a person by teaching and admonition, or, on the contrary, submitting himself to another who requests or teaches or persuades him — and who, having acted accordingly as he intended, and not being carried away with pride, but conducting himself with moderation and measure in all these {{{ 399c }}} things, and being content with the outcome. These two harmonies, then — violent and voluntary, of the unfortunate, of the fortunate, of the moderate, of the brave — whichever ones will best imitate the sounds, leave those.

Well, he said, you are not asking to leave any others than the ones I just mentioned.

Then we shall have no need, I said, of many strings or of a pan-harmonic instrument in our songs and melodies.

It does not seem so to me, he said.

Then we will not keep craftsmen of trigons and pektides and all the instruments {{{ 399d }}} that are many-stringed and many-tuned.

No, it seems we will not.

What next? Will you admit makers of auloi or aulos-players into the city? Or is this not the most many-stringed thing of all, and do not the pan-harmonic instruments themselves happen to be an imitation of the aulos?

Clearly, he said.

The lyre, then, I said, and the kithara are left for you as useful in the city; and in the countryside, in turn, some sort of syrinx might be for the shepherds.

So at least, he said, our argument indicates. {{{ 399e }}}

We are doing nothing new, my friend, I said, in preferring Apollo and Apollo's instruments to Marsyas and his instruments.

No, by Zeus, he said, it does not seem to me that we are.

And by the dog, I said, without even noticing it, we have been purifying again the city we just now said was living in luxury.

Showing our moderation, at any rate, he said.

Come then, I said, let us purify what is left. For what follows next, in due order after the harmonies, would be the subject of rhythms — we should not pursue elaborate rhythms or every kind of metrical foot, but rather see what the rhythms of an orderly and courageous life are; and once we have seen those, we should compel the {{{ 400a }}} foot — and the melody too — to follow the words of such a life, and not the words to follow the foot and the melody. But which these rhythms actually are, it is your job, just as with the harmonies, to tell me.

But by Zeus, he said, I cannot say. As for the fact that there are some three kinds from which the metrical feet are woven, just as there are four among the notes, from which come all the harmonies — that I could say, having observed it; but which kinds are imitations of which sort of life, I am not able to say. {{{ 400b }}}

But these matters, I said, we shall take counsel over with Damon too — which feet suit illiberality and insolence, or madness and other vice, and which rhythms must be left for their opposites; and I think I have heard him — though not clearly — naming a certain compound enoplios, and the dactyl, and the heroic foot too, arranging it somehow, I do not know how, and setting it equal above and below, resolving into short and long, and, as I think, he called one an iamb and another a trochee, attaching to them lengths and {{{ 400c }}} shortnesses. And in some of these, I think, he criticized and praised the movements of the foot no less than the rhythms themselves — or perhaps some combination of both, I cannot say — but these matters, as I said, let us defer to Damon, since to sort them out is no small task. Or do you think otherwise?

By Zeus, not I.

But can you distinguish at least this much — that gracefulness and gracelessness follow good rhythm and bad rhythm?

How could I not?
