## Choosing and Testing the Students

It does to me, at any rate, he said.

What remains, then, I said, is the distribution — to whom we shall give these studies, and in what way.

Clearly, he said.

Do you remember, then, the earlier selection of the rulers, what sort of people we selected?

Of course, he said — how could I not?

As for the rest, I said, you must suppose that those same natures need to be selected; for the steadiest and the most courageous are to be preferred, and, so far as possible, the most graceful in form; {{{ 535b }}} and besides these, we must look not only for men noble and stern in character, but also for their having the natural traits suited to this education.

Which ones do you mean?

Keenness, my good friend, I said, is what they must have for their studies, and they must not find learning difficult. For souls grow far more faint-hearted in the face of demanding studies than in physical exercises; for the toil is more their own — private, and not shared with the body.

True, he said. {{{ 535c }}}

And indeed we must look for one who is retentive in memory, sturdy, and thoroughly fond of hard work. Or in what way do you suppose anyone will be willing to work hard at the body's exercises, and also carry through so much learning and practice?

No one, he said, unless he is altogether well-gifted by nature.

At any rate, I said, the present mistake and the disrepute that have fallen on philosophy for these reasons — as we said before too — come of this: that people take hold of it in a way unworthy of it; for it ought not to be bastards who lay hold of it, but true-born sons.

How so? So he asked. {{{ 535d }}}

First, I said, the one who is to take hold of it must not be lame in his love of toil, being industrious in one half but idle in the other. This happens whenever someone is fond of gymnastics and of hunting, and loves toil in everything done through the body, but is not fond of learning, nor fond of listening, nor inquisitive, but instead hates the toil in all these things; and the one who has shifted his love of toil the other way around is lame as well.

What you say is very true, he said.

So then, with a view to truth as well, I said, shall we in this same way set down as crippled {{{ 535e }}} a soul that hates the voluntary lie and bears it ill, growing exceedingly indignant whenever others lie, but welcomes the involuntary lie easily and, caught somewhere in ignorance, feels no indignation, but wallows readily in ignorance like a pig? {{{ 536a }}}

Quite so, he said.

And with a view to moderation too, I said, and courage, and magnificence, and all the parts of virtue, we must be no less on our guard to distinguish the bastard from the true-born. For whenever a private person or a city does not know how to examine such matters thoroughly, they unwittingly make use of lame and bastard men for whatever purpose these happen to serve — some as friends, others as rulers.

Yes indeed, he said, that is how it is.

We, then, I said, must be very careful about all such matters; {{{ 536b }}} for if we bring men sound of limb and sound of mind to so great a course of learning and so great a training, and educate them accordingly, Justice herself will not blame us, and we shall preserve the city and its constitution, but if we lead in men of a different kind, we shall do everything the opposite way, and pour down even more ridicule on philosophy.

That would indeed be shameful, he said.

Quite so, I said; and I myself seem to have suffered ⟨something⟩ ridiculous even just now.

What sort of thing? So he asked. {{{ 536c }}}

I forgot, I said, that we were only playing, and I spoke with more intensity than I meant to. For as I was speaking, I happened to glance toward philosophy, and, seeing her disgracefully mistreated, I seem to myself to have grown indignant and, as though angry at those responsible, to have said what I said more in earnest than I should.

No, by Zeus, he said, not to me, at least, as your listener.

But it seemed so to me, I said, as the one speaking. But let us not forget this: that in the earlier selection we chose older men, {{{ 536d }}} whereas in this one it will not be possible; for we must not trust Solon when he says that a man growing old is capable of learning much — less, rather, than he can run; and all the great and numerous labors belong to the young.

It must be so, he said.

The subjects of calculation, then, and geometry, and all the preliminary training that must be taught before dialectic, must be set before them while they are still children, without giving the form of the instruction any character of compulsion.

Why is that? {{{ 536e }}}

Because, I said, no free person should learn any subject under compulsion, the way a slave does. For while bodily labors performed under compulsion do the body no harm, no forced learning stays with the soul.

True, he said.

Do not, then, I said, my excellent friend, raise the children in their {{{ 537a }}} studies by force, but through play, so that you may also be better able to discern the natural bent of each.

There is reason in what you say, he said.

Do you not remember, then, I said, that we also said the children must be brought to war as spectators, mounted on horseback, and, wherever it is safe, brought close and given a taste of blood, like young hounds?

I remember, he said.

In all these things, then, I said — the labors, the studies, and the fears — whoever always proves quickest must be enrolled in a select number. {{{ 537b }}}

At what age, he asked, is this to happen?

When, I said, they are released from the required exercises; for this period — whether it turns out to be two years or three — is capable of nothing else, since weariness and sleep are hostile to learning. And at the same time, this is also one of the trials, and not the least of them — what each one shows himself to be in the gymnastic exercises.

How could it not? So he said.

After this period, then, I said, those selected from the twenty-year-olds will receive greater honors than the rest, {{{ 537c }}} and the subjects that were given to them scattered throughout their childhood education must now be gathered together for them into a synoptic view of the kinship of the studies with one another and with the nature of being.

That kind of learning alone, at any rate, he said, is secure, in whomever it comes to take root.

And it is also the greatest test, I said, of a dialectical nature or the lack of one; for the one who can take a synoptic view is a dialectician, and the one who cannot is not.

I agree, he said.

These things, then, I said, will require you to observe which of them, {{{ 537d }}} above all, prove to be of this kind, and steadfast in their studies, steadfast in war, and in the other lawful pursuits, and then, when these in turn pass beyond thirty years, to select them out from among those already preferred, establish them in greater honors, and examine them, testing them by the power of dialectic, to see who is capable of letting go of the eyes and the rest of the senses and going, together with truth, toward being itself. And it is just here, my friend, that a task of great caution lies.
