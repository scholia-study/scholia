## The Mathematical Studies: Arithmetic, Geometry, and Solids

{{{ 524d }}} Most rightly, he said.

This, then, is just what I was trying to say a moment ago: that some things summon the understanding while others do not, defining as summoning whatever falls upon perception together with its own opposite, and whatever does not do this as not rousing reason.

I understand now, he said, and it seems so to me.

What then? To which of the two do number and the one seem to belong?

I do not see it, he said.

But reason it out, I said, from what has already been said. For if the one is adequately seen just by itself, or grasped by some other perception, {{{ 524e }}} it would not draw the soul toward being, as we said in the case of the finger; but if some opposite is always seen along with it at the same time, so that it appears no more one than its opposite, there would then be need of something to judge between them, and in that case the soul would be compelled to be at a loss and to inquire, stirring the notion within itself, and to ask what the one itself really is, and thus the study of the one would be among the things that lead and turn {{{ 525a }}} the soul toward the vision of being.

But surely, he said, the sight of it has this feature not least; for we see the same thing at once as one and as unlimited in multitude.

Then if this is true of the one, I said, does not all number undergo the same thing?

How could it not?

But surely calculation and arithmetic are wholly concerned with number.

Very much so. {{{ 525b }}}

And these plainly lead toward truth.

Yes, extraordinarily so.

Then it would seem to be among the studies we are seeking; for the man of war must learn these for the sake of his formations, while the philosopher must learn them because he has to rise up out of becoming and lay hold of being, or else never become skilled in calculation.

That is so, he said.

And our guardian happens to be both a man of war and a philosopher.

Of course.

It would then be a fitting study, Glaucon, to establish by law, and to persuade those who are to share in the greatest things in the city {{{ 525c }}} to go in for calculation and to take hold of it, not in a layman's way, but until they arrive, by reason itself, at the contemplation of the nature of numbers, not for the sake of buying and selling, practicing it as merchants or shopkeepers do, but for the sake of war, and for the soul's own ease in turning from becoming to truth and being.

You put it very well, he said.

And indeed, I said, now that the study {{{ 525d }}} of calculations has been mentioned, I also notice how subtle it is and how useful to us in many ways for what we want, if one pursues it for the sake of knowing rather than for the sake of peddling.

In what way? So he asked.

In this way — what we were just saying, that it strongly leads the soul upward somewhere and compels it to discourse about the numbers themselves, in no way accepting it if someone offers for discussion numbers that have visible or tangible bodies. For you know, I suppose, that those who are clever in these matters, if someone attempts to cut the one itself {{{ 525e }}} in argument, laugh at him and will not accept it, but if you break it into fractions, they multiply it, taking care that the one should never appear to be not one but many parts.

Most true, he said. {{{ 526a }}}

What then do you think, Glaucon, if someone should ask them, "You wonderful men, what numbers are you talking about, in which the one is such as you maintain — each one equal to every other, differing not even slightly, and having no part within itself?" What do you think they would answer?

This is what I, at any rate, think they would say: that they are speaking of things which admit only of being thought, and which cannot be handled in any other way.

You see then, my friend, I said, that this study {{{ 526b }}} really does seem to be necessary for us, since it plainly compels the soul to use reason itself upon truth itself?

And indeed, he said, it certainly does that.

And what of this? Have you already observed this, that those who are by nature good at calculation are born quick, so to speak, at all studies, while the slow, if they are trained and exercised in this, even if they gain no other benefit, still all make progress, at least, toward becoming quicker than they themselves were?

It is so, he said. {{{ 526c }}}

And indeed, as I think, you would not easily find many studies that give the learner and practitioner more toil than this.

No, indeed not.

For all these reasons, then, this study must not be abandoned, but those of the best natures must be educated in it.

I agree, he said.

Let this, then, be settled as one point for us; and let us consider, as a second, whether what comes next after it is at all fitting for us.

Which is that? Or do you mean geometry, he said?

That very thing, I said. {{{ 526d }}}

So far as it bears on warfare, he said, it is clear that it is fitting; for in setting up camps, and seizing positions, and massing and extending an army, and in all the other formations that armies take up in the actual battles and on the march, a man would differ from himself according as he is a geometer or not.

But still, I said, for purposes of that sort a small portion of geometry and calculation would suffice; what we must examine is whether the greater part of it, going on further, tends toward that {{{ 526e }}} — toward making it easier to discern the form of the good. And we say that everything tends toward that same point which compels the soul to turn around toward that region in which is the most blessed part of being, which it must by every means behold.

You are right, he said.

Then if it compels the soul to contemplate being, it is fitting; but if it compels it to contemplate becoming, it is not fitting.

So we say, at any rate. {{{ 527a }}}

Then this, at least, I said, is something no one with even a little experience of geometry will dispute with us — that this knowledge is entirely the opposite of what those who practice it say about it in their accounts.

How is that? So he asked.

They speak, I suppose, in a very ridiculous way, though they cannot help it; for as though they were doing something and making all their arguments for the sake of action, they talk of "squaring," and "applying," and "adding," and use all such expressions, when in fact the whole {{{ 527b }}} study is pursued for the sake of knowledge.

Yes, entirely so, he said.

Then must not this further be agreed?

What is that?

That it is knowledge of what always is, and not of anything that at some time comes to be and passes away.

That is readily agreed, he said; for geometrical knowledge is knowledge of what always is.

Then, my noble friend, it would draw the soul toward truth, and would produce a philosopher's understanding, so as to hold above what we now wrongly hold below.

As much as possible, he said. {{{ 527c }}}

Then, I said, it must be enjoined as strongly as possible that those in your beautiful city shall in no way hold themselves apart from geometry. For even its by-products are not small.

What are they? So he asked.

Those you mentioned yourself, I said, the ones concerning war, and then, for all studies too, in respect of taking them in better — we know, I suppose, that it makes the whole and entire difference whether a man has laid hold of geometry or not.

The whole difference indeed, by Zeus, he said.

Shall we then set this down as a second study for the young?

Let us set it down, he said. {{{ 527d }}}

And what of this? Shall we set down astronomy as a third? Or does it not seem so to you?

It does to me, at any rate, he said; for to be more keenly sensitive about the seasons, and the months, and the years, is fitting not only for farming and seafaring, but no less for generalship.

You are a pleasant fellow, I said, in that you seem afraid of the multitude, lest you should seem to be prescribing useless studies. But in fact it is no trifle, though it is hard to believe, that in these studies a certain instrument of the soul of each person is purified and {{{ 527e }}} rekindled, after it has been destroyed and blinded by other pursuits, though it is worth more to have it preserved than ten thousand eyes; for by it alone is truth seen. Now to those who agree with this, you will seem to speak marvelously well, but those who have never in any way perceived it will naturally think you are saying nothing; for they see no benefit from these studies worth mentioning. Consider {{{ 528a }}} then, right away, which of the two you are addressing; or whether you are addressing neither, but are making these arguments chiefly for your own sake, though you would not begrudge it to anyone else either, if someone were able to derive some benefit from them.

That is what I choose, he said, to speak and to ask and to answer chiefly for my own sake.

Go back, then, I said; for just now we did not correctly take up what comes next after geometry.

How did we take it? So he asked.

After the plane, I said, we took up the solid {{{ 528b }}} already in revolution, before taking the solid itself by itself; but the correct order is to take up the third dimension right after the second. This, I suppose, has to do with the dimension of cubes, and whatever partakes of depth.

It is indeed, he said; but this, Socrates, does not yet seem to have been discovered.

The causes are twofold, I said; because no city holds these studies in honor, they are pursued feebly, being difficult as they are, and those who pursue them need a director, without whom they would not discover anything — a director who, in the first place, is difficult to come by, and then, even once he has come to be, as things now stand, {{{ 528c }}} those who are inquiring into these matters, thinking too highly of themselves, would not be persuaded by him. But if the whole city were to direct the inquiry together, holding these studies in honor, then those men would be persuaded, and these matters, sought continuously and intently, would become clear as they really are; since even now, dishonored and cut short as they are by the many, and pursued by inquirers who have no account to give of their usefulness, they nevertheless, against all this, grow by force of their own charm, and it would be nothing surprising if they should come to light. {{{ 528d }}}

And indeed, he said, their charm is truly exceptional. But tell me more clearly what you were just saying. For you were setting down the study of the plane as geometry, I suppose.

Yes, I said.

Then, he said, at first you put astronomy after this, but afterward you drew back.

In hurrying, I said, to go quickly through everything, I am only going more slowly; for since the method of the dimension of depth, because of the ridiculous state of its inquiry, comes next in order, I passed over it and spoke of astronomy {{{ 528e }}} right after geometry, though astronomy is itself a motion involving depth.
