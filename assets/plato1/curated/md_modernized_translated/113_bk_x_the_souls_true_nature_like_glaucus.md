## The Soul's True Nature, Like Glaucus

That is necessary, he said.

Let this, then, be so, I said; but if it is so, you realize that they would always be the same in number. For they could not become fewer, I suppose, since none is destroyed, nor again more; for if any of the immortal things whatever were to increase, you know that it would come to be out of the mortal, and everything would end up being immortal.

What you say is true.

But, I said, let us not think this — for the argument will not {{{ 611b }}} allow it — nor, again, that the soul is, in its truest nature, such as to be full of great variety and unlikeness and difference within itself.

What do you mean? He said.

It is not easy, I said, for something composed of many parts, and not put together in the best way, to be everlasting — as the soul just now appeared to us to be.

That certainly does not seem likely.

That the soul, then, is immortal, both the argument just given and the others would compel us to admit; but as for what it truly is, one must view it not as maimed {{{ 611c }}} by its association with the body and other evils, the way we now view it, but must contemplate it adequately by reasoning as it is when it becomes pure, and then one will find it far more beautiful, and will see justice and injustice, and everything we have just gone through, more clearly. But now we have spoken truly about it, as it appears in its present state; we have, however, viewed it in a certain condition, just as those who look at {{{ 611d }}} the sea-god Glaucus could no longer easily make out his original nature, because the old parts of his body are partly broken off, and partly ground down and altogether maimed by the waves, while other things have grown onto him — shells and seaweed and stones — so that he looks more like some wild beast than what he was by nature; so too we see the soul in just such a state, from countless evils. But we must look there, Glaucon.

Where? He said. {{{ 611e }}}

To its love of wisdom, and to what it lays hold of and what kind of company it longs for — seeing that it is akin to what is divine and immortal and to what always is — and to what it would become if it gave itself wholly to following such things, and were lifted by this impulse out of the sea it is now in, and had the rocks {{{ 612a }}} and shells knocked from around it, those that now, because it feasts on earth, have grown over it in such numbers, earthy and stony and wild, from those banquets that are called happy. And then one might see its true nature, whether it is many-formed or single-formed, or in whatever state and however it is; but for now we have, I think, gone through its experiences and kinds in human life well enough.

Quite so, he said.
