## The Great Beast: The Crowd as Educator

{{{ 492a }}} Now the nature of the philosopher that we laid down, if it happens, I think, to meet with the learning that suits it, must of necessity grow to arrive at every virtue; but if it is sown and reared in unsuitable soil and tended there, it must arrive, in turn, at every opposite, unless some god comes to its aid. Or do you too suppose, as most people do, that certain young men are corrupted by sophists, and that private sophists corrupt them to any degree worth mentioning, and not rather that the very men who say this are themselves the greatest sophists {{{ 492b }}} of all, and educate most completely, turning out young and old, men and women, into just the sort of people they want?

When, exactly? So he asked.

Whenever, I said, great numbers of people sit crowded together in assemblies, law courts, theaters, army camps, or some other public gathering of the multitude, and with a great deal of noise find fault with some of what is said or done, and praise other parts of it, both to excess, shouting and clapping, and, besides {{{ 492c }}} them, the very rocks and the place they are in echo back and double the din of blame and praise. In such a situation, as the saying goes, what heart do you think a young man has? Or what private education [would] hold out in him, one that will not be swamped by blame and praise of that sort and go off, borne along on the current wherever it may carry it, so that he calls the same things fine and shameful as they do, pursues the same things as they do, and becomes such a person himself? {{{ 492d }}}

Quite inevitably, Socrates, he said.

And yet, I said, we have not yet mentioned the greatest necessity.

What sort? So he said.

The one these educators and sophists apply in deed, when they fail to persuade with words. Or do you not know that they punish whoever is not persuaded with loss of civic rights, fines, and death?

Very much so indeed, he said.

So what other sophist, or what private arguments running counter to these, do you think will prevail? {{{ 492e }}}

None, I think, he said.

No indeed, I said, and even to attempt it is great folly. For no character schooled toward virtue apart from the education these men give is coming to be, has come to be, or ever will come to be — no human one, at any rate, my friend; the divine, though, let us except from the argument, as the old saying goes — for you must know well that in such a condition of constitutions, whenever anything is preserved {{{ 493a }}} and turns out as it should, you will not be wrong to say that it was a god's portion that preserved it.

Nor do I think otherwise, he said.

Then let this be your view as well, I said, in addition to what we have said.

What sort of thing?

Each of the hired private individuals — the ones these men call sophists and regard as rivals in their craft — teaches nothing but these very opinions of the many, the ones they hold whenever they are gathered together, and calls this wisdom; it is just as if someone were studying the moods and {{{ 493b }}} appetites of a great, strong beast being reared — how to approach it and how to handle it, when it is fiercest or gentlest and from what causes, and the sounds it is accustomed to make in each case, and what sounds, spoken by another, tame it or make it savage — and, having learned all this by living with it over a long time, were to call it wisdom, and, having put it together as a craft, were to turn to teaching it, knowing nothing in truth about which of these opinions and appetites is fine or shameful, {{{ 493c }}} or good or bad, or just or unjust, but were to apply all these names according to the opinions of the great creature, calling the things it delights in good and the things that vex it bad, having no other account of them, but calling the necessary things just and fine, without ever having seen, or being able to show anyone else, how far the necessary differs in truth from the good. A man like that, by Zeus, would he not seem to you an absurd educator?

He does, he said.

Does he seem to you, then, to differ at all from this man — the one who counts it wisdom to have made out the temper and the pleasures of the many and {{{ 493d }}} motley crowd when they come together, whether in painting, or in music, or indeed in politics? For whenever someone associates with these people, displaying his poetry or some other piece of craftsmanship, or a service to the city, and makes the many his masters over and above what is necessary, it is what they call the Diomedean necessity that compels him to do whatever they praise; but as for these things being also good and fine in truth, have you ever yet heard one of them give an account of that which was not laughable? {{{ 493e }}}

I think not, he said, and I don't expect I ever shall.

So, having taken all this to heart, recall that other point — the beautiful itself and not the many beautiful things, or each thing itself and {{{ 494a }}} not the many particular things: is there any way a multitude will put up with it, or think that such a thing is?

Not in the least, he said.

So it is impossible, I said, for a multitude to be philosophic.

Impossible.

And so those who philosophize must necessarily be blamed by them.

Necessarily.

And by these private individuals too, all those who, associating with the crowd, desire to please it.

Clearly.

Given all this, then, what salvation do you see for a philosophic nature, such that it remains in its pursuit and comes through to its end? Consider it {{{ 494b }}} from what came before. For we have agreed that ease in learning, memory, courage, and magnificence belong to this nature.

Yes.

So won't such a person, right from childhood, be first in everything, especially if his body too grows to match his soul?

Why wouldn't he be? So he said.

I think, then, that when he grows older, his relatives and fellow citizens will want to make use of him for their own affairs.

Of course. {{{ 494c }}}

So they will grovel before him, begging and honoring him, staking their claim in advance on the power that is going to be his and flattering it before it comes.

That's certainly how it tends to happen, he said.

So what do you think, I said, such a person will do in circumstances like these, especially if he happens to belong to a great city, and in it to be rich and well-born, and on top of that good-looking and tall? Won't he be filled with impossible hope, believing that he will be capable {{{ 494d }}} of managing the affairs of both Greeks and barbarians, and on the strength of this exalt himself, puffed up with pretension and an empty conceit devoid of intellect?

Very much so, he said.

Now if someone quietly approaches a person disposed like this and tells him the truth — that he has no intellect in him, though he needs it, and that it cannot be acquired unless one serves as a slave to acquiring it — do you think it easy for him to listen, surrounded by so many evils?

Far from it, he said.

But if, then, I said, because of his good nature and his kinship {{{ 494e }}} with such arguments, he perceives something of this and bends and is drawn toward philosophy, what do we think those people will do — the ones who believe they are losing the use of him and his companionship? Will they not do every deed and say every word, both about him, so that he won't be persuaded, and toward the one persuading him, so that he won't be able to succeed, plotting against him privately and bringing him into public contests? {{{ 495a }}}

Quite inevitably, he said.

Is there any way, then, that such a person will philosophize?

Not at all.

So do you see, I said, that we were not wrong to say that the very parts of a philosophic nature themselves, when they fall into bad rearing, are in a way responsible for its falling away from its pursuit, along with the so-called goods — wealth and all such equipment?

No indeed, he said, but it was said rightly.

This, then, my good man, I said, is the ruin and corruption, {{{ 495b }}} so great and of such a kind, that befalls the best nature for the best pursuit — a nature that is rare enough on its own, as we say. And indeed it is from these men that those who work the greatest evils on cities, and on private people, arise — and also those who work the greatest goods, whenever they happen to be swept in that direction instead; but a small nature never accomplishes anything great, for a private person or for a city.

Very true, he said.
