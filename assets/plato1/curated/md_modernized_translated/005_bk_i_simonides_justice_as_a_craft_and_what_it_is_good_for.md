## Simonides: Justice as a Craft, and What It Is Good For

{{{ 331e }}} Tell me, then, I said, you who have inherited the argument — what do you say Simonides means when he speaks rightly about justice?

That giving back to each what is owed is just, he said; in saying this he seems to me to speak well.

But indeed, I said, it is not easy to distrust Simonides — he is a wise and inspired man — yet what he actually means by this, you perhaps know, Polemarchus, while I do not; for clearly he does not mean what we were just saying, that whatever a man has deposited should be given {{{ 332a }}} back to him when he asks for it out of his mind. And yet surely what he deposited is something owed — is it not?

Yes.

And it is not to be given back at all, at a time when the one asking for it is out of his mind?

True, he said.

Then it seems Simonides means something other than this when he says that giving back what is owed is just.

Something else indeed, by Zeus, he said; for he thinks that friends owe it to friends to do them some good and no harm.

I understand, I said — that whoever gives back gold deposited with him is not giving back what is owed, if the giving back {{{ 332b }}} and the taking turn out harmful, though giver and receiver are friends — is that not how you say Simonides means it?

Certainly.

What then? Should one give back to enemies whatever happens to be owed?

Certainly, he said — whatever is indeed owed to them; and what is owed, I suppose, from an enemy to an enemy, and what is fitting, is some harm.

Simonides, then, it seems, I said, spoke in riddles, poet that he is, {{{ 332c }}} about what justice is. For it seems he had in mind that justice is giving to each what is fitting, and this he called what is owed.

But what do you think? So he asked.

By Zeus, I said, suppose then someone had asked him — Simonides, to whom, and giving what that is owed and fitting, is a craft called medicine? What do you think he would have answered us?

Clearly, he said — the craft that gives to bodies drugs, food, and drink.

And to whom, and giving what that is owed and fitting, is a craft called cookery? {{{ 332d }}}

The craft that gives seasonings to dishes.

Well then; to whom, and giving what, would a craft be called justice?

If indeed, he said, we must follow what was said before, Socrates — the craft that gives benefits to friends and harms to enemies.

So he means that doing good to friends and harm to enemies is justice?

So it seems to me.

Then who is most capable of doing good to friends who are sick, and harm to enemies, with regard to disease and health?

The doctor. {{{ 332e }}}

And who, for those sailing, with regard to the danger of the sea?

The captain.

And the just man? In what activity, and with a view to what task, is he most capable of helping friends and harming enemies?

In making war and fighting as an ally, it seems to me.

Very well; but to those who are not sick, my dear Polemarchus, the doctor is useless.

True.

And to those not sailing, the captain.

Yes.

Then is the just man also useless to those not at war?

That does not seem quite right to me.

So justice is useful even in peace? {{{ 333a }}}

Useful.

And so is farming — or not?

Yes.

For getting produce?

Yes.

And shoemaking too?

Yes.

For getting shoes, I suppose you would say?

Certainly.

What then? For the need or getting of what would you say justice is useful in peace?

For contracts, Socrates.

And by contracts do you mean partnerships, or something else?

Partnerships, certainly. {{{ 333b }}}

Is the just man, then, a good and useful partner for a game of draughts, or the draughts-player?

The draughts-player.

But for laying bricks and stones, is the just man a more useful and better partner than the builder?

Not at all.

Then for what partnership is the just man a better partner than the builder and the lyre-player, just as the lyre-player is better than the just man for playing notes?

For money, it seems to me.

Except perhaps, Polemarchus, for the use of money — when one needs to join in buying or selling a horse; {{{ 333c }}} then, I think, it is rather the horseman. Is that not so?

It appears so.

And indeed when it is a ship, the shipwright or the captain?

So it seems.

Then when is the just man more useful than the others for a joint use of silver or gold?

When it needs to be deposited and kept safe, Socrates.

Then you mean when there is no need to use it at all, but only to have it lying there?

Certainly.

So when silver is useless, then justice is useful {{{ 333d }}} for it?

So it seems.

And when a pruning-hook needs guarding, justice is useful, both jointly and privately; but when it needs using, it is the vine-dresser's craft?

It appears so.

And will you say that with a shield and a lyre too, when they need guarding and no use at all, justice is useful, but when they need using, it is the soldier's craft and the musician's?

It must be so.

And so with everything else as well, is justice useless when each thing is in use, but useful when it is not?

So it seems. {{{ 333e }}}

Then justice, my friend, could hardly be a very serious thing, if it turns out to be useful only for useless things. But let us look at this. Is it not true that whoever is cleverest at striking a blow in a fight, whether boxing or any other kind, is also cleverest at guarding against one?

Certainly.

And whoever is clever at guarding against disease — is he not also cleverest at inflicting it without being noticed?

So it seems to me.
