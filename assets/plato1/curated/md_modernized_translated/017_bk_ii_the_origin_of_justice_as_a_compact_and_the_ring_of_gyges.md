## The Origin of Justice as a Compact, and the Ring of Gyges

{{{ 358e }}} Excellently put, he said; and listen now to what I said I would speak of first — what justice is, and where it has come from.

For they say that to do wrong is by nature good and to suffer wrong is bad, but that the badness of suffering wrong outweighs the goodness of doing it, so that when men wrong one another and are wronged in turn, and taste of both, those who cannot {{{ 359a }}} escape the one and seize the other find it profitable to make a compact with one another neither to do wrong nor to suffer it; and from this they began to set up laws and agreements among themselves, and to call what the law commanded lawful and just; and this, they say, is the becoming and being of justice, a thing between the best case — doing wrong and paying no penalty for it — and the worst — suffering wrong with no power to get redress; and the just, lying in the middle between the two, is cherished not as a {{{ 359b }}} good, but as something honored out of an inability to do wrong — since a man who had the power to do it, a true man, would never make a compact with anyone, neither to do wrong nor to suffer it; he would be mad to. So then, Socrates, this is the nature of justice, and this is its character, and these the things it naturally grows from — or so the argument runs.

We would see most clearly that even those who practice justice do so unwillingly, from an inability to do wrong, if we carried out this {{{ 359c }}} experiment in thought — give each of them license to do whatever he wants, the just man and the unjust alike, and then follow along and watch where each one's desire leads him. So we would catch the just man red-handed, converging on the very same goal as the unjust man, because of that desire for more which every nature is inclined to pursue as good, but which law forcibly diverts toward honoring equality. The license I am speaking of would be at its fullest if they came to have such {{{ 359d }}} power as they say once came to the ancestor of [Gyges] the Lydian. He was a shepherd, working for hire under the ruler of Lydia at the time, and when heavy rain and an earthquake struck, the ground split open at a certain place, and a chasm formed where he was pasturing his flock. Seeing it, he marveled, and climbed down, and there among the other wonders the storytellers speak of he saw a bronze horse, hollow, fitted with little windows, through which he stooped and looked in and saw a corpse that seemed more than human in size, wearing nothing else, but on {{{ 359e }}} its hand a gold ring — he slipped it off and climbed back out. Now when the shepherds held their customary gathering, at which they would report to the king each month about the flocks, he came too, wearing the ring; and as he sat there among the others, he happened to turn the bezel of the ring around toward himself, to the inside of his hand, and when this happened, he {{{ 360a }}} became invisible to those sitting beside him, and they fell to talking as though he had gone. He was amazed, and feeling for the ring again, he turned the bezel back outward, and as he turned it, he became visible once more. Realizing this, he tested the ring to see whether it truly had this power, and found that this was exactly what happened: turning the bezel inward made him unseen, and outward, seen again; and as soon as he grasped this, he at once contrived to become one of the messengers who went up to the king, and once {{{ 360b }}} there, seduced the king's wife, and with her plotted against the king, killed him, and so seized the throne. So if there were two such rings, and the just man put one on and the unjust man the other, no one, it seems, would prove so adamant as to stand firm in justice and bring himself to keep away from what belongs to others and not lay a hand on it — with it in his power to take without fear whatever he liked, even from the marketplace, and {{{ 360c }}} to go into houses and lie with whomever he wanted, and to kill or set free from bonds whomever he wished, and to do everything else among men as though he were a god. Acting this way, he would do nothing different from the other, but both would end up traveling the same road. And yet someone might say this is strong proof that no one is just by choice, but only under compulsion, on the ground that justice is no private good — since wherever each man thinks he will be able to do wrong, he does it. For every man believes that injustice pays him far {{{ 360d }}} better, privately, than justice does — and believes truly, as the one making this argument will say — since if someone got hold of such power and never wished to do wrong, and never touched what belonged to others, he would seem, to those who learned of it, most wretched and most foolish, even though they would praise him in one another's presence, deceiving each other out of fear of being wronged themselves. So much, then, for that.
