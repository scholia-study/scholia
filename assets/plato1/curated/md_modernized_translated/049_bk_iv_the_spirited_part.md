## The Spirited Part

{{{ 439c }}} Quite so, he said.

Shall we say, then, that there are people who, though thirsty, are sometimes unwilling to drink?

Indeed, he said, many people, and often.

What then, I said, would one say about this? Is it not that there is in their soul the thing that bids them drink, and also the thing that forbids them to drink, which is something else and overpowers the thing that bids?

That is my view too, he said.

Is it not the case, then, that the thing forbidding such things arises, whenever it does arise, {{{ 439d }}} from reasoning, while the things that lead and drag us come about through feelings and disorders?

It appears so.

Then it is not without reason, I said, that we shall claim these are two distinct things, different from one another — calling the part with which the soul reasons the rational part, and the part with which it loves and hungers and thirsts and is agitated over the other desires the irrational and appetitive part, companion of certain fillings and pleasures. {{{ 439e }}}

No — we would rightly hold that view, he said.

Let these two forms, then, I said, be marked off for us as present in the soul; but as for the thing belonging to spirit, by which we feel anger, is it a third, or would it share its nature with one of these two?

Perhaps, he said, with the other one, the appetitive.

But, I said, I once heard something, and I trust it: that Leontius, son of Aglaion, coming up from the Piraeus along the outside of the north wall, noticed corpses lying beside the public executioner, and at once he desired to look, but at the same time felt disgust and turned himself away, and for a while he struggled with himself and covered his eyes — {{{ 440a }}} but overpowered at last by the desire, he tore his eyes wide open, ran up to the corpses, and cried, "There you are, you wretched men — take your fill of the beautiful sight."

I too have heard it, he said.

Well, this story, I said, shows that anger sometimes wars against the desires, as one thing against another.

It does show that, he said.

Then do we not also notice, I said, in many other places, whenever desires use force on someone contrary to reason, {{{ 440b }}} that he reproaches himself and grows angry at the thing within him doing the forcing, and that, as though two parties were at odds, his spirit becomes an ally of his reason in such a case? But I think you would say that you have never noticed such a thing happening in yourself — spirit making common cause with the desires when reason has decided that one ought not to resist them — nor, I think, in anyone else either.

No, by Zeus, he said. {{{ 440c }}}

What then, I said, about when someone thinks he is acting unjustly? Is it not the case that the nobler he is, the less he is able to feel anger — even while hungering and suffering cold and enduring anything else of that kind — at someone he believes to be treating him justly, and that, as I say, his spirit is unwilling to be roused against that person?

True, he said.

But what about when someone believes he is being wronged? In that case does he not seethe and grow harsh and fight on the side of what seems just, and, enduring hunger and cold and everything else of that kind, {{{ 440d }}} hold out and prevail, and not give up his noble efforts, until either he succeeds or dies, or is called back and calmed, like a dog by a shepherd, by the reason within him?

Quite so, he said; it does resemble what you are describing — and indeed in our city we set the auxiliaries, like dogs, obedient to the rulers, who are like shepherds of the city.

You understand well, I said, what I want to say. But do you also notice this besides? {{{ 440e }}}

What is that?

That the opposite of what appeared to us just now is turning out to be true about the spirited part. For at that time we thought it was something appetitive, but now we say it is far from that — rather, in the conflict within the soul, it much more takes up arms on the side of the rational part.

Quite so, he said.

Is it, then, something distinct from this too — from the rational part — or is it some form of the rational, so that there are not three but two forms in the soul, the rational and the appetitive? Or, just as in the city three {{{ 441a }}} classes held it together — the money-making, the auxiliary, and the deliberative — so too in the soul is this spirited part a third thing, by nature an ally of the rational part, unless it is corrupted by bad upbringing?

It must be a third, he said.

Yes, I said, provided it turns out to be something other than the rational part, just as it turned out to be different from the appetitive part.

But it is not hard to show, he said; one can see this even in children — that they are full of spirit right from birth, while as for reason, some, it seems to me, never come to share in it at all, {{{ 441b }}} and most only quite late.

By Zeus, yes, I said, well put. And one could see in animals too what you say, that it is so. And besides this, what we said above [there], I believe, will also be confirmed by that verse of Homer's —
+ He struck his chest and rebuked his heart with these words;
for here Homer has plainly represented {{{ 441c }}} the part that had reasoned about the better and the worse as rebuking the part that was raging without reason — the one rebuking the other.
