## Are the Guardians Happy? The Happiness of the Whole

{{{ 419a }}} And Adeimantus broke in: What then, Socrates, will you say in your defense, if someone claims that you are not making these men very happy at all, and this through their own doing — men to whom the city in truth belongs, while they get no good from the city, as others do, who own land and build fine, large houses, and acquire furnishings to suit them, and offer private sacrifices to the gods, and entertain guests, and indeed, as you were just now saying, possess gold and silver and everything that is thought needful for those who are going to be blessed? But plainly, one might say, they seem to sit in {{{ 420a }}} the city like hired auxiliaries, doing nothing but standing guard.

Yes, I said, and what is more, fed at a common table and not even receiving pay beyond their food, as the others do, so that not even if they wish to travel abroad on their own business will it be possible for them, nor to give money to mistresses, nor to spend it wherever else they might wish, in the way those who are thought to be happy spend it. You leave these and many other such things out of the charge.

Well, he said, let these too be added to the charge. {{{ 420b }}}

So what, you are asking, shall we say in our defense?

Yes.

By going along the same road, I said, we shall find, I think, what we ought to say. For we shall say that it would be no wonder if these men were happiest just as they are, but that in founding the city we are not looking to this, that some one class in it should be exceptionally happy, but that the whole city should be so as far as possible. For we thought that in such a city we would most find justice, and again in the worst-governed {{{ 420c }}} one injustice, and that by observing both we could judge the question we have long been seeking. Now then, as we suppose, we are molding the happy city, not marking off a few in it and setting them apart as such, but shaping the whole; and presently we shall examine the opposite kind of city. So then, just as if someone came up to us while we were painting a statue and found fault, saying that we do not apply the most beautiful colors to the most beautiful parts of the creature — for the eyes, the most beautiful part, are painted not with purple but with black — we would seem to answer him reasonably enough {{{ 420d }}} by saying: My good man, do not suppose that we ought to paint eyes so fine that they no longer look like eyes, nor the other parts either, but consider whether, by rendering to each its due, we make the whole beautiful; and so now too, do not force us to fasten on the guardians a happiness of that sort, which {{{ 420e }}} will make them anything rather than guardians. For we know how to dress the farmers in fine robes, deck them with gold, and bid them work the land for pleasure, and lay the potters down by the fire on their right side, drinking and feasting, their wheel set beside them, to make pottery whenever they please, and to make all the rest blessed in the same way, so that the whole city may be happy. But do not advise us in this way; for if we were to obey you, the farmer would no longer {{{ 421a }}} be a farmer, nor the potter a potter, nor would anyone else keep any of the roles out of which a city is made. But the case of the others matters less; for if cobblers turn out badly and are corrupted, and pretend to be what they are not, it is no terrible thing for the city, but when the guardians of the laws and of the city are not truly such but only seem to be, you see that they destroy the whole city root and branch, and, on the other hand, they alone hold the power to govern it well and make it happy. If then we are truly making guardians who do the least harm to the city, while he who says the other thing means some sort of {{{ 421b }}} happy farmers, feasting as if at a festival rather than living in a city, he would be talking about something other than a city. We must consider, then, whether it is with this in view that we are to appoint the guardians, so that the greatest possible happiness will come to be in them, or whether we must look rather to the city as a whole and watch for that happiness there, and must compel and persuade these auxiliaries and the {{{ 421c }}} guardians to do that other thing, so that they will be the best craftsmen of their own work, and all the others likewise, and so, with the whole city growing and being well founded, we must leave each class to partake of happiness in whatever way its nature allows.
