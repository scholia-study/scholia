## The Work of the Soul, and an Inconclusive End

Consider it, then, he said.

I am considering it, I said. Now tell me: do you think a horse has some function? {{{ 352e }}}

I do.

Then would you take this to be the function both of a horse and of anything else — whatever one can do only with that thing, or do best with it?

I don't understand, he said.

Well, put it this way: is there anything else you could see with, besides eyes?

Certainly not.

And what about this? Could you hear with anything other than ears?

Not at all.

Then [might] we not rightly say that these are their functions?

Certainly. {{{ 353a }}}

And what about this? Could you cut a vine-shoot with a knife, or with a chisel, or with many other tools?

Of course.

But not as well, I think, with any of those as with a pruning-hook made for that very purpose.

True.

Then shall we not set this down as its function?

We shall indeed.

Now I think you'll understand better what I was asking just now, when I was inquiring whether the function of a thing is not what it alone can do, or does better than anything else.

Well, he said, I do understand, and it seems to me that this is indeed the function of each {{{ 353b }}} thing.

Very well, I said. Then don't you also think there is a virtue for each thing that has some function assigned to it? Let's go back to the same examples again: do eyes, we say, have some function?

There is one.

Then is there also a virtue of eyes?

There's a virtue too.

And what of this? Did ears have some function?

Yes.

And a virtue as well?

A virtue too.

And what about all the other things? Isn't it the same with them?

It is.

Now hold on: could eyes ever perform their own function well {{{ 353c }}} if they lacked their proper virtue, and had vice instead of virtue?

How could they? So he said — I take it you mean blindness instead of sight.

Whatever their virtue turns out to be, I said — that's not what I'm asking yet, but rather whether the things that do the work will do their own function well by their proper virtue, and badly by vice.

What you say is true, he said.

Then won't ears, too, deprived of their own virtue, perform their own function badly?

Certainly. {{{ 353d }}}

Shall we then set down all the other things under the same account?

I think so.

Come now, after this consider the following. There is a function of the soul that you couldn't accomplish with anything else that exists — something like this: taking care of things, ruling, deliberating, and everything of that sort — is there anything other than the soul to which we could rightly assign these and say they belong to it alone?

To nothing else.

And what about living, in turn? Shall we not say that it is the function of the soul?

Most certainly, he said.

Then don't we also say there is some virtue of the soul?

We do. {{{ 353e }}}

Then will the soul ever perform its own functions well, Thrasymachus, if deprived of its proper virtue, or is that impossible?

Impossible.

Then a bad soul must necessarily rule and take care of things badly, while a good soul does all these things well.

It's necessary.

Then didn't we agree that the virtue of the soul is justice, and its vice injustice?

We did agree to that.

Then the just soul and the just man will live well, and the unjust man badly.

So it appears, he said, according to your argument. {{{ 354a }}}

But surely the one who lives well is blessed and happy, and the one who doesn't is the opposite.

Of course.

Then the just man is happy, and the unjust man is wretched.

Let it be so, he said.

But surely being wretched is not profitable, while being happy is.

Of course.

Then, my blessed Thrasymachus, injustice is never more profitable than justice.

Well then, Socrates, he said, let this be your banquet at the festival of Bendis.

Thanks to you indeed, I said, Thrasymachus, since you've grown gentle with me and given up being harsh. Yet I haven't {{{ 354b }}} feasted well — that's my own fault, not yours; but just like gluttons who snatch a taste of each new dish as it's carried past, before they've properly enjoyed the one before, I seem to have done the same thing myself: before finding what we set out to examine at the start, namely what justice actually is, I let that go and rushed on to consider whether it is vice and ignorance, or wisdom and virtue, and then, when a further argument came up afterward — that injustice is more profitable than justice — I couldn't keep myself from abandoning that question for this one, so that the upshot of the whole discussion, for me, is that I now {{{ 354c }}} know nothing; for since I don't know what justice is, I'm scarcely going to know whether it is in fact some kind of virtue or not, or whether the person who has it is unhappy or happy.
