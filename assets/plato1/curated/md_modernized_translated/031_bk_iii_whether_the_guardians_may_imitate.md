## Whether the Guardians May Imitate

{{{ 394e }}} Now consider this too, Adeimantus: must our guardians be imitative or not — or does this too follow from what we said before, that each single person could practice one occupation well, but not many, and if he tried, in taking hold of many he would fail at all of them, so that he would be nowhere of note?

Why would it not?

Then does not the same argument hold about imitation too — that the same person is not able to imitate many things well, as he can one?

No, indeed not. {{{ 395a }}}

Then it is scarcely likely that the same person could at once practice any worthwhile occupation and also imitate many things and be imitative, since the same people cannot even imitate well at the same time two imitations that seem close to each other — for instance, composing both comedy and tragedy. Or did you not just now call these two imitations?

I did — and you are right that the same people cannot do it.

Nor indeed can the same be rhapsodes and actors at once.

True.

But surely the same people are not actors for both comedy and tragedy either — and all these are {{{ 395b }}} imitations. Or are they not?

They are imitations.

And beyond these, Adeimantus, it seems to me that human nature is chopped up into even smaller pieces than these, so that it is unable to imitate many things well, or to do the very things themselves of which the imitations are likenesses.

Very true, he said.

If then we are to preserve our first principle — that our guardians, released from all other crafts, must be exact craftsmen of {{{ 395c }}} the city's freedom and practice nothing else that does not bear on this — then surely they ought to do nothing else, nor imitate anything else; but if they do imitate, they should imitate, right from childhood, what is fitting for them: the brave, the moderate, the pious, the free, and everything of that kind, while doing none of what is unfree, nor being clever at imitating it, nor anything else shameful, lest from the imitation {{{ 395d }}} they come to reap the reality itself. Or have you not noticed that imitations, if they are kept up from youth far into life, settle into habits and nature, in body, voice, and mind alike?

Indeed I have, he said.

We will not, then, permit those we say we care for, who must become good men, to imitate — men though they are — a woman, young or old, reviling her husband, or quarreling with the gods and vaunting herself against them, thinking herself {{{ 395e }}} happy, or in the grip of misfortunes and griefs and dirges; and as for a woman who is sick, or in love, or in labor, we shall be very far indeed from allowing it.

Quite so, he said.

Nor indeed female or male slaves doing what is fit for slaves.

No, not that either.

Nor, it seems, bad men either — cowardly, doing the opposite of what we just described, reviling and mocking one another, speaking foully, drunk or {{{ 396a }}} sober, or whatever else such people do wrong in word and deed, against themselves and others; and I think they should not even be trained to make themselves like madmen in word or deed, for they must know both mad and wicked men and women, but must do none of these things, nor imitate them.

Very true, he said.

What about this? I said — must they imitate men forging bronze, {{{ 396b }}} or practicing some other craft, or rowing triremes, or calling out orders to the rowers, or anything else connected with such things?

And how could they? These are men, he said, who will not even be allowed to pay attention to any of it?

What about this? Will they imitate whinnying horses, and bellowing bulls, and roaring rivers, and crashing sea, and thunder, and all such things again?

But it is forbidden them, he said, either to be mad or to liken themselves to the mad.

If then, I said, I understand what you mean, there is a certain kind of diction and narration in which the truly good and noble man would narrate, whenever he needed to say something, and another {{{ 396c }}} kind again, unlike this one, to which the man of opposite birth and upbringing would always keep, and in which he would narrate.

What are these, then, he said?

The moderate man, I said, it seems to me, whenever he comes in his narration to some speech or action of a good man, will be willing to report it as if he himself were that man, and not be ashamed of such imitation — imitating, above all, the good man acting {{{ 396d }}} safely and sensibly, but doing so less, and less willingly, when that man has been thrown off balance by sickness or love, or by drunkenness, or by some other misfortune; and whenever he comes upon someone unworthy of himself, he will not be willing to liken himself in earnest to the worse man, except perhaps briefly, when he does something good — but he will be ashamed, both because he is untrained at imitating such people, and because he is loath to mold and set {{{ 396e }}} himself into the patterns of worse men, holding it in contempt in his thought, except for the sake of play.

That's likely, he said.

Then he will use the kind of narration we went through a little earlier concerning Homer's verses, and his diction will partake of both — imitation and the rest of narration — but with only a small portion of imitation in a long stretch of speech? Or am I talking nonsense?

Quite so, he said — that must indeed be the pattern for such a speaker. {{{ 397a }}}

Then, I said, the man who is not like this, on the other hand — the more inferior he is, the more he will narrate everything, and think nothing beneath him, so that he will attempt to imitate everything in earnest and before a large audience: the things we just mentioned, thunder and the noise of winds and hail, and axles and pulleys, and the sounds of trumpets and flutes and pipes and every instrument, and further the cries of dogs and sheep and birds; {{{ 397b }}} and will not the whole of his diction then consist of imitation, in voice and gesture, with only a small part of it narration?

That too is necessary, he said.

These, then, I said, are the two kinds of diction I meant.

Yes, they are, he said.

Then one of the two has small variations, and if someone assigns a fitting harmony and rhythm to the diction, one who speaks it correctly comes very near to speaking throughout in the same way, and in a single harmony — for the variations are small — and indeed also in {{{ 397c }}} some correspondingly similar rhythm?

Quite so, he said, that is how it is.

But what of the kind belonging to the other? Does it not need the opposite — all the harmonies and all the rhythms — if it is in turn to be spoken fittingly, because it has variations of every sort of shape?

Very much so — that is how it is.

Do all the poets, then, and all those who say anything, hit upon one or the other of these two types of diction, or upon some blend mixed from both?

Necessarily, he said. {{{ 397d }}}

What then shall we do? I said — shall we admit all of these into our city, or one of the unmixed types, or the mixed one?

If my view prevails, he said, the unmixed imitator of the decent man.

But indeed, Adeimantus, the mixed one is pleasant too, and by far the most pleasant to children and their attendants is the type opposite to the one you choose, and to the great mass of people.

Yes, for it is the most pleasant.

But perhaps, I said, you would say it does not fit our {{{ 397e }}} constitution, because there is no double man among us, nor a manifold one, since each person does one thing.

No, indeed it does not fit.

Is it not for this reason that only in such a city will we find the shoemaker a shoemaker and not also a pilot besides his shoemaking, and the farmer a farmer and not also a judge besides his farming, and the soldier a soldier and not also a money-maker besides his soldiering, and everyone the same way?

True, he said. {{{ 398a }}}

So a man, it seems, capable through his skill of becoming all things and imitating every kind of thing, if he should come to our city wanting to display himself and his poems, we would bow down to him as someone sacred, wonderful, and pleasing, but we would tell him that there is no such man in our city, nor is it right that there should be, and we would send him off to another city, pouring myrrh over his head and crowning him with wool, while we ourselves would make use of the more austere and less pleasing poet {{{ 398b }}} and storyteller, for the sake of benefit, the one who would imitate for us the diction of the decent man and say what is said in those patterns which we laid down at the start, when we set about educating our soldiers.

Indeed, he said, that is what we would do, if it were up to us.

Now then, my friend, I said, the part of our music concerning speech and stories has, it seems, been fully completed for us; for both what must be said and how it must be said have been stated.

It seems so to me too, he said.
