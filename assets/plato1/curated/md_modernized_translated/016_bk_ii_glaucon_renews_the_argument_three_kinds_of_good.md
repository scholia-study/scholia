## Glaucon Renews the Argument: Three Kinds of Good

{{{ 357a }}} Now when I had said this, I thought I was done with the argument; but that was, it seems, only a prelude. For Glaucon, as it happens, is always the most courageous of men in every matter, and on that occasion too he would not accept Thrasymachus's withdrawal, but said: Socrates, do you want merely to seem to have persuaded us, or {{{ 357b }}} truly to persuade us, that it is in every way better to be just than unjust?

Truly, I said, I would choose that, if it were up to me.

Then you are not doing what you want, he said. Tell me this: does it seem to you that there is a good of this sort — one we would want to have not because we desire its consequences, but because we welcome it for its own sake, such as feeling joy, and all the pleasures that are harmless, and because of which nothing else comes about in the time that follows, beyond the enjoyment of having them?

Yes, I said, it seems to me that there is such a good. {{{ 357c }}}

What of this? Is there a good that we cherish both for its own sake and for what comes from it — such as thinking, seeing, and being healthy? For we welcome things like these, I suppose, on both counts.

Yes, I said.

And do you see a third kind of good as well, he said, which includes exercising, being treated when sick, the practice of medicine itself, and the other ways of making money? We would call these things burdensome, yet beneficial to us; and we would not choose to have them for their own sake, but {{{ 357d }}} for the sake of the wages and whatever else comes from them.

Yes, I said, this too is a third kind. But why do you ask?

In which of these, he said, do you place justice? {{{ 358a }}}

I myself think, I said, in the finest kind — the one that anyone who is to be happy must love both for itself and for what comes from it.

That is not, however, he said, what most people think; rather, they place it in the burdensome kind, the sort that must be pursued for the sake of wages and the good standing that a reputation brings, but must be avoided for its own sake as being harsh.

I know, I said, that this is how it seems, and that Thrasymachus has long been finding fault with justice on this ground, and praising injustice instead; but I, it seems, am somewhat slow to learn. {{{ 358b }}}

Come then, he said, hear me out as well, and see whether you still think the same. For Thrasymachus seems to me to have been charmed by you, like a snake, sooner than he should have been; but the proof about each of the two has not yet come out to my satisfaction — I want to hear what each of them is, and what power it has, by itself, when present in the soul, and to let the wages and their consequences go. So this is what I will do, if it suits you too: I will take up {{{ 358c }}} Thrasymachus's argument again — first I will state what people say justice is and where it comes from, second that all who practice it do so unwillingly, as something necessary rather than good, and third that they have good reason to act so, since the life of the unjust is, as they say, far better than that of the just. Since to me, at least, Socrates, that does not seem so at all — yet I am at a loss, my ears worn out from listening to Thrasymachus and countless others, for I have never yet heard from anyone the argument on behalf of justice, that it is {{{ 358d }}} better than injustice, in the way that I want to hear it — I want to hear it praised in and of itself — and I think I could learn this best of all from you. That is why I will strain every nerve to speak in praise of the unjust life, and once I have spoken, I will show you the manner in which I want, in turn, to hear you as well, blaming injustice and praising justice. But see whether what I say suits you.

More than anything, I said — for what topic would a sensible person rather speak and hear about, again and again, than this?
