## The Principle of Opposites: Reason and Appetite

So it seems to me too, he said.

Let us then try to define them this way, whether they are the same as one another or different.

How?

Clearly the same thing will not be willing to do or undergo opposites in the same respect and in relation to the same thing at the same time, so that if we ever find this happening in them, we will know that it was not one thing {{{ 436c }}} but more than one.

Very well.

Consider, then, what I mean.

Speak on, he said.

Is it possible, I said, for the same thing to stand still and be in motion at the same time in the same respect?

In no way.

Let us then agree still more precisely, so that we do not fall into dispute as we go on. For if someone were to say of a man standing still, but moving his hands and his head, that the same man both stands still and moves at the same time, I do not think we would consider it right to speak {{{ 436d }}} that way, but rather that part of him stands still and part moves. Is that not so?

Just so.

Then if the man who said this were to be wittier still, refining the point by saying that spinning tops — whole ones — both stand still and move at the same time, whenever they fix the peg in one spot and turn around it, or that anything else going around in a circle in the same place does the same, we would not accept it, on the ground that such things are not then at rest and in motion in the same respect {{{ 436e }}} of themselves; rather we would say that they have a straight element and a curved element in them, and that with respect to the straight they stand still — for they do not lean in any direction — while with respect to the curved they move in a circle, and that when, as it turns, the straight axis leans to the right or to the left or forward or back, then there [is] no standing still for it at all.

And rightly so, he said.

Then none of these sayings will astonish us, nor persuade us any more that a thing that is the same could ever undergo or be or do opposites at {{{ 437a }}} the same time in the same respect in relation to the same thing.

Not me, at any rate, he said.

Still, I said, so that we are not forced to draw this out by going through every such dispute and establishing that it is not true, let us assume that this is so and proceed on that basis, agreeing that if it should ever appear otherwise, everything that follows from it for us will be undone.

We must do that, he said. {{{ 437b }}}

Then ⟨would⟩ you set nodding assent against shaking the head, and reaching to take something against refusing it, and drawing something toward oneself against pushing it away — all such pairs — among things that are opposites of one another, whether as things done or things undergone? For it will make no difference here.

Opposites, he said.

What then? I said — thirst and hunger, and desires as a whole, and again wishing and wanting, would you not put all these {{{ 437c }}} somewhere among those forms just now mentioned? For instance, will you not say that the soul of one who desires always either reaches out for the thing it desires, or draws toward itself what it wishes to have come to be, or else, to the extent that it wants something provided for it, nods assent to this within itself, as though someone were asking it a question, straining after that thing's coming to be?

I would.

And what of this? Shall we not place unwillingness, and not wishing, and not desiring, under pushing away and driving off from oneself, and among all the opposites of those other things {{{ 437d }}}?

How could we not?

Since this is so, shall we say that there is a form of desires, and that among these the clearest are the ones we call thirst and hunger?

We shall, he said.

One of drink, then, and the other of food?

Yes.

Then, to the extent that it is thirst, would it be, in the soul, a desire for anything more than that of which we say it is the desire — is thirst, say, thirst for hot drink or cold, for much or little, or in a word, for a drink of some particular kind? Or is it rather that if some heat attaches itself to the {{{ 437e }}} thirst, it will supply along with it a desire for something cold, and if coldness attaches itself, a desire for something hot? And if, because a great quantity is present, the thirst is great, will it supply a desire for a great quantity, and if it is slight, a desire for a small quantity? But thirst itself will never become a desire for anything other than that for which it is by nature a desire, namely drink itself, and hunger in turn a desire for food itself?

Yes, he said, each desire itself is a desire only for that particular thing which it is by nature a desire for; the desire for this kind or that kind is something added on. {{{ 438a }}}

Now let no one confound us, since we have not examined this carefully, by objecting that no one desires drink, but good drink, nor food, but good food. For everyone, they will say, desires good things; so if thirst is a desire, it must be a desire for something good, whether drink or whatever else it is a desire for, and likewise with the other desires.

Yes, he said, perhaps the one saying this would seem to have a point.

But surely, I said, of things that are the sort to be {{{ 438b }}} relative to something, those that are qualified in some way are relative to something qualified in that same way, as it seems to me, while those that are simply what they are, are relative only to a thing that is simply what it is.

I do not understand, he said.

Do you not understand, I said, that the greater is such as to be greater than something?

Certainly.

Greater than the lesser?

Yes.

And the much greater is greater than the much lesser. Is that not so?

Yes.

And what was once greater is greater than what was once lesser, and what will be greater is greater than what will be lesser?

What else would it be? So he said. {{{ 438c }}}

And more, then, in relation to less, and double in relation to half, and all such pairs, and again heavier in relation to lighter, and faster in relation to slower, and further still, hot in relation to cold, and everything like these — is it not the same in all these cases?

Certainly it is.

And what about matters concerning knowledge? Is it not the same way? Knowledge itself is knowledge of a subject of learning itself, or of whatever else we must posit as the object of knowledge, while a particular kind of knowledge, qualified in some way, is knowledge {{{ 438d }}} of something qualified in some way and of some particular thing. I mean something like this: was it not because a knowledge of house-construction came into being that it differed from the other branches of knowledge, so that it came to be called the craft of building?

What else?

Is it not by its being of a particular kind, one shared by none of the others?

Yes.

Then, since it was of something qualified in a particular way, did it not itself become qualified in a particular way? And so too the other crafts and branches of knowledge?

That is how it is.

This, then, I said, is what you should take me to have meant back then, if indeed you now understand it — that whatever is of such a nature as to be relative to something is, in itself, relative only to a thing that is itself alone, while what is qualified in some way is relative to something qualified in some way. {{{ 438e }}} And I do not mean that things turn out to be of the same kind as that to which they are relative — as though, say, the knowledge of the healthy and the diseased is itself healthy and diseased, or the knowledge of bad things and good things is itself bad and good; rather, since knowledge did not become knowledge of the very thing of which it is knowledge, but of something qualified in a particular way — and this was the healthy and the diseased — it too came to be qualified in a particular way, and this is what caused it to no longer be called simply knowledge, but, once that particular quality was added, medicine.

I understand, he said, and it seems to me to be so. {{{ 439a }}}

And thirst, I said — will you not place it among these, as being, in respect of what it itself is, a thirst of something? And it is, presumably, thirst of—

I do, he said — of drink, at least.

Then thirst for a drink of a particular kind is a particular kind of thirst, but thirst itself is by nature a thirst neither for much nor for little, neither for good nor for bad, nor in a word for a drink of any particular kind, but simply for drink itself?

Absolutely.

Then the soul of the thirsty person, insofar as it thirsts, {{{ 439b }}} wants nothing other than to drink, and reaches for this, and is set in motion toward this.

Clearly so.

Then if something ever pulls it back when it is thirsty, would there not be something else in it, different from the very thing that thirsts and drives it, like an animal, toward drinking? For surely, we say, the very same thing ⟨would⟩ never do opposite things to itself, by the same part of itself, in the same respect, at the same time.

No, it would not.

Just as, I think, in the case of the archer, it is not correct to say that his hands at the same time push the bow away and draw it toward him, but rather that one hand is the one pushing it away, and the other is the one drawing it toward him.
