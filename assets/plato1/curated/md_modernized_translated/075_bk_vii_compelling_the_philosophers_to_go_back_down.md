## Compelling the Philosophers to Go Back Down

Likely enough, he said.

What then? Isn't this likely, I said, and indeed necessary from what we've said before: that neither the uneducated, who have no experience of truth, {{{ 519c }}} could ever adequately govern a city, nor those who are allowed to spend their whole lives in education — the first because they have no single mark in life at which they must aim in doing everything they do, in private and in public, and the second because they will not act willingly, believing that while still alive they have already settled in the Isles of the Blessed?

True, he said.

It is our task, then, I said, as founders, to compel the best natures to arrive at the study which we said before was the greatest, to see the good {{{ 519d }}} and to make that ascent, and once they have ascended and seen enough, not to allow them what is now allowed them.

What is that?

To remain there, I said, and not be willing to go back down again to those prisoners, nor to share in the labors and honors among them, whether these be of less or of greater account.

Then, he said, shall we be doing them an injustice, making them live worse when a better life is possible for them? {{{ 519e }}}

You're forgetting again, my friend, I said, that the law's concern is not that any one class in a city should fare exceptionally well, but that this should come about in the city as a whole — fitting the citizens together by persuasion and by compulsion, making them share {{{ 520a }}} with one another whatever benefit each is able to confer on the community, and itself producing such men in the city, not so as to let each turn wherever he wishes, but so as to make full use of them for binding the city together.

True, he said — for I had forgotten.

Consider then, Glaucon, I said, that we will not be doing an injustice to the philosophers who arise among us either, but will be saying just things to them, when we compel them to care for the others as well and to guard them. For we will say that those who become such in other {{{ 520b }}} cities reasonably take no part in the labors within them, since they spring up of their own accord, against the will of the constitution under which each grows; and it is just that what grows of itself, owing nourishment to no one, should not be eager to pay anyone back the price of its upbringing; but you we have begotten, for yourselves and for the rest of the city alike, as leaders and kings, as though in a hive of bees, educated better and more completely than those others, and better able {{{ 520c }}} to take part in both. Each of you, then, must go down in turn to dwell together with the others, and grow accustomed to observing the things in the dark; for once accustomed, you will see a thousand times better than those there, and will know what each of the images is, and of what, through having seen the truth about beautiful things and just things and good things. And so the city will be governed by us and by you in waking reality and not in a dream, unlike most cities today, which are governed by men shadow-fighting against one another and quarreling over ruling {{{ 520d }}} as though it were some great good. But the truth, I think, is something like this: in the city where those who are to rule are least eager to rule, that city must of necessity be governed best and with the least civil strife, while the one that gets the opposite kind of rulers is governed in the opposite way.

Certainly, he said.

Do you think, then, that our nurslings will disobey us when they hear this, and will be unwilling each to do their share of the work in the city in turn, wishing instead to spend the greater part of their time dwelling with one another in the pure region? {{{ 520e }}}

Impossible, he said — for we will be laying just commands on just men. Beyond all doubt, each of them will go to rule as to something necessary, the very opposite of those who now rule in each city.

That is how it stands, my friend, I said: if you can discover {{{ 521a }}} a life better than ruling for those who are going to rule, then a well-governed city is possible for you; for only in that city will the truly rich rule — rich not in gold, but in what the happy person must be rich in, a good and sensible life. But if men who are poor and hungry for private goods go into public affairs, thinking that from there they must seize the good, it is not possible; for then ruling becomes something fought over, and such a war, being internal and domestic, destroys both them and the rest of the city.

Very true, he said. {{{ 521b }}}

Do you know of any other life, then, I said, that looks down on political offices besides that of true philosophy?

No, by Zeus, he said.

And yet those who go to it must not be lovers of ruling — otherwise the rival lovers will fight over it.

How could it be otherwise?

Whom else, then, will you compel to go to the guardianship of the city, except those who have the most practical wisdom about the things through which a city is governed best, and who have other honors and a better life than the political one?

No others, he said.
