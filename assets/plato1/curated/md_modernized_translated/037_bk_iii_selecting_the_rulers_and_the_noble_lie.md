## Selecting the Rulers, and the Noble Lie

{{{ 412b }}} It will indeed be necessary, so far as that is at all possible.

These, then, would be the outlines of education and upbringing. For why should one go through the particulars of such things — dances, and hunting and the chase, and gymnastic and equestrian contests? For it is more or less clear that these must follow from what has been said, and are no longer hard to discover.

Perhaps not hard, he said.

Well then, I said — what ought we to distinguish next? Is it not the question of these same men themselves — which of them shall rule, and which be ruled? {{{ 412c }}}

Of course.

Is it not clear that the rulers must be older, and the ruled younger?

Clear.

And clear too that the rulers must be the best of them?

That too.

And are not the best farmers the ones most skilled at farming?

Yes.

So now, since they must be the best of the guardians, must they not be the ones most skilled at guarding the city?

Yes.

Must they not then be sensible and capable for this, and moreover devoted to the care of the city? {{{ 412d }}}

That is so.

And a person would care most for that which he happens to love.

Necessarily.

And indeed he would love most that whose interest he believed to be the same as his own, [especially] when he thought that if it fared well he himself fared well too, and if not, the opposite.

So it is, he said.

We must therefore choose, from among the other guardians, such men as appear to us, on the closest observation, to hold to this above all throughout their whole {{{ 412e }}} life: whatever they judge to benefit the city they do with all eagerness, and whatever they judge not to, they are unwilling to do by any means.

Yes, for such men would be fit for it, he said.

It seems to me that we must watch them at every stage of life, to see whether they remain guardians of this conviction, and are not, whether bewitched or forced, made to cast out and forget the opinion that one must do what is best for the city.

What kind of casting-out do you mean, he said?

I will tell you, I said. It seems to me that an opinion leaves the mind either willingly or unwillingly — willingly in the case of the false {{{ 413a }}} opinion of one changing his mind, and unwillingly always in the case of the true one.

The willing kind, he said, I understand; but I need to learn about the unwilling kind.

What then? Do you not yourself believe, I said, that men are deprived of good things unwillingly, but of bad things willingly? Or is not being deceived about the truth a bad thing, and holding to the truth a good one? Or does it not seem to you that holding an opinion of things as they are is the same as holding to the truth?

You are right, he said, and it seems to me that men are deprived of a true opinion unwillingly. {{{ 413b }}}

Then is it not by being robbed, or bewitched, or forced, that this happens to them?

I do not understand even now, he said.

I am in danger of speaking like a tragedian, I said. For those who are talked out of it, and those who forget, I call robbed — since from the one, time takes it away unnoticed, and from the other, argument does; surely you understand that now?

Yes.

And those whom some pain or distress makes change their opinion, I call forced.

That too I understand, he said, and you are right. {{{ 413c }}}

And bewitched, I think, you too would call those who change their opinion, either charmed by pleasure or scared by some fear.

Yes, he said, everything that deceives seems to bewitch.

So then, as I was just saying, we must look for whoever are the best guardians of the conviction within them: that whatever they always judge to be best for the city must be done [by them]. We must watch them, then, right from childhood, setting them tasks in which one would be most likely to forget such a thing, or to be deceived out of it, and we must approve the one who remembers {{{ 413d }}} and is hard to deceive, and reject the one who is not. Is that not so?

Yes.

And we must set them, in turn, labors and pains and contests, in which the same things must be watched.

Right, he said.

Then, I said, we must also set up for them a third kind of contest, one of bewitchment, and observe them — just as men lead colts amid noises and uproar to see whether they are fearful, so must young men be led into certain terrors and then, in turn, {{{ 413e }}} changed over into pleasures, testing them far more thoroughly than gold is tested in fire — to see whether each shows himself hard to bewitch and graceful in everything, a good guardian of himself and of the music he was taught, keeping himself rhythmic and well-attuned throughout all these trials, being of such a kind as would be most useful both to himself and to the city. And whoever is tested throughout — as a boy, as a young man, and as a man — {{{ 414a }}} and comes through always unspoiled, must be established as a ruler of the city and a guardian, and must be given honors both in life and in death, receiving the greatest privileges in burial and in other memorials; but whoever is not of this kind must be rejected. Something of this kind, Glaucon, I said, seems to me to be the selection and establishment of the rulers and guardians — spoken of, that is, in outline, and not with precision.

It appears to me that way too, he said. {{{ 414b }}}

Then is it truly most correct to call these men complete guardians, against enemies from without and against friends within — so that the one sort will have no wish, and the other no power, to do wrong — and to call the young men, whom we have until now been calling guardians, auxiliaries and helpers to the convictions of the rulers?

It seems so to me, he said.

What device, then, I said, might we find — among those useful falsehoods we were just now speaking of, told when they are needed — by which we might tell one noble {{{ 414c }}} falsehood and persuade, best of all, the rulers themselves, or failing that, the rest of the city?

What sort? He asked.

Nothing new, I said, but something Phoenician — a thing that has already happened in many places before now, as the poets tell us and have persuaded people, though it has not happened in our own time, and I do not know whether it could happen, and it would take no small persuasion to bring anyone to believe it.

You seem, he said, reluctant to tell it.

And you will think, I said, that I am reluctant with very good reason, once I have told it.

Tell it, he said, and do not be afraid. {{{ 414d }}}

I will tell it then — though I do not know with what daring or what words I shall say it — and I will try to persuade, first, the rulers themselves and the soldiers, and then the rest of the city as well, that all this upbringing and education we gave them was, as it were, something they only dreamed was happening to them and around them, while in truth, at that time, they were being formed and reared beneath the earth, they themselves and their {{{ 414e }}} weapons and the rest of their equipment being fashioned there too, and that when they were completely finished, the earth, being their mother, sent them up, and that now they must think of the land in which they live as a mother and a nurse, and take counsel for it and defend it, should anyone attack it, and must think of the rest of the citizens as their brothers, born of the same earth.

No wonder, he said, that you were so long ashamed to tell the falsehood. {{{ 415a }}}

Quite rightly so, I said; but all the same, hear the rest of the story too. You are all brothers, all of you in the city, we shall say to them, telling the story further — but the god, in forming you, mixed gold at birth into those of you who are fit to rule, and that is why they are the most honored; silver into the auxiliaries; and iron and bronze into the farmers and the other craftsmen. Now since you are all kindred, for the most part you will beget children like yourselves, {{{ 415b }}} but sometimes a silver child will be born from a golden parent, or a golden child from a silver one, and likewise all the rest from one another. So the god commands the rulers, first and above all, that of nothing shall they be such good guardians, and nothing shall they guard so closely, as their offspring, watching for whichever of these metals is mixed into their souls, and if a child of their own should be born with an admixture of bronze or iron, in no {{{ 415c }}} way shall they show it mercy, but shall give it the honor proper to its nature and thrust it out among the craftsmen or the farmers; and if, in turn, a child should be born of these with an admixture of gold or silver, they shall honor it and raise it up, some into the guard, others into the auxiliary force, since there is an oracle that the city will be destroyed whenever it is guarded by a man of iron or of bronze. Do you have any device, then, by which they might be persuaded of this story?
