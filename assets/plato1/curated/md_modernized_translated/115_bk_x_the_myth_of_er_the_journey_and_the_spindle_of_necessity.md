## The Myth of Er: The Journey, and the Spindle of Necessity

{{{ 614b }}} Do go on, he said, since there is not much else I would hear more gladly.

It is not, though, a tale of Alcinous that I shall tell you, I said, but the tale of a valiant man, Er son of Armenius, a Pamphylian by birth, who once died in war; when the dead were taken up on the tenth day, already decayed, he was taken up sound, and, carried home and about to be buried on the twelfth day, he came back to life as he lay on the pyre, and once revived he told what he had seen there. He said that when his soul went out of him it traveled {{{ 614c }}} with many others, and they came to a certain supernatural place, in which there were two chasms in the earth, adjoining each other, and, opposite them, two others in the heaven above. Judges sat between them, and these, whenever they had given judgment, ordered the just to travel the road to the right and upward through the heaven, with signs of the judgments passed fastened on them in front, and the unjust the road to the left and downward, these too wearing on their backs signs of everything they {{{ 614d }}} had done. And when he approached, they told him that he must become a messenger to mankind of the things there, and they urged him to listen and to observe everything in the place. He saw, then, at each of those two chasms — the one of heaven and the one of earth — souls departing once judgment had been given for them, and, at the other two, from one, souls going up out of the earth, full of squalor and dust, and from the other, different souls coming down pure from {{{ 614e }}} heaven. And the souls that kept arriving seemed to have come as if from a long journey, and, going off gladly into the meadow, camped there as though at a festival, and those who knew one another greeted each other, and the ones who had come from the earth asked the others about the things there, and those from heaven about what had befallen the others. And they told each other their stories: {{{ 615a }}} the ones from the earth, wailing and weeping, recalling how many things and what things they had suffered and seen in their journey beneath the earth — the journey lasted a thousand years — while the ones from heaven, in turn, told of delights and of sights of inconceivable beauty. Now to relate most of it, Glaucon, would take a long time; but he said the main point was this: for all the wrongs anyone had ever done, and to however many people, each had paid the penalty for all of them in turn, tenfold for each — this being reckoned {{{ 615b }}} by each period of a hundred years, human life being taken as that length — so that they might pay tenfold requital for the wrong, and, for instance, if some had been the cause of many deaths, either by betraying cities or armies, and had thrown men into slavery, or had been accessories to some other affliction, for all of these they received tenfold pains for each, and again, if they had done good deeds and had been just and pious, they received their due reward in the same {{{ 615c }}} proportion. And concerning those who had just been born and lived only a short time, he told other things not worth mentioning. And for impiety and piety toward the gods, and toward parents, and for murder by one's own hand, he described still greater recompenses.

For he said that he was present when one was asked by another where Ardiaeus the Great was. Now this Ardiaeus had been tyrant in a certain city of Pamphylia, a thousand years already before that time, having killed his aged father {{{ 615d }}} and his elder brother, and having done many other unholy things besides, as it was said. He said, then, that the one who was asked replied: "He has not come, nor will he come here. For indeed we witnessed this too, one of the terrible sights: when we were near the mouth, about to go up, and had gone through everything else as well, we suddenly caught sight of him and others — nearly all of them tyrants, though there were also some private men who {{{ 615e }}} had committed great wrongs — whom, though they thought they were now about to go up, the mouth would not receive, but it bellowed whenever one of those so incurably disposed toward wickedness, or who had not paid a sufficient penalty, tried to go up. At that point, he said, savage men, fiery to look at, standing by and recognizing the sound, seized some and led them off, and, having bound Ardiaeus and others {{{ 616a }}} hand and foot and head, they threw them down and flayed them, and dragged them out along the road, tearing them on thorn bushes, and explaining to all who passed by why this was done, and that they were being led away to be cast into Tartarus. There, then, he said, though many and varied fears had come upon us, this one surpassed the rest — each man's fear that the sound would come for him as he was going up — and each went up most gladly when it stayed silent." And such, then, were the judgments and {{{ 616b }}} punishments, and the benefits, in turn, corresponded to these. And when seven days had passed for each of them in the meadow, they had to rise up from there and set out on the eighth day, and arrive on the fourth day at a place from which they could see, from above, a straight light stretched through the whole of heaven and earth, like a column, most closely resembling the rainbow, but brighter and purer; and they reached it after advancing a day's journey, and saw there, at {{{ 616c }}} the middle of the light, the ends of its bonds stretched down from heaven — for this light was the bond of heaven, like the undergirding cables of triremes, holding its whole revolution together in this way — and from the ends stretched the spindle of Necessity, through which all the revolutions turned; its shaft and its hook were made of adamant, and the whorl was a mixture of this and other kinds of material. The {{{ 616d }}} nature of the whorl was as follows: its shape was like that of the ones here, but one must understand, from what he said, that it was of this kind — as if, within one great whorl, hollow and scooped out all the way through, another such whorl, smaller, lay fitted inside it, like buckets that fit into one another, and so, too, a third besides, and a fourth, and four others. For the whorls were eight in all, lying one within another, {{{ 616e }}} showing their rims as circles from above, and forming, around the shaft, the continuous surface of a single whorl; and the shaft itself was driven right through the middle of the eighth. Now the first and outermost whorl had the widest circle of rim; the second widest was that of the sixth; third was that of the fourth; fourth was that of the eighth; fifth was that of the seventh; sixth was that of the fifth; seventh was that of the third; and eighth was that of the second.
