## Second Proof: Three Parts, Three Pleasures, Three Lives

What is this?

Since, I said, just as a city is divided into three kinds, so too the soul of each individual is threefold — [the rational part] — and, as it seems to me, will also admit of another proof.

Which proof do you mean?

This one. Since there are three parts, three pleasures too appear to me to exist, one peculiar to each; and likewise desires and ruling principles.

How do you mean? He said.

The one, we say, was that by which a person learns, the other that by which he feels anger, and the third — because of its many forms — we had no single name of its own to call it {{{ 580e }}} by, but named it after what was greatest and strongest in it; for we have called it appetitive, because of the intensity of its desires for food and drink and sex and whatever else follows on these, and indeed money-loving too, since it is chiefly by means of money {{{ 581a }}} that such desires are fulfilled.

And rightly so, he said.

So then, if we should say that its pleasure and its love too are for gain, would we best fix it under one heading in our account, so as to make something clear to ourselves whenever we speak of this part of the soul, and would we be right to call it money-loving and gain-loving?

So it seems to me at least, he said.

What next? Do we not say that the spirited part is wholly and always set upon mastering and conquering and winning good repute {{{ 581b }}}?

Very much so.

If then we were to call it victory-loving and honor-loving, would that be fitting?

Most fitting indeed.

But surely, as for the part by which we learn, it is clear to everyone that it is always wholly bent on knowing the truth, whatever its nature, and that of money and reputation this part cares least of all.

Very much indeed.

Then we would be naming it aptly if we called it learning-loving and philosophic?

How could we not?

Then, I said, in the souls of some {{{ 581c }}} this part rules, and in the souls of others one or the other of the remaining two, whichever it happens to be?

So it is, he said.

Is it for this reason then that we say there are three primary kinds of people: philosophic, victory-loving, and gain-loving?

Quite so.

And three forms of pleasure too, one underlying each of these?

Certainly.

Do you know then, I said, that if you were willing to ask each of three such people in turn which of these lives is pleasantest, each would praise his own most highly? And the money-maker {{{ 581d }}} will say that, set beside the pleasure of gain, the pleasure of being honored or that of learning is worth nothing, unless either of them actually brings in money?

True, he said.

But what of the honor-lover? I said — does he not think the pleasure from money something vulgar, and in turn the pleasure from learning, unless the study brings honor, mere smoke and nonsense?

That is how it is, he said.

But the philosopher, I said, what do we think he reckons the other {{{ 581e }}} pleasures worth, compared with that of knowing the truth however it stands, and of always being engaged in learning something of that kind? Very far from [pleasure], would he not say? And does he not truly call them necessary, since he would have no need of the rest, were there no necessity?

Yes, he said, one must know that well, must one not?

Since, then, I said, the pleasures of each kind and life itself are disputed — not merely with regard to living more nobly or basely, nor more badly or better, but as to which is actually {{{ 582a }}} the more pleasant and the less painful — how could we know which of them speaks most truly?

I myself am not at all able to say, he said.

But look at it this way: by what must things be judged, if they are to be judged well? Is it not by experience and practical wisdom and argument? Or could one find a better criterion than these?

And how could there be one? He said.

Consider, then: of the three men, which is most experienced in all the pleasures we have mentioned? Does the gain-lover, in learning what the truth itself is like, seem to you more experienced in the pleasure {{{ 582b }}} that comes from knowing, than the philosopher is in the pleasure that comes from gaining?

There is a great difference, he said. For the one, starting from childhood, has necessarily tasted the other pleasures; but the gain-lover, though he learns the nature of the things that are, is under no necessity to taste this pleasure and know how sweet it is, or to become experienced in it — rather, even if he were eager, it would not be easy.

The philosopher, then, I said, differs greatly from the gain-lover in experience of both pleasures {{{ 582c }}}.

Very much so indeed.

And what of the honor-lover? Is the philosopher any more inexperienced in the pleasure that comes from being honored than that man is in the pleasure that comes from exercising practical wisdom?

But honor, he said, follows all of them alike, provided each accomplishes what he has set out to do — for the rich man too is honored by many, and so is the courageous man, and the wise man — so that, from being honored at least, all of them are experienced in what that pleasure is like; but of the vision of what truly is, and what pleasure it holds, none but the philosopher can possibly have tasted {{{ 582d }}}.

So, as far as experience goes, I said, he is the best judge among these men.

By far.

And moreover, he alone will have come to be experienced together with practical wisdom.

Why would it not be so?

Moreover, the instrument by which judgment must be made is not the instrument of the gain-lover nor of the honor-lover, but of the philosopher.

Which one?

We said, I think, that judgment must be made through argument. Did we not?

Yes.

And argument is above all his instrument.

How could it not be?

So then, if the matters {{{ 582e }}} in question were best judged by wealth and gain, whatever the gain-lover praised and blamed would necessarily be the truest.

There would be every necessity.

And if by honor and victory and courage, would it not be whatever the honor-lover and victory-lover praised?

Clearly.

But since it is by experience and practical wisdom and argument?

It is necessary, he said, that whatever the philosopher and the lover of argument praises be truest {{{ 583a }}}.

There being three pleasures, then, would the pleasure of that part of the soul by which we learn be the pleasantest, and would the life of the one in whom this part rules be the pleasantest life?

How could it not? So he said; the man of practical wisdom, being the authoritative judge, praises his own life.

And which life does the judge say is second, I said, and which pleasure is second?

Clearly that of the warlike man and the honor-lover; for it is closer to his own than that of the money-maker.

Last, then, it seems, comes that of the gain-lover.

Why should it not be so? He said.
