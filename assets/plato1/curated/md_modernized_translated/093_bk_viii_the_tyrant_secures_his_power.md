## The Tyrant Secures His Power

Quite necessarily, he said.

This man, then, I said, becomes the one who stirs up strife against those who own property.

This man.

So then, if he is driven out and returns in spite of his enemies, does he come back a finished tyrant?

Clearly. {{{ 566b }}}

But if they are unable to expel him or kill him by denouncing him to the city, they plot in secret to kill him by a violent death.

That is, at any rate, he said, how it tends to happen.

At this point, then, all who have advanced this far discover the notorious request typical of tyrants: to ask the people for some guards for his person, so that the people's helper may be kept safe for them.

Very much so, he said.

And I suppose they grant it, fearing for him but feeling confident about themselves. {{{ 566c }}}

Quite so.

Now, whenever a man who has property sees this, and along with his property gets a reputation for hating the people, then, my friend, this man, in accordance with the oracle given to Croesus—
+ …by the many-pebbled Hermus
+ flees, and does not stay, nor is ashamed to be a coward.

No, he said, for he would not be put to shame a second time.

But whoever is caught, I said, is, I suppose, given over to death.

Necessarily.

But that protector himself, it is clear, does not lie fallen, great {{{ 566d }}} and mighty; instead, having cast down many others, he stands in the chariot of the city, made into a tyrant instead of a protector.

Why should he not? He said.

Shall we, then, go through the happiness, I said, of the man and of the city in which such a mortal comes to be?

By all means, he said, let us go through it.

So then, I said, is it not the case that in the first days and for some time he smiles at and greets everyone he happens to meet, and {{{ 566e }}} says he is not a tyrant, and promises many things both privately and publicly, and frees people from debts and distributes land to the people and to those around him, and pretends to be gracious and gentle to everyone?

Necessarily, he said.

But when, I suppose, he is reconciled with some of his enemies abroad and destroys others, and there is peace from them, then first he is always stirring up some wars, so that the people may be in need of a leader.

That is likely. {{{ 567a }}}

Is it not also so that, by paying taxes, they become poor and are forced to be occupied with their daily needs, and so plot against him less?

Clearly.

And if he suspects, I suppose, that some men of free spirit will not allow him to rule, does he not contrive to destroy them under some pretext, by handing them over to the enemy? For all these reasons, is it always necessary for a tyrant to stir up war?

Necessarily.

And by doing these things, is he not all the more certain to be hated by the {{{ 567b }}} citizens?

How could it be otherwise?

Is it not also the case that some of those who helped set him in power, and who hold power themselves, speak freely both to him and to one another, criticizing what is going on — whichever of them happen to be the boldest?

That is likely.

The tyrant, then, must quietly do away with all of these, if he is to rule, until he leaves no one, whether friend or enemy, who is of any use.

Clearly.

He must, then, look sharply to see who is brave, who is large-minded, who is wise, {{{ 567c }}} who is rich; and so happy is he that, whether he wishes it or not, he is compelled to be an enemy to all these and to plot against them, until he has purged the city.

A beautiful purge indeed, he said.

Yes, I said, the opposite of what physicians do to bodies; for they remove the worst and leave the best, but he does the opposite.

So it seems, he said, for it is a necessity for him, if he is indeed to rule. {{{ 567d }}}

He is bound, then, I said, by a blessed necessity, which commands him either to live among the base majority and be hated by them, or not to live at all.

By such a necessity, he said.

So then, is it not the case that the more he is hated by the citizens for doing these things, the more numerous and more trustworthy the bodyguards he will need?

How could it be otherwise?

Who, then, are the trustworthy ones? And from where will he send for them?

Many will come flying to him of their own accord, he said, if he offers pay.

Drones, I said — by the dog — you seem to me, again, {{{ 567e }}} to be talking about some sort of foreign and motley men.

Your impression of me, he said, is a true one.

But who from here at home? Would he not be willing—

How so?

To take the citizens' slaves away, free them, and make them part of his own bodyguard.

Certainly, he said; since these are indeed the most trustworthy to him.

Truly, I said, you speak of a blessed sort of tyrant, if he makes use of such {{{ 568a }}} friends and trustworthy men, after destroying those earlier ones.
