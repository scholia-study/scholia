## Justice Is the Advantage of the Stronger

{{{ 338c }}} Listen, then, he said. For I say that justice is nothing other than the advantage of the stronger. Well, why don't you praise me? But you won't be willing to.

Only if I first learn what you mean, I said; for as yet I don't know. You say that the advantage of the stronger is just. And whatever do you mean by this, Thrasymachus? Surely you do not mean something like this: that if Pulydamas the pancratiast is stronger than we are, and beef is advantageous to his body, then this {{{ 338d }}} food is both advantageous and just for us too, the weaker.

You're disgusting, Socrates, he said, and you take hold of the argument in whatever way will do it the most damage.

Not at all, my good man, I said; but tell me more clearly what you mean.

Don't you know, he said, that among cities some are ruled by tyranny, some by democracy, and some by aristocracy?

How could I not?

So it's this that has power in each city — the ruling element?

Certainly. {{{ 338e }}}

Now each government establishes laws for its own advantage — a democracy makes democratic laws, a tyranny makes tyrannical ones, and the others do likewise; and once they have established them, they declare this — what is to their own advantage — to be just for their subjects, and they punish anyone who oversteps it as a lawbreaker and a wrongdoer. This, then, my excellent fellow, is what I mean when I say that the same thing is just in every {{{ 339a }}} city — the advantage of the established government; and since it is this that holds power, it follows for anyone who reasons correctly that the same thing is just everywhere: the advantage of the stronger.

Now, I said, I understand what you mean; but whether it's true or not, I'll try to find out. So then, Thrasymachus, you too have answered that the advantageous is just — although you forbade me to give that very answer — but now, it seems, there's this addition: the advantage of the stronger. {{{ 339b }}}

A small addition, perhaps, he said.

It's not yet clear whether it's even a large one; but it is clear that this must be examined — whether what you say is true. Since I too agree that justice is something advantageous, while you add something and say that it is the advantage of the stronger — which I don't know to be so — we must examine it.

Examine it, he said.

That's what I'll do, I said. And tell me: don't you also say that it's just to obey the rulers?

I do. {{{ 339c }}}

And are the rulers in each city infallible, or are they the sort who can also make mistakes?

Surely, he said, they're the sort who can also make mistakes.

So when they set about making laws, don't they make some correctly, and some not correctly?

I suppose so.

And is making laws correctly just this — making them advantageous to themselves — and making them incorrectly, disadvantageous? Or what do you mean?

Just so.

And whatever they establish must be done by the ruled, and that is what is just?

How could it not be? {{{ 339d }}}

Then according to your account, it's just not only to do what is advantageous to the stronger, but also the opposite — what is not advantageous.

What do you mean? So he asked.

I think I'm just saying what you say; but let's look at it more carefully. Hasn't it been agreed that the rulers, in giving orders to the ruled to do certain things, sometimes miss what is best for themselves, and yet that it's just for the ruled to do whatever the rulers order? Hasn't this been agreed?

I suppose it has, he said. {{{ 339e }}}

Consider then, I said, that you've also agreed that it's just to do what's disadvantageous to the rulers and to the stronger, whenever the rulers unintentionally order what's bad for themselves, while you say it's just for the ruled to do what those rulers ordered — doesn't it then necessarily follow, wisest Thrasymachus, that it works out exactly this way: that it's just to do the opposite of what you say? For surely what's disadvantageous to the stronger is just what the weaker are ordered to do. {{{ 340a }}}

Yes, by Zeus, Socrates, Polemarchus said, quite plainly.

Only if you testify on his behalf, said Cleitophon, cutting in.

And what need is there, he said, of a witness? For Thrasymachus himself agrees that the rulers sometimes order what's bad for themselves, and that it's just for the ruled to do it.

That's because Thrasymachus laid it down, Polemarchus, that doing what's commanded by the rulers is just.

But he also laid it down, Cleitophon, that the advantage of the stronger {{{ 340b }}} is just. And having laid down both of these, he then agreed that the stronger sometimes order the weaker and the ruled to do what's disadvantageous to themselves. And from these admissions, the advantage of the stronger would be no more just than the disadvantage.

But, said Cleitophon, what he meant by the advantage of the stronger was whatever the stronger believes to be advantageous to himself — that's what the weaker must do, and that's what he took to be just.

But that's not how it was said, said Polemarchus. {{{ 340c }}}

It makes no difference, Polemarchus, I said; but if that's how Thrasymachus means it now, let's accept it from him that way. So tell me, Thrasymachus: was this what you meant to call justice — the advantage of the stronger as it seems to the stronger, whether it's really advantageous or not? Shall we say that's what you mean?

Not at all, he said; do you think I call the man who's making a mistake stronger at the moment he's making it?

I did, I said; I thought you meant this when you agreed that the rulers aren't infallible but also make mistakes.
