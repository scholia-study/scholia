## The Feverish City, and the Need for Guardians

Very well, I said; I follow. It seems we are examining not merely how a city comes to be, but a luxurious city as well. Perhaps that is no bad thing, either — by examining a city of that sort too, we might catch sight of how justice and injustice actually take root in cities. The city we have gone through so far seems to me to be the true one, a sort of healthy city; but if you like, let us look at a feverish city too — nothing stops us. {{{ 373a }}} For it seems this will not satisfy some people, nor this way of living either, but couches will be added, and tables, and other furniture, and relishes too, and perfumes, and incense, and courtesans, and cakes — each of these in every variety. And moreover, what we spoke of at first — houses and clothes and shoes — must no longer be treated as merely necessary, but painting and embroidery must be brought into play, and gold and ivory and everything of that sort must be acquired. Is that not so? {{{ 373b }}}

Yes, he said.

So the city must in turn be made bigger; for that healthy one is no longer sufficient, but must now be filled out with bulk and numbers — with all the people in cities who are there for something other than necessity: hunters of every kind, and imitators, many of them concerned with shapes and colors, many with music — poets and their assistants, reciters, actors, dancers, contractors, and makers of every kind of article, {{{ 373c }}} including those for women's adornment. And moreover we shall need more servants — or do you not think we shall need tutors, wet-nurses, nurses, hairdressers, barbers, and cooks and chefs besides? We shall need swineherds too, besides; this had no place in our earlier city — there was no need of it — but in this one there will be need of that as well. And we shall need great numbers of other livestock besides, if people are going to eat them — is that not so?

How could it not be? {{{ 373d }}}

So shall we not be far more in need of doctors too, living this way, than we were before?

Far more so.

And the land, too, which was then sufficient to feed the people of that time, will now become small, from having been sufficient. Or how shall we put it?

That is so, he said.

So must we not cut off a piece of our neighbors' land, if we are to have enough for pasture and plowing, and they in turn a piece of ours, if they too let themselves go after limitless acquisition of wealth, overstepping the boundary of what is necessary? {{{ 373e }}}

A great necessity, Socrates, he said.

Shall we go to war after that, then, Glaucon? Or how else will it be?

So it will, he said.

And let us not yet say anything, I said, about whether war does any harm or good, but only this much — that we have now found the origin of war, in the very things that most bring evil upon cities, both privately and publicly, whenever it does occur.

Quite so.

So the city needs to be bigger still, my friend, not by a small amount, {{{ 374a }}} but by a whole army, which will go out and fight to the finish with any invaders, on behalf of all our property and everything we have just been talking about.

What do you mean? So he asked — are they not enough by themselves?

Not if you and all the rest of us agreed rightly, I said, when we were molding the city; for we agreed, I think, if you remember, that it is impossible for one person to practice many crafts well.

That is true, he said. {{{ 374b }}}

What then? I said — does the contest of war not seem to be a matter of craft?

Very much so, he said.

Then should we care more about shoemaking than about the craft of war?

Not at all.

But surely we did not allow the shoemaker to try to be a farmer at the same time, or a weaver, or a builder, but kept him a shoemaker only, so that the work of shoemaking would turn out well for us; and in the same way we gave each of the others in turn a single occupation, the one for which each was naturally suited, and at which each was to have leisure from the rest {{{ 374c }}} throughout his life, working at that alone and never letting the right moments pass, so as to produce it well; but is the business of war, of all things, not among those it matters most to have carried out well? Or is it so easy that a man farming will at the same time be a warrior too, and a man making shoes, or working at any other craft whatever — while no one could become a competent player of draughts or dice without practicing that very thing from childhood, but only treating it as a sideline? {{{ 374d }}} And will a man, merely by picking up a shield or some other weapon or instrument of war, become on that very day a competent contestant in hoplite combat or any other kind of fighting proper to war, when none of the other instruments, once picked up, makes anyone a craftsman or an athlete, nor is of any use to one who has neither grasped the knowledge proper to each nor given it sufficient practice?

In that case, he said, the instruments would be worth a great deal.

So then, I said, in so far as the guardians' work is the greatest, {{{ 374e }}} by so much would it need the most leisure from everything else, and in turn the greatest craft and care.

I think so myself, he said.

Then would it not also need a nature suited to that very pursuit?

How could it not?

Then it would be our task, it seems, if we are able, to select which people and what kind of natures are suited to the guarding of a city.

Ours indeed.

By Zeus, I said, then it is no trivial task we have taken on; still, we must not shrink from it, so far as our strength allows.
