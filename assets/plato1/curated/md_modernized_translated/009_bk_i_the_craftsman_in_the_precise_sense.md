## The Craftsman in the Precise Sense

{{{ 340d }}} You're a quibbler in argument, Socrates, he said; for instance, do you call a doctor who makes a mistake with his patients a doctor in respect of that very mistake? Or do you call the reckoner, when he blunders in a calculation, a reckoner in respect of that very blunder, at the moment he blunders? But I think that's just how we speak of it in words — that the doctor erred, and the reckoner erred, and the schoolmaster; whereas each of these, I think, insofar as he is that {{{ 340e }}} which we call him, never errs at all; so that, by the precise account — since you're the one insisting on precision — none of the craftsmen ever errs. For the one who errs errs through a failure of knowledge, in which respect he is not a craftsman; so that no craftsman or wise man or ruler ever errs at the time he is ruling, even though everyone would say that the doctor erred and the ruler erred. So take it that I too answered you just now in that spirit; but the most precise statement happens to be this: that the ruler, {{{ 341a }}} insofar as he is a ruler, does not err, and, not erring, lays down what is best for himself, and this the one ruled must carry out. So what I said from the beginning is what I call just — to do what is to the advantage of the stronger.

Well then, I said, Thrasymachus — do you think I'm quibbling?

Very much so, he said.

Do you think I asked what I asked out of malice, meaning to play you foul in argument?

I'm quite sure of it, he said. And it will do you no good at all — {{{ 341b }}} for you won't escape my notice playing foul, and, once caught, you won't be able to force your way through by argument.

Nor would I even attempt it, my good man, I said. But so that nothing of that kind happens between us again, make clear which way you mean the ruler and the stronger — the one so called in a loose manner of speaking, or the one in the precise sense you were just now describing, whose advantage, since he is the stronger, it will be just for the weaker to serve.

The one who is a ruler in the most precise sense, he said. Against that, play foul and quibble if you can — I ask no quarter of you — but you'll never manage it. {{{ 341c }}}

Do you think me so mad, I said, as to attempt to shave a lion and play the quibbler with Thrasymachus?

You did attempt it just now, at any rate, he said, and that though you are a nobody.

Enough of that sort of thing, I said. But tell me — the doctor in the precise sense, the one you were just now describing, is he a moneymaker, or one who cares for the sick? And speak of the one who is really a doctor.

One who cares for the sick, he said.

And what of the ship's captain? Is the true captain a ruler of sailors, or a sailor himself?

A ruler of sailors. {{{ 341d }}}

This, I think, should not be reckoned against him — that he sails in the ship — nor should he be called a sailor for that; for it's not in virtue of sailing that he's called a captain, but in virtue of his craft and his rule over the sailors.

True, he said.

So is there something advantageous to each of these?

Certainly.

And isn't craft, I said, by nature directed at this — at seeking out and providing what is advantageous to each?

At that, he said.

Is there, then, some advantage for each craft itself, other than being as complete as possible? {{{ 341e }}}

What do you mean by that question?

Like this, I said: if you were to ask me whether it's enough for a body to be a body, or whether it needs something further, I'd say that it certainly does need something. That's why the craft of medicine has now been discovered — because the body is deficient and it isn't enough for it to be merely such as it is. It was to supply the body with what is advantageous to it that this craft was devised. Do I seem to you to be right in putting it this way, I said, or not?

Right, he said. {{{ 342a }}}

What then? Is medicine itself deficient, or does some other craft too stand in need of some virtue of its own — as eyes need sight and ears hearing, and for that reason need some craft over them to consider and supply what is advantageous to them — is there, then, some deficiency present in the craft itself, and does each craft need another craft to consider its advantage, and does the craft doing the considering in turn need yet another such craft, and is this without end? {{{ 342b }}} Or will it consider its own advantage by itself? Or does it need neither itself nor another craft to consider what is advantageous with a view to its own deficiency — since no deficiency or fault is present in any craft at all, nor is it fitting for a craft to seek the advantage of anything other than that of which it is the craft, while the craft itself, being correct, is unharmed and undamaged, so long as each one is precisely and wholly what it is? And consider it by that precise account — is it so, or otherwise?

So it appears, he said. {{{ 342c }}}

Then medicine, I said, does not consider the advantage of medicine, but of the body.

Yes, he said.

Nor does horsemanship consider the advantage of horsemanship, but of horses; nor does any other craft consider its own advantage — for it has no need of that — but that of the thing of which it is the craft.

So it appears, he said.

But surely, Thrasymachus, the crafts rule and hold power over that of which they are the crafts.

He agreed to this, though only with great reluctance.

Then no knowledge at all considers or enjoins the advantage of the stronger, but that of the weaker and the one ruled {{{ 342d }}} by it.

He agreed to this too in the end, though he tried to fight it on the way — and since he had agreed, is it not true, I said, that no doctor either, insofar as he is a doctor, considers or enjoins the advantage of the doctor, but that of the patient? For it has been agreed that the precise doctor is a ruler of bodies, not a moneymaker. Or has that not been agreed?

He agreed.

And is not the true captain too a ruler of sailors, rather than a sailor? {{{ 342e }}}

It has been agreed.

Then a captain and ruler of that kind will not consider or prescribe the advantage of the captain, but that of the sailor and the one ruled.

He agreed, reluctantly.

So too, Thrasymachus, I said, no one else in any position of rule, insofar as he is a ruler, considers or enjoins his own advantage, but that of the one he rules and for whom he practices his craft, keeping his eye fixed on that person and on what is advantageous and fitting for him, and it is with this in view that he says everything he says and does everything he does.
