## What City Would Suit the Philosopher

{{{ 497a }}} And yet, he said, it would be no small thing he had accomplished when he departed.

Nor the greatest, I said, if he has not found a fitting constitution — for in one that fits, he himself will grow more and, along with his own concerns, will preserve those of the community as well.

As for philosophy, then — why it has incurred slander, and that it has incurred it unjustly — I think that has been adequately stated, unless you yourself still have something more to say.

But, he said, I have nothing more to say about this — yet which of the constitutions now current do you say is fitting for it? {{{ 497b }}}

Not any whatsoever, I said, but this is exactly what I charge: that none of the constitutions now existing is worthy of a philosophic nature — and that is why it is twisted and altered, just as foreign seed sown in another soil tends, once overpowered, to fade into the native growth, so too this kind, as things stand now, does not hold to its own power but falls away into an alien character; but if it should obtain the best constitution, just as {{{ 497c }}} it is itself the best, then it will show that this nature was in truth divine, while the rest — both of natures and of pursuits — are merely human. It is plain, then, that after this you will ask what this constitution is.

You have guessed wrong, he said, for that is not what I meant, but whether this is the constitution we went through in founding our city, or a different one.

In other respects, I said, it is this one; but this very point was made even then — that there would always need to be present in the city something {{{ 497d }}} that holds the same account of the constitution that you, the lawgiver, held when you laid down the laws.

It was indeed said, he replied.

But it was not made sufficiently clear, I said, from fear of the very things which you, by taking hold of them, have shown to make its demonstration long and difficult — since what is left is not the easiest thing in the world to go through either.

What sort of thing?

In what way a city that takes up philosophy will not be destroyed. For all great things are indeed precarious, and, as the saying goes, fine things are truly difficult. {{{ 497e }}}

But all the same, he said, let the proof be brought to completion, once this point has been made clear.

It is not unwillingness, I said, but, if anything, inability that will stand in the way — and since you are here, you will at least see my eagerness. Observe, even now, how eagerly and how recklessly I am about to say that a city must take up this pursuit in the opposite way from how it does now.

How?

Now, I said, those who even take it up — being young men, just {{{ 498a }}} out of childhood, in the interval between household management and money-making — come close to its hardest part and then break off, these being the ones counted the most philosophic — and by hardest I mean the part concerning arguments — while afterward, if, when others pursue it, they are invited and are willing to become listeners, they think it a great thing, supposing that it ought to be practiced only as a side matter; and toward old age, except for a few, they are extinguished far more thoroughly than the {{{ 498b }}} Heraclitean sun, in that they never kindle again.

But how ought it to be? So he asked.

Just the opposite: while they are still lads and boys they should take up an education and a philosophy suited to their years, and take very good care of their bodies during the time when these are sprouting and growing toward manhood, thereby acquiring a servant for philosophy; and as their age advances, when the soul begins to come to its maturity, they should intensify its exercises; and when their strength gives out and they are past political and {{{ 498c }}} military service, then at last they should be turned loose to graze and do nothing else except as a side matter — those, that is, who mean to live happily and, when they have died, to crown the life they have lived with the portion that befits it there.

You truly seem to me, he said, to speak eagerly at any rate, Socrates; I think, however, that most of those listening will resist all the more eagerly, and will not be persuaded in the least, beginning with Thrasymachus.

Do not set me at odds with Thrasymachus, I said, now that we have just {{{ 498d }}} become friends — not that we were enemies before. For we will not relax our effort in the least, until we either persuade both him and the others, or achieve something of use for that life to come, whenever, being born again, they happen upon such arguments.

You have named a short time indeed, he said.

For no time at all, I said — that is, compared with all time. However, it is no wonder that most people do not believe what is said, for they have never yet seen the thing now described {{{ 498e }}} actually occur, but rather have seen only such sayings as are deliberately made to resemble one another, and not, as now, coinciding by sheer chance. But a man made equal to virtue and, so far as possible, fully assimilated to it in both deed and word, holding power {{{ 499a }}} in some other such city, they have never yet seen — neither one such man nor several. Or do you think they have?
