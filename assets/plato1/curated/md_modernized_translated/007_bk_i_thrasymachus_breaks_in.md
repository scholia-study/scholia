## Thrasymachus Breaks In

{{{ 336b }}} Thrasymachus, though he had often tried to break in and seize hold of the argument while we were in the middle of our discussion, was each time held back by the people sitting near him, who wanted to hear the argument out; but when we paused and I had said this, he could no longer keep still, but gathered himself together like a wild beast and hurled himself at us as if to tear us apart.

Polemarchus and I were frightened and thrown into a panic; but he broke in among us and said, What foolish talk {{{ 336c }}} has had hold of you two for so long, Socrates? And why do you keep playing the fool with each other, giving way to one another like this? But if you truly want to know what justice is, don't just ask questions, and don't go chasing the glory of refuting whoever answers — you know perfectly well it's easier to ask than to answer — but answer yourself, and say what you claim justice is. And mind you don't tell me it is {{{ 336d }}} the obligatory, or the beneficial, or the profitable, or the gainful, or the advantageous — say plainly and exactly what you mean, since I won't accept it if you spout such drivel.

Hearing this, I was thunderstruck, and looking at him I was afraid, and I think that if I had not caught sight of him before he saw me, I would have been struck dumb. But as it was, just when he was beginning to turn savage at the argument, I caught sight of him first, {{{ 336e }}} so that I was able to answer him, and I said, trembling a little, Thrasymachus, don't be harsh with us; if he and I are making some mistake in our inquiry into the argument, be assured that we are going wrong unwillingly. Don't imagine that if we were searching for gold, we would ever willingly give way to each other in the search and ruin our chance of finding it, but that when we're searching for justice, a thing worth far more than piles of gold, we're being so foolish as to yield to one another like this, and not doing everything we can to bring it to light. You may think that, my friend, if you like. But I think we simply cannot; so it's surely far more {{{ 337a }}} reasonable that we should be pitied by you clever people than treated harshly.

And when he heard this, he gave a very sardonic laugh and said, By Heracles, this is that habitual irony of Socrates — I knew it, and I told these men beforehand that you would refuse to answer, and would play the ironist, and do anything at all rather than answer, if anyone asked you something.

You are a clever man, Thrasymachus, I said, so you knew very well that if you asked someone what twelve is, and in asking told him {{{ 337b }}} in advance — "Now, sir, don't you tell me that twelve is twice six, or three times four, or six times two, or four times three, since I won't accept it if you talk such nonsense" — it would be obvious to you, I think, that no one could answer a person who asks in that way. But what if he had said to you, "Thrasymachus, what do you mean? Am I not to answer any of what you forbade? Or, my remarkable friend, am I not to say it even if one of them happens to be true, but must say something {{{ 337c }}} else, other than the truth? Or what do you mean?" What would you have said to him in reply to that?

Well, he said — as though this were anything like that.

There's nothing to stop it being so, I said; and even if it isn't really similar, but seems so to the person being asked, do you think he'll be any less likely to answer what appears true to him, whether we forbid it or not?

So are you going to do the same thing, then, he said, and answer with one of the things I forbade?

I wouldn't be surprised, I said, if that is how it seemed to me once I had looked into it. {{{ 337d }}}

Well then, he said, what if I showed another answer about justice, different from all of these, and better than they are? What penalty do you think you deserve?

What else, I said, than what's fitting for the one who doesn't know? And surely it's fitting to learn from the one who knows; so that's the penalty I think I deserve.

How charming you are, he said, but besides learning, you must pay money too.

Certainly, once I have some, I said.

But there is money, said Glaucon. So speak, Thrasymachus, for the sake of the money — we'll all contribute it for Socrates. {{{ 337e }}}

I'm sure you will, he said, so that Socrates can pull his usual trick — not answer himself, but seize on the argument when someone else answers, and refute it.

How could anyone answer, my excellent fellow, I said, when in the first place he does not know and does not even claim to know, and then, even if he does think something, he has been forbidden by no ordinary man to say anything of what he thinks? No, it's far more {{{ 338a }}} fitting that you should speak instead, since you do claim to know and to have something to say. So don't do otherwise — do me the favor of answering, and don't grudge teaching Glaucon here, and the rest of us.

When I had said this, Glaucon and the others begged him not to do otherwise. Thrasymachus was plainly eager to speak, so that he could win approval, since he thought he had a splendid answer to give, but he kept up the pretense of insisting that I should be the one to answer. In the end he gave way, {{{ 338b }}} and then said, So this is Socrates's wisdom — he himself refuses to teach, but goes around learning from others, and doesn't even pay them thanks for it.

That I learn from others, I said, is true, Thrasymachus, but when you say I don't pay them thanks for it, you're wrong; I pay as much as I can. I can only pay in praise, since I have no money. But just how eagerly I do this, whenever someone seems to me to speak well, you'll find out very soon, as soon as you answer — for I think you're going to speak well.
