## Is Such a City Possible? The Third Wave

Let us take that as agreed, he said, and grant that both these points and what came before are sound.

But it seems to me, Socrates, that if anyone lets you talk on like this, you will never get back to the point you pushed aside before saying all this — whether this constitution can come about at all, and in what way it can; since, if it did come about, everything would indeed be good for the city in which it came about, and I will say myself the things you leave out: that they would fight the enemy {{{ 471d }}} best by abandoning one another the least, recognizing one another and calling each other by these names — brothers, fathers, sons; and if the women also campaigned together, whether stationed in the same rank or posted behind, both to frighten the enemy and in case there should ever be need of help, I know that in this way too they would be altogether unconquerable; and I see, besides, how many good things are left out that they would have at home. {{{ 471e }}} But — taking it that I grant all this would be so, and countless other things besides, if this constitution came about — say no more about it; instead, let us now try to persuade ourselves of this very thing, that it is possible, and in what way it is possible, and let the rest go. {{{ 472a }}}

You have made a sudden raid on my argument, I said, and you show no mercy to a man who is dragging his feet. For perhaps you do not know that, having barely escaped the first two waves, you are now bringing on me the greatest and harshest of the triple wave; once you have seen and heard it, you will fully forgive me, understanding that I was right to hesitate and to be afraid of stating and attempting to examine so paradoxical a claim.

The more you say such things, he said, the less you will be let off {{{ 472b }}} by us from saying how this constitution can come to be. So speak, and do not waste time.

Well then, I said, first of all we must remember this — that it was in seeking what justice is, and injustice, that we came to this point.

We must; but what of that? He said.

Nothing; but if we find what justice itself is like, shall we also require the just man to differ from it in no way, but to be in every respect such as justice {{{ 472c }}} is? Or shall we be content if he is as close to it as possible and shares in it more than others do?

That way, he said — we shall be content.

It was for the sake of a pattern, then, I said, that we were seeking justice itself, what it is, and the perfectly just man, if such a one could come to be, and what he would be like if he did, and injustice too, in turn, and the most unjust man — so that, looking to them and seeing how they appear to us with respect to happiness and its opposite, we would be forced to agree, about ourselves as well, that whoever is most {{{ 472d }}} like them will have a fate most like theirs — not for the sake of proving that these things can actually come about.

That is true, he said.

Do you think, then, that a painter is any less good who, having painted a pattern of what the most beautiful man would be like, and having rendered everything in the picture adequately, is unable to prove that such a man could actually exist?

No, by Zeus, not I, he said.

What then? Were we not also, we say, making a pattern {{{ 472e }}} in speech of a good city?

Certainly.

Do you think, then, that we are speaking any less well on this account, if we cannot prove that it is possible to found a city in the way we described?

Certainly not, he said.

That, then, I said, is how the truth stands; but if we must also be eager to prove, for your sake, above all how and in what respect it would be most possible, then agree with me again, for the sake of such a proof, on the same points.

What points? {{{ 473a }}}

Is it possible for anything to be done exactly as it is described in speech, or is it the nature of action to touch truth less than speech does, even if someone does not think so? But do you agree that this is so, or not?

I agree, he said.

Do not, then, compel me to show that the very things we went through in speech ⟨would⟩ have to come about in every respect in deed as well; but if we are able to find how a city could be governed as closely as possible to what has been described, let us say {{{ 473b }}} that we have discovered that these things you require can come about. Or will you not be content to get this much? I, for one, would be content.

So would I, he said.

Next, then, it seems, let us try to seek out and demonstrate what it is that is now being done badly in cities and keeps them from being governed in this way, and what smallest change, if it took place, would bring a city around to this manner of constitution — best of all a single change, failing that two, and failing that, the fewest in number and the smallest in power. {{{ 473c }}}

Certainly, he said.

With a single change, then, I said, we do seem to me to be able to show that it could be transformed — not, however, a small or easy one, but a possible one.

What change? He said.

I am now, I said, at the very point that we compared to the greatest wave. It shall be said, in any case, even if it is likely to drown me, quite literally, in laughter breaking over me like a wave, and in disrepute. But consider what I am about to say.

Speak, he said.

Unless, I said, either philosophers rule as kings {{{ 473d }}} in our cities, or those who are now called kings and rulers take up philosophy, genuinely and fully, so that political power and philosophy coincide in the very same place, while the many natures that now travel separately toward the one or the other are of necessity shut out, there will be no end to troubles for our cities, dear Glaucon, nor, I think, for the human race {{{ 473e }}} either — nor will this constitution we have now gone through in speech ever grow up as far as it can and see the light of day. But this is what has long made me hesitate to say it, seeing how far it will run against common opinion; for it is hard to see that no other city could be happy, neither in private life nor in public.

And he said: Socrates, you have thrown out a saying and a claim of such a kind that, once you have said it, you must expect that a great many people, and {{{ 474a }}} not undistinguished ones, will now rush at you just like that — as if, throwing off their cloaks and stripping naked, they had snatched up whatever weapon each man happened to have and were running flat out to do something extraordinary; and if you do not ward them off with argument and make your escape, you will truly pay the penalty of being laughed at.

So it is you, I said, who are responsible for this?

And quite right of me too, he said. But I will surely not abandon you — I will defend you with what means I have; and what I have is goodwill and encouragement, and perhaps I could answer you {{{ 474b }}} more fittingly than someone else would. But since you have such a helper, try to show those who doubt that it is as you say.

We must try, I said, since you are offering me so great an alliance. So it seems necessary to me, if we are somehow going to escape those you speak of, to mark out, with respect to the philosophers themselves, whom we mean when we venture to say that they must rule, so that once this is made clear, one can defend the claim, showing {{{ 474c }}} that it belongs by nature to some to take hold of philosophy and to lead in the city, while it belongs to the rest not to take hold of it, but to follow the one who leads.
