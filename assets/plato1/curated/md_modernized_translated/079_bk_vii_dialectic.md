## Dialectic

And I too, he said, guess as much. But that is an enormous task you speak of, Socrates.

Do you mean the prelude, I said, or what? Or don't we know that all these things are preludes to the very strain that must be learned? For surely those who are clever at this sort of thing do not seem to you {{{ 531e }}} to be dialecticians.

No, by Zeus, he said, except perhaps a very few of those I've met.

But then, I said, will people who are unable to give and receive an account ever know any of the things we say must be known?

Nor that, either, he said. {{{ 532a }}}

Well then, Glaucon, I said, is this now the very strain that dialectic carries through to its end? It is a strain that, intelligible though it is, the power of sight would imitate — the power which, we said, attempts to look at the living creatures themselves, and at the stars themselves, and finally at the sun itself. So too, whenever someone attempts, by dialectic, without any aid from the senses, to set out through argument toward what each thing itself is, and does not stop before {{{ 532b }}} he grasps, by reason itself, what the good itself is, he arrives at the very end of the intelligible, just as the other man then arrived at the end of the visible.

Absolutely, he said.

What then? Do you not call this journey dialectic?

Of course.

This release, I said, from the bonds, and the turning around from the shadows to the images and the light, and the way up out of the underground dwelling into the sun — where one is still unable {{{ 532c }}} to look at the animals and plants and the light of the sun, but can look at divine appearances in water and at shadows of the things that are, rather than at shadows of images cast by another light of the same kind, judged in relation to the sun — all this business of the crafts we have gone through has just this power: to lead the best part of the soul up to the vision of the best among the things that are, just as before it led the clearest part in the body up to the sight of the brightest thing in the region that is bodily and {{{ 532d }}} visible.

That is how I accept it, he said, for my part. And yet it seems to me altogether difficult to accept, and difficult, in another way again, not to accept. Nevertheless — for these things must not be heard only on the present occasion, but one must come back to them often again afterward — let us take them to hold as they are now stated, and go on to the strain itself, and go through it just as we went through the prelude. Tell me, then, what is the character of the power of dialectic, and {{{ 532e }}} into what kinds it is in fact divided, and again what its ways are; for these, it seems, would now at last be the ones leading to that very thing itself, and on arriving there one would have, as it were, a rest from the road and the end of the journey. {{{ 533a }}}

No longer, I said, dear Glaucon, will you be able to follow — though for my part nothing would be lacking in eagerness — nor would you still see an image of what we are speaking of, but the truth itself, as it at any rate appears to me; whether it really is so or not is no longer worth insisting on, but that there is something of this kind to be seen must be insisted on. Is that not so?

Of course.

And also that the power of dialectic alone could reveal this to someone experienced in what we have just gone through, and that it is possible in no other way?

This too, he said, is worth insisting on. {{{ 533b }}}

At any rate, I said, no one will dispute this with us when we say that, concerning each thing itself — what each thing is — no other method attempts, in an orderly way, to grasp it in every instance. But all the other crafts are turned either toward the opinions and desires of men, or toward generation and composition, or toward the tending of the things that grow and the things that are put together; while the remaining ones, which we said do lay hold of something of what is — geometry and the studies that follow it — we see dreaming, as it were, {{{ 533c }}} about what is, but unable to see it while awake, so long as they make use of hypotheses and leave these undisturbed, unable to give any account of them. For when the beginning is something one does not know, and the end and the middle are woven together out of what one does not know, what device is there for such an agreement ever to become knowledge?

None, he said.

Then, I said, the dialectical method alone proceeds in this way — doing away with the hypotheses and going to the beginning itself {{{ 533d }}}, so as to make it secure — and gently draws and leads upward the eye of the soul, which is, in truth, buried in a kind of barbaric mud, using the crafts we went through as fellow-workers and helpers in the turning around; these we have often called forms of knowledge out of habit, though they need another name, one clearer than opinion but dimmer than knowledge — understanding, as we defined it, I believe, earlier — though this, as it seems to me, is not a dispute about {{{ 533e }}} a name, for people whose inquiry concerns matters as great as those now before us.

Certainly not, he said.

But any name that only somehow makes plain the state in the soul, in point of clarity, ⟨will suffice? Yes.⟩

It will suffice, then, I said, just as before, to call the first part knowledge, the second understanding, the third {{{ 534a }}} belief, and the fourth imagination; and these two together, opinion, and those two together, reason; and opinion concerned with becoming, and reason concerned with being; and that as being is to becoming, so reason is to opinion, and as reason is to opinion, so knowledge is to belief and understanding is to imagination; and let us leave aside, Glaucon, the proportion among the things these are set over, and the twofold division of each — of the opinable and the intelligible — so that it does not fill us with a great many more arguments than those already gone through. {{{ 534b }}}

Well, he said, the rest seems right to me too, at least so far as I'm able to follow.

Do you also call dialectical the one who grasps the account of the being of each thing? And will you say that the one who lacks this — to the extent he cannot give an account to himself and to another — has, to that same extent, no intellect about the matter?

How, he replied, could I say he did?

Then likewise concerning the good: won't you say that whoever cannot mark off the {{{ 534c }}} form of the good in argument, separating it from everything else, and cannot, as in a battle, press through every refutation, eager to test things not by opinion but by being, and come through all of them with an argument that never trips, knows neither the good itself nor any other good, but that if he somehow lays hold of some image of it, he lays hold of it by opinion and not by knowledge, and that he dreams and drowses away his present life, and, before waking here, arrives in Hades {{{ 534d }}} first and falls wholly asleep there?

By Zeus, he said, I will certainly say all that.

But surely, I think, you would not allow your own children, whom you raise and educate in words, if you were ever to raise them in deed, being as irrational as lines, to be rulers in the city with authority over the greatest matters.

Certainly not, he said.

Then you will make it law for them to take hold, above all, of this education, from which they will be most able to ask questions and give answers with the greatest knowledge? {{{ 534e }}}

I will make it law, he said, together with you at least.

Does it seem to you, then, I said, that dialectic lies for us on top of the studies like a coping-stone, and that no other study could rightly be set any higher than this, but that the course of studies now has {{{ 535a }}} its completion?
