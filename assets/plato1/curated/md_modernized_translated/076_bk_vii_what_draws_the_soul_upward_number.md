## What Draws the Soul Upward: Number

{{{ 521c }}} Do you want us, then, to consider this right away — in what way such men will come into being, and how one will lead them up into the light, just as some are said to have ascended from Hades to the gods?

How could I not want it? So he said.

This, then, as it seems, would not be the turning of a potsherd, but a turning around of the soul from a kind of day that is really night, toward the true day — a rising up to what is — which we shall say is true philosophy.

Certainly.

Then is it not necessary to consider which of our studies has such {{{ 521d }}} a power?

How could it not be?

What, then, Glaucon, would be a study that draws the soul from becoming toward being? And here is what I have in mind as I ask it — did we not say that these men, while young, must of necessity be athletes of war?

We did say that.

So the study we are seeking must attend to this as well, in addition to that other requirement.

Which one?

That it not be useless to men of war.

It must indeed, he said, if that is possible.

Now, earlier in our discussion, they were educated by us in gymnastic and music {{{ 521e }}}.

That was so, he said.

And gymnastic, I think, has to do with what comes to be and passes away; for it presides over the growth and decay of the body.

So it appears.

This, then, would not be the study we are seeking. {{{ 522a }}}

No, it would not.

But is it music, then, as much of it as we went through before?

But that, he said, was the counterpart of gymnastic, if you remember — training the guardians by habits, imparting through harmony a certain harmoniousness, not knowledge, and through rhythm a certain rhythmicality, and having, in its stories too, other habits akin to these, both the stories that were mythical and those that were more true; but as for a study leading toward the sort of thing {{{ 522b }}} you are now seeking, there was none in it.

You remind me most accurately, I said — for in truth it had nothing of the kind. But, my good Glaucon, what could such a thing be? For all the crafts, I suppose, seemed to be vulgar—

How could they not? And yet what other study is left, apart from music and gymnastic and the crafts?

Come, I said, if we have nothing further to grasp outside of these, let us take up something that extends over all of them.

Which is that? {{{ 522c }}}

Something like this common thing, which all crafts and all forms of understanding and knowledge make use of — which indeed everyone must learn first of all.

Which is that? So he asked.

This humble thing, I said — distinguishing one from two from three; in a word, I mean number and calculation. Or is that not how things stand with them — that every craft and every branch of knowledge is compelled to partake of them?

Very much so, he said.

And is not the craft of war, I said, one of them?

Quite necessarily, he said. {{{ 522d }}}

At any rate, I said, Palamedes always makes Agamemnon out to be utterly ridiculous as a general in the tragedies. Or have you not noticed that he claims to have discovered number, and so to have set the army's ranks in order at Troy and counted the ships and everything else — implying that before this all was uncounted, and that Agamemnon, it seems, did not even know how many feet he had, if he really did not know how to count? And yet what sort of general do you think he would be?

A strange one, he said, for my part, if that were true. {{{ 522e }}}

Shall we not, then, I said, set down as a necessary study for a man of war the ability to calculate and count?

Most of all, he said, if he is going to understand anything at all about the ranks — or rather, if he is even to be a human being.

Do you notice, I said, the same thing about this study that I do?

Which is that? {{{ 523a }}}

It looks as though it is by nature one of the studies we are seeking, one of those that lead toward reason, yet no one uses it rightly, suited as it entirely is to draw the soul toward being.

What, he said, do you mean?

I will try, I said, to make clear what seems to me to be so. For as to what I distinguish in my own mind as leading where we say and as not leading — join me in looking, and either agree or say no, so that we may see this more clearly too, whether it is as I surmise.

Show me, he said.

Then I am showing you, I said, if you can make it out — on the one hand, the things among our perceptions {{{ 523b }}} that do not summon reason to examine them, since they are judged well enough by perception itself, and on the other hand, the things that entirely summon it to examine them, since perception does nothing sound there.

You obviously mean the things that appear at a distance, he said, and things done in shadow-painting.

You have not quite hit on what I mean, I said.

What sort of thing, he said, do you mean?

The things that do not summon, I said, are those that do not {{{ 523c }}} at the same time pass over into the opposite perception; and I set down as summoning those that do pass over, whenever the perception reveals this no more than its opposite, whether it strikes us from nearby or from a distance. You will grasp what I mean more clearly in this way. These, we say, would be three fingers — the smallest, the second, and the middle one.

Certainly, he said.

Think of me, then, as speaking of them as seen from nearby. But consider this about them for me.

What is that?

Each of them appears alike, I suppose, as a finger, and {{{ 523d }}} in this respect it makes no difference at all whether it is seen in the middle or at the end, whether it is white or black, whether it is thick or thin, and everything of that sort. For in all these cases, the soul of ordinary people is not compelled to ask reason what a finger actually is — since sight never signals to it, at the same time, that a finger is the opposite of a finger.

No, indeed not, he said.

Then, I said, it stands to reason that such a case would {{{ 523e }}} not be apt to summon reason, or to awaken it.

Reasonably so.

But what of this? Does sight see their largeness and smallness well enough, and does it make no difference to it whether one of them lies in the middle or at the end? And does touch likewise perceive thickness and thinness, or softness and hardness, well enough? And do the other perceptions not reveal such things deficiently? Or {{{ 524a }}} does each of them work like this — the perception stationed over the hard is compelled to be stationed over the soft as well, and it reports to the soul that, as it perceives it, hard and soft are the same thing?

That is so, he said.

Then, I said, is it not necessary that in such cases, again, the soul be at a loss — as to what this perception means by hard, if it calls the same thing soft as well; and as to what light and heavy are, if it signifies that the heavy is light and the light is heavy? {{{ 524b }}}

Yes indeed, he said — these reports are strange to the soul, and in need of examination.

It stands to reason, then, I said, that in such cases the soul first summons calculation and reason and tries to examine whether each of the things reported to it is one or two.

How could it not?

Then if it appears as two, does each of the two appear as distinct and as one?

Yes.

If, then, each is one, but both together are two, it will conceive the two {{{ 524c }}} as separate — for it would not conceive two as inseparable, but rather as one.

Rightly.

Yet, we say, sight also saw the large and the small, but not as separate — rather as something blended together. Is that not so?

Yes.

And in order to get clarity here, reason in turn was compelled to see the large and the small, not blended together but distinguished — the opposite of what sight did.

True.

Then is it not from somewhere here that it first occurs to us to ask what, then, the large and the small actually are?

Absolutely.

And so indeed we called the one intelligible, and the other visible.
