## The Guardian Who Is Also a Thief

{{{ 334a }}} But surely the same man is also a good guardian of an army — the one who is able to steal the enemy's plans and their other operations as well?

Certainly.

So whoever is a clever guardian of a thing is also a clever thief of it.

So it seems.

If then the just man is clever at guarding money, he is also clever at stealing it.

That, at any rate, he said, is what the argument suggests.

So the just man has turned out, it seems, to be some sort of thief, and you are likely to have picked this up from Homer; for he too is fond of {{{ 334b }}} Odysseus's grandfather on his mother's side, Autolycus, and says that he surpassed all men in thieving and the swearing of oaths. So it seems that, according to you and to Homer and to Simonides alike, justice is a kind of thievery, though for the benefit of friends and the harm of enemies. Wasn't that what you were saying?

No, by Zeus, he said, I no longer know what I was saying; but this much still seems right to me — that justice benefits friends and harms enemies. {{{ 334c }}}

But do you mean by friends those who seem to each person to be good, or those who really are, even if they don't seem so — and enemies in the same way?

It's natural, he said, to love those one considers good, and hate those one considers bad.

But don't people make mistakes about this, so that many seem good to them who are not, and many the opposite?

They do make mistakes.

Then for these people the good are enemies, and the bad are friends?

Certainly.

But is it nevertheless just for them, in that case, to benefit the {{{ 334d }}} wicked and harm the good?

It appears so.

But surely the good are just, and the sort not to do wrong?

True.

Then by your account it is just to treat badly those who do no wrong.

Not at all, Socrates, he said; that argument seems to me a bad one.

Then is it just, I said, to harm the unjust and benefit the just?

That looks better than the other one.

Then, Polemarchus, it will follow for many people — {{{ 334e }}} everyone who has misjudged others — that it is just to harm their friends, since to them their friends are wicked, and to benefit their enemies, since to them their enemies are good; and so we shall be saying the very opposite of what we said Simonides meant.

Yes indeed, he said, that is exactly how it works out. But let us change our position; for it looks as though we set down wrongly who the friend is and who the enemy.

Set them down how, Polemarchus?

That the one who seems good is a friend.

Then how, I said, should we change it now?

The one who both seems good and is good is a friend, he said; {{{ 335a }}} but the one who seems good but is not, only seems to be a friend, and is not one. And the same holds for an enemy.

So on this account, it seems, the good man will be a friend, and the wicked man an enemy.

Yes.

So you are telling us to add something to what we said at first, when we said it was just to treat a friend well and an enemy badly; and now, on top of that, we are to say that it is just to treat a friend well when he is good, and to harm an enemy when he is bad? {{{ 335b }}}

Yes, certainly, he said; that seems to me the right way to put it.

Is it then, I said, the mark of a just man to harm any human being at all?

Certainly, he said; the wicked, at least, and one's enemies, must be harmed.

And when horses are harmed, do they become better or worse?

Worse.

Worse with respect to the virtue of dogs, or of horses?

Of horses.

And when dogs are harmed, don't they become worse with respect to the virtue of dogs, and not of horses?

Necessarily. {{{ 335c }}}

And shall we not say the same of human beings, my friend — that when they are harmed, they become worse with respect to human virtue?

Yes, certainly.

But is justice not a human virtue?

That too must be granted.

Then it follows, my friend, that human beings who are harmed necessarily become more unjust.

So it seems.

Well then, can musicians make people unmusical by music?

Impossible.

Or can horsemen make people bad riders by horsemanship?

No, they cannot.

Then can the just, by justice, make people unjust? Or, {{{ 335d }}} in short, can the good, by virtue, make people bad?

No, that is impossible.

For it is not the function of heat, I take it, to cool, but of its opposite.

Yes.

Nor of dryness to moisten, but of its opposite.

Certainly.

Nor is it the function of the good to harm, but of its opposite.

So it appears.

And the just man is good?

Certainly.

So it is not the function of the just man, Polemarchus, to harm anyone — not a friend, not anyone else — but of his opposite, the unjust man.

You seem to me to be entirely right, Socrates, he said. {{{ 335e }}}

So if someone says that justice is giving to each what is owed, and means by this that harm is owed to enemies by the just man, and benefit to friends, then whoever said this was not wise. For he was not telling the truth; nowhere has it appeared to us that harming anyone is just.

I agree, he said.

Then you and I, I said, will fight side by side, if anyone claims that Simonides said it, or Bias, or Pittacus, or any other of the wise and blessed men.

I, at any rate, he said, am ready to join in the fight. {{{ 336a }}}

But do you know, I said, whose saying I believe this is — that it is just to benefit friends and harm enemies?

Whose? So he asked.

I think it belongs to Periander, or Perdiccas, or Xerxes, or Ismenias the Theban, or some other rich man who thought himself powerful.

Very true, he said.

Well then, I said; since it has turned out that this is not what justice is, nor the just, what else might one say it is?
