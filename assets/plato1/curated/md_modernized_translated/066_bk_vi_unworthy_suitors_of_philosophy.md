## Unworthy Suitors of Philosophy

These men, then, falling away in this manner — the very men to whom she belongs most {{{ 495c }}} — leave philosophy deserted and unwed, and themselves live a life neither fitting nor true, while she, like an orphan bereft of her kin, is invaded by others unworthy of her, who disgrace her and fasten reproaches on her, the very reproaches you yourself say the revilers bring, that of those who keep her company some are worth nothing, and most of them deserve a great many ills.

Yes indeed, he said, that is exactly what is said.

With good reason, I said. For other little men, seeing this ground growing empty, yet full of fine names {{{ 495d }}} and pretensions, gladly leap out of their crafts into philosophy — just as men who escape from prison flee into a temple — those, that is, who happen to be the cleverest at their own petty craft. For all the same, even with philosophy in such a state, her prestige remains grander than that of the other crafts, and it is this that many men reach for — men stunted in their natures, who, just as their bodies have been maimed by their crafts and their trades, so too {{{ 495e }}} have had their souls broken and battered by their menial labor — or is that not inevitable?

Very much so, he said.

Do you think, then, I said, that it is any different to see them from seeing some bald little smith who has come into money — lately freed from his fetters, washed at the baths, wearing a brand-new cloak, got up like a bridegroom, about to marry his master's daughter because of his master's poverty and want of friends? {{{ 496a }}}

It is not at all different, he said.

What sort of things, then, are such men likely to beget? Not bastard and paltry ones?

Quite inevitable.

What then? When men unworthy of education draw near to her and consort with her unworthily, what sort of thoughts and opinions shall we say they beget? Is it not, in truth, fitting to call them sophistries, containing nothing genuine, and touching nothing [worthy] of true practical wisdom?

Absolutely, he said.

A very small remnant, then, I said, Adeimantus, is left {{{ 496b }}} of those who keep company with philosophy as she deserves — perhaps some noble and well-bred character, overtaken by exile, who for lack of corrupters has stayed true to his nature and remained with her; or when a great soul is born in a small city and, thinking the city's affairs beneath it, looks past them; and here and there some naturally gifted man might come to her from another craft, having rightly come to despise it. And there might also be the bridle of our friend Theages, one fit to hold a man back; for in Theages's case {{{ 496c }}} everything else stands ready for his falling away from philosophy, but the poor health of his body, barring him from political life, holds him fast. As for my own case — the divine sign — it is not worth mentioning; for it has come to perhaps one other person, or to no one at all, among those before me. And those who have come to belong to this small number, and have tasted how sweet and blessed a possession philosophy is, and who have, moreover, seen well enough the madness of the many, and that, one might almost say, no one does anything sound concerning the business of cities, nor is there {{{ 496d }}} any ally with whom a man might go to the aid of justice and come through alive, but rather, like a man who has fallen among wild beasts, neither willing to share in their wrongdoing nor able, alone, to hold out against all their savagery, he would perish before he could do any good to his city or his friends, and so prove useless to himself and to others — taking all this into account by reasoning, he keeps quiet and attends to his own affairs, as one who, in a storm of dust and hail driven before the wind, steps aside behind a wall, and, seeing the others filled with lawlessness, is content if somehow he himself can remain pure of injustice and unholy {{{ 496e }}} deeds, both living out his life here and, when his release from it comes, departing with fair hope, gracious and kindly.
