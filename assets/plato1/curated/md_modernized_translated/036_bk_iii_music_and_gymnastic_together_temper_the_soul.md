## Music and Gymnastic Together Temper the Soul

{{{ 410b }}} Then will not the musical man, if he wishes, catch gymnastic too by following these same tracks, so that he has no need of medicine beyond what is unavoidable?

I think so.

And as for the exercises and hardships themselves, he will engage in them with an eye to the spirited element of his nature and to rousing it, rather than for strength, not treating diet and toil the way other athletes do, for the sake of bodily power.

Quite right, he said.

Then, I said, Glaucon, do not those who establish {{{ 410c }}} education through music and gymnastic do so not for the reason some suppose, that the body might be tended by the one and the soul by the other?

Well, what else? He said.

They are likely, I said, to establish both mainly for the sake of the soul.

How so?

Do you not notice, I said, how the mind itself is disposed in those who consort with gymnastic throughout life but never touch music? Or again, how those who are disposed the opposite way turn out?

What do you mean, he asked? {{{ 410d }}}

Savagery and hardness, I said, and again softness and gentleness—

I do, he said — because those who indulge in unmixed gymnastic turn out more savage than is proper, while those who give themselves to music turn softer than is good for them.

And indeed, I said, it is the spirited part of one's nature that would produce savagery — rightly reared, it would be brave, but stretched more tightly than it should, it would naturally turn hard and harsh.

I think so, he said. {{{ 410e }}}

And what about this? Would not the philosophic nature possess gentleness — softer than it should be if let too slack, but gentle and well-ordered if reared well?

That is so.

And we say that the guardians must have both these natures.

Yes, they must.

Then must they not be attuned to one another?

How could they not?

And is not the soul of one who is well attuned moderate and brave? {{{ 411a }}}

Certainly.

And the soul of one who is not attuned, cowardly and boorish?

Very much so.

So when someone lets music pipe into his soul and pour through it, through his ears as through a funnel, those sweet and soft and plaintive harmonies we were just now speaking of, and spends his whole life humming and delighting in song, such a man, at first, if he had any spirit in him, softens it like iron and makes it useful {{{ 411b }}} instead of useless and hard; but when he does not let up but keeps charming it without relief, from that point on he melts and dissolves it away, until he has melted out the spirit entirely and cut the sinews from his soul, as it were, and made a soft spearman.

Quite so, he said.

And if, I said, he takes hold of a spiritless nature from the start, he accomplishes this quickly; but if the nature is spirited, by weakening its spirit he makes it prone to sudden swings, easily provoked {{{ 411c }}} and easily quenched by small things. So instead of being spirited, they become irritable and short-tempered, full of peevishness.

Exactly so.

And what if, in turn, he toils much at gymnastic and feasts very well, but does not touch music or philosophy? Does he not, first, being in good bodily condition, become filled with high spirit and confidence, and grow braver than he was before?

Very much so.

But what happens when he does nothing else and has no share {{{ 411d }}} in the Muse at all? Does not whatever love of learning was in his soul, since it tastes no study and inquires into nothing, and takes no part in reasoned discourse or the rest of music, become weak and deaf and blind, since it is neither roused nor nourished, and his perceptions are never purified?

So it is, he said.

Such a man, I think, becomes a hater of reasoned discourse and unmusical, and no longer makes any use of persuasion through argument, but goes about everything by force and {{{ 411e }}} savagery like a wild beast, and lives in ignorance and clumsiness, without rhythm and without grace.

That is altogether so, he said.

Then since these two things exist, it seems, I would say some god has given two crafts to mankind — music and gymnastic — directed at the spirited part and the philosophic part, not at soul and body, except incidentally, but at those two, so that {{{ 412a }}} they might be attuned to each other, stretched and slackened to the proper degree.

So it seems, he said.

Then the man who blends gymnastic with music most beautifully and brings them to bear on the soul in the best measure — him we would most rightly call the most fully musical and best attuned, far more than the man who tunes strings together with one another.

With good reason, Socrates, he said.

Then in our city too, Glaucon, will there not always be need of some such overseer, if the constitution is to be preserved?
