## Education Begins: The Tales We Tell

Let us posit it, he said.

Then the one who is to become a fine and good guardian of the city will be philosophical, spirited, quick, and strong by nature.

Absolutely, he said.

That, then, is how he would start out. But in what way will these men be reared and educated by us? And is it of any use to us, in examining this, for catching sight of the very thing for the sake of which {{{ 376d }}} we examine everything — in what way justice and injustice arise in a city? The aim is not to pass over an argument that serves, nor to drag out a long one.

And Glaucon's brother said, Certainly — I for one expect this inquiry to be of use toward that very end.

By Zeus, I said, my dear Adeimantus, then it must not be abandoned, even if it happens to be rather long.

No, indeed not.

Come then — as if telling a story and with leisure to spare, let us educate the men in our discussion. {{{ 376e }}}

Yes, we must.

What then is education? Or is it hard to find one better than that discovered over a long span of time? There is, I suppose, gymnastic for bodies, and music for the soul.

There is indeed.

Then shall we not begin our education with music rather than gymnastic?

How could we not?

Under music, I said, do you include stories, or not?

I do.

And there are two kinds of stories, one true, the other false?

Yes. {{{ 377a }}}

And must the children be educated in both, but first in the false ones?

I don't understand, he said, what you mean.

Don't you understand, I said, that we tell tales to children first? That, generally speaking, is false, though there is some truth in it too. And we make use of tales with children before physical exercises.

That is so.

This, then, is what I meant, that music must be taken up before gymnastic.

Correctly, he said.

Then don't you know that the beginning is the most important part of any task, especially {{{ 377b }}} for anything young and tender? For that is when it is most malleable, and it takes on whatever stamp one wishes to impress on it.

Quite so.

Then shall we so casually allow the children to hear any chance tales, made up by anyone who happens along, and to take into their souls opinions for the most part opposite to those which, once they are grown, we shall think they ought to hold?

We shall not allow it in any way at all.

First then, it seems, we must oversee the tale-makers, {{{ 377c }}} and if they compose a good [tale], it must be approved, but if not, it must be rejected. And the approved ones we shall persuade the nurses and mothers to tell to the children, shaping their souls with these tales far more than they shape their bodies with their hands — and of the tales they now tell, most must be thrown out.

Which ones? So he asked.

In the greater tales, I said, we shall also see the lesser ones. For the greater and the lesser alike must surely share the same stamp {{{ 377d }}} and have the same effect. Or don't you think so?

I do, he said — but I don't even know which ones you mean by the greater tales.

Those, I said, that Hesiod and Homer told us, and the other poets too. For these men, I suppose, put together false tales and told them to people, and still do.

Which ones, he said, and what fault do you find with them?

The very fault, I said, that must be found first and foremost, especially when someone lies badly.

What is that? {{{ 377e }}}

Whenever someone represents badly, in his account, [the nature] of gods and heroes, what sort of beings they are, like a painter whose painting resembles nothing of what he meant to portray.

Yes indeed, he said, it is right to find fault with things of that sort. But how exactly do we mean this, and which tales?

First, I said, whoever told the greatest lie, about the greatest matters, did not lie well — how Uranus did what Hesiod says he did, and how Cronus {{{ 378a }}} in turn took his revenge on him. As for the deeds of Cronus, and what he in turn suffered at the hands of his son, I would not think it right for these to be told so casually to the thoughtless and the young, even if they were true, but rather that they should above all be passed over in silence, and if there were some necessity to tell it, that it be heard by as few as possible under a bond of secrecy, after sacrificing not a pig but some great and scarce offering, so that the fewest possible might chance to hear it.

Yes indeed, he said, these tales are harsh. {{{ 378b }}}

And they must not be told, I said, Adeimantus, in our city. Nor should it be said to a young listener that in committing the utmost injustice he would be doing nothing to wonder at, nor again that in punishing an unjust father by every means he would only be doing what the first and greatest of the gods did.

No, by Zeus, he said, it does not seem fitting to me either to say such things.

Nor, I said, should it be said at all that gods make war on gods, {{{ 378c }}} plot against one another, and fight — for that isn't even true — if indeed those who are to guard our city must think it most shameful to quarrel with one another lightly; far from it, battles of giants must not be told to them as tales, nor embroidered, nor the many and varied other feuds of gods and heroes against their own kin — but rather, if we are somehow to persuade them that no citizen has ever hated another, and that to do so is not holy, such things must instead be told {{{ 378d }}} to the children from the very start by old men and old women alike, and, as they grow older, the poets must be compelled to compose stories close to this pattern for them too. And the binding of Hera by her son, and the hurling down of Hephaestus by his father when he was about to defend his mother as she was being beaten, and all the battles of the gods that Homer has composed — none of these are to be admitted into our city, whether they are composed with hidden meanings or without them. For a young person is not able to judge what is a hidden meaning and what is not; rather, whatever opinions they form in their souls at that age tend to become hard to erase {{{ 378e }}} and difficult to change — and this, perhaps, is why it matters most of all that the first stories they hear be told in the finest way possible, with virtue in mind.

That makes sense, he said. But if someone were to ask us again just what these are, and which tales we mean, what would we say?

And I said, Adeimantus, you and I are not poets {{{ 379a }}} at present, but founders of a city; and it befits founders to know the patterns within which the poets must tell their tales, and not to permit them to compose contrary to these, though they need not compose the tales themselves.
