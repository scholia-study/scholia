## Cephalus on Old Age

And Glaucon said, it seems we must stay.

Well, if you think so, I said, that is what we must do.

So we went to Polemarchus's house, and there we found Lysias and Euthydemus, Polemarchus's brothers, and also Thrasymachus of Chalcedon, Charmantides of Paeania, and Cleitophon son of Aristonymus; and Polemarchus's father Cephalus was there too. And he seemed to me quite old now — it had been a long time {{{ 328c }}} since I had last seen him. He was sitting, crowned with a wreath, on a sort of cushioned stool, for he had just finished a sacrifice in the courtyard. So we sat down beside him, since there were some stools set out there in a circle.

As soon as Cephalus saw me, he greeted me and said, Socrates, you don't come down to see us in the Piraeus very often. Yet you should. If I still had the strength to make the walk to town easily, there would be no need for you to come {{{ 328d }}} here — we would come to you instead; but as it is, you must come here more often. You may be sure that, for me, the more the other pleasures of the body wither away, the more the desires and pleasures of conversation grow. So do not do otherwise — keep company with these young men, and come around to us often, as to friends who are truly your own.

Indeed, I said, Cephalus, I really do enjoy talking {{{ 328e }}} with the very old, for it seems to me we ought to learn from them, as from people who have already traveled a road that we too will perhaps have to travel, what sort of road it is — rough and hard, or easy and smooth. And in particular I would gladly hear from you how it looks to you, now that you have reached the time of life the poets call the threshold of old age — whether it is a hard stretch of life, or how you yourself would describe it. {{{ 329a }}}

By Zeus, Socrates, he said, I will tell you just how it looks to me. A number of us who are about the same age often meet together, keeping up the old saying — and most of us, when we get together, complain, missing the pleasures of our youth and remembering sex, drinking, feasting, and all the other things that go along with them, and they take it hard, as if they had been robbed of something great, and as if they had lived well then but are not even living now. {{{ 329b }}} Some also grieve over the disrespect old age brings from their own families, and on that account they go on and on about all the troubles old age has caused them. But it seems to me, Socrates, that they are not blaming the real cause. For if old age itself were the cause, I too would have felt exactly the same, on account of my age alone, and so would everyone else who has reached this time of life. But as it is, I have met others who do not feel this way — and in particular I was once present when someone asked the poet Sophocles, {{{ 329c }}} "Sophocles, how do things stand with you where sex is concerned? Are you still able to have intercourse with a woman?" And he replied, "Hush, man — I was only too glad to escape it, like escaping from a raving, savage master." I thought he had put it well then, and I think so no less now. For with respect to such things there comes, in old age, a great peace and freedom — once the desires stop straining and slacken off, it is exactly as Sophocles said: {{{ 329d }}} one is rid of a great many masters, and mad ones at that. But of these matters, and of one's dealings with one's own family too, there is a single cause — not old age, Socrates, but a person's character. For if people are orderly and easygoing, old age too is only moderately burdensome; but if not, then both old age and youth turn out hard for that kind of person, Socrates.

And I, admiring him for saying this and wanting him to go on {{{ 329e }}} talking, pressed him further and said, Cephalus, I think most people, when you say this, do not accept it, but suppose that you bear old age easily not because of your character but because you possess a great deal of property — for the rich, they say, have many consolations.
