## Reading the Cave: The Turning of the Soul

This image, then, I said, dear Glaucon, {{{ 517b }}} must be attached, the whole of it, to what was said before — likening the region revealed through sight to the dwelling of the prison, and the light of the fire within it to the power of the sun; and if you take the upward ascent and the viewing of what is above as the soul's journey up into the intelligible place, you will not be wide of my own expectation, at any rate, since that is what you are eager to hear. God, I suppose, knows whether it happens to be true. What appears to me, at any rate, appears like this: in the realm of the knowable, the {{{ 517c }}} form of the good is seen last, and only with difficulty, but once seen, one must conclude that it is, for all things, the cause of everything right and beautiful — in the visible realm giving birth to light and to the lord of light, and being itself sovereign in the intelligible realm, providing truth and intellect — and that whoever intends to act sensibly, whether in private or in public, must see it.

I think so too, he said, so far as I am able.

Come, then, I said, join me in believing this too, and do not be surprised that those who have reached that point are unwilling to engage in human affairs, but their souls are always eager to spend their time above — {{{ 517d }}} for that is reasonable, I suppose, if indeed this too holds according to the image we described before.

That is reasonable, he said.

But what about this? Do you think it at all surprising, I said, if someone who has come from divine contemplations to human evils behaves awkwardly and looks quite ridiculous, still dim-sighted, and before he has become sufficiently accustomed to the darkness around him is forced to contend in the courts, or somewhere else, about the shadows of justice, or about the statues of which they are the shadows, and to dispute {{{ 517e }}} about how these things are understood by people who have never yet seen justice itself?

Not at all surprising, he said. {{{ 518a }}}

But anyone with intellect, I said, would remember that disturbances of the eyes are of two kinds and come from two causes — when they pass from light into darkness, and from darkness into light. And if he thought that the same things happen to the soul too, then whenever he saw a soul disturbed and unable to see something clearly, he would not laugh unthinkingly, but would consider whether, coming from a brighter life, it has been darkened through unfamiliarity, or whether, coming from greater ignorance into a brighter place, it has been filled with a dazzling {{{ 518b }}} glare — and so he would count the one happy for its condition and its life, and pity the other, and if he wanted to laugh at it, his laughter would be less absurd than laughter at the soul that has come down from above, out of the light.

You put that very reasonably, he said.

We must, then, I said, think something like this about these matters, if this is true: that education is not what it is claimed to be by those who profess to teach it. They say, I suppose, that although knowledge is not present in the {{{ 518c }}} soul, they put it in — as if putting sight into blind eyes.

So they do say, he said.

But the present argument, I said, indicates that this power is already present in each person's soul, and also the instrument by which each person comes to understand — just as, if the eye could not turn from darkness toward brightness except together with the whole body, so too it must be turned around, together with the whole soul, away from becoming, until it becomes able to bear looking at being, and at the brightest part of being; and this, we say, is the {{{ 518d }}} good. Isn't that so?

Yes.

Of this, then, I said, there would be a craft — the craft of this turning around, of how it will be turned most easily and most effectively — not a craft of implanting sight in it, but one that, since it already has sight but is not turned the right way and is not looking where it should, contrives to bring this about.

So it seems, he said.

The other things called virtues of the soul, then, do look to be close to those of the body—for in truth they are {{{ 518e }}} not present beforehand, but are later implanted by habits and exercises—but the virtue of thought, it seems, turns out to be something more divine than anything else, and it never loses its power, but through the turning around becomes useful and beneficial, {{{ 519a }}} or, again, useless and harmful. Or have you not noticed yet, concerning people said to be wicked but clever, how keenly their little soul sees, and how sharply it makes out the things it has turned toward, since its sight is not poor at all, but is forced to serve vice — so that the sharper it sees, the more evils it works?

Certainly, he said.

This, however, I said — this element of such a nature — if it were hammered at from childhood and so cut free of the leaden weights, akin to {{{ 519b }}} becoming, which grow onto it through eating and pleasures and gluttonies of that kind and turn the soul's vision downward — if, released from these, it were turned around toward the things that are true, then this same faculty in these same people would see those things most sharply, just as it now sees the things it is turned toward.
