## Injustice Breeds Faction

I remember, he said; but even what you're saying now doesn't please me, and I have things to say about it. So if I said them, I know well that {{{ 350e }}} you'd say I was making a speech. So either let me say as much as I want, or, if you'd rather ask questions, ask them; and I'll do for you what people do for old women telling their tales — I'll murmur along, nodding yes and shaking my head no.

Not against your own opinion, I said.

Then it will have to be as pleases you, he said, since you won't let me speak. And yet what else do you want?

Nothing, by Zeus, I said; but if that's what you're going to do, do it — I'll ask the questions.

Ask away, then.

This, then, is what I'm asking, the same thing as just now, so that we can go on and examine the argument {{{ 351a }}} in order — what justice actually turns out to be in relation to injustice. For it was said, I believe, that injustice is more capable and stronger than justice; but now, I said, if justice is wisdom and virtue, I think it will easily turn out to be stronger than injustice as well, since injustice is ignorance — no one could fail to see that any longer — though that's not at all the simple way I want to examine it, Thrasymachus, but rather like this: {{{ 351b }}} would you say that a city can be unjust, and that it tries to enslave other cities unjustly and has enslaved them, and holds many in subjection under itself, having reduced them to slavery?

How could it not? So he said. And the city that is best at it will do this most of all, being the most completely unjust.

I understand, I said, that this was your point. But here's what I'm considering about it: does the city that becomes more powerful than another city have this power apart from justice, or is it under some necessity to have it together with justice? {{{ 351c }}}

If it's as you were just saying, he said — justice being wisdom — then together with justice; but if it's as I was saying, together with injustice.

I admire that very much, I said, Thrasymachus, that you're not just nodding and shaking your head, but answering very well too.

That's because I'm doing you a favor, he said.

And a good favor it is; but do me this favor too, and tell me: do you think that a city, or an army, or a band of robbers, or thieves, or any other group that sets out together on some unjust venture, would be able to accomplish anything, if its members acted unjustly toward one another? {{{ 351d }}}

Certainly not, he said.

And what if they didn't act unjustly? Wouldn't they be more able to?

Very much so.

For injustice, Thrasymachus, produces faction, and hatred, and fighting among people, I take it, while justice produces unity of mind and friendship — isn't that so?

Let it be so, he said, so that I won't be at odds with you.

Good of you to do that, my excellent fellow. But tell me this: if it's the function of injustice to produce hatred wherever it's present, then when it arises among free people and slaves alike, won't it make them hate one another and be factious and unable to act together with {{{ 351e }}} one another?

Very much so.

And what if it arises between two people? Won't they be at odds, and hate each other, and become enemies both to one another and to the just?

They will, he said.

And if, my remarkable friend, injustice arises in a single person, will it really lose its own power, or will it have it no less?

Let it have it no less, he said.

So doesn't it appear to have some such power as this: that whatever it arises in — whether some city, or a family, or an army, or {{{ 352a }}} anything else whatever — it first makes that thing unable to act together with itself, because of being factious and at odds, and further makes it an enemy both to itself and to everything opposed to it, including the just? Isn't that so?

Very much so.

And in a single person too, I imagine, being present in him, it will produce these very same effects it's naturally suited to produce: first it will make him unable to act, being factious and at odds with himself, and then an enemy both to himself and to the just — isn't that so?

Yes.

And the gods too are just, my friend, aren't they?

Let it be so, he said. {{{ 352b }}}

Then the unjust person will be an enemy to the gods too, Thrasymachus, while the just person will be their friend.

Feast on your argument, he said, and don't be shy about it; I certainly won't oppose you, so as not to make enemies of this company here.

Come then, I said, and finish out the rest of my feast for me by answering just as you are now. That the just appear wiser and better and more capable of acting, while the unjust are not even able {{{ 352c }}} to act together with one another — indeed, when we say that some people, being unjust, have ever forcefully accomplished anything together in common, we are not speaking entirely truly, for they would not have kept their hands off one another if they were thoroughly unjust, but clearly there was some justice in them, which kept them from wronging one another as well as those they were jointly moving against, and it was through that justice that they accomplished what they did accomplish, though they set out on their unjust deeds only half-corrupted by injustice, since those who are utterly wicked and completely unjust are also completely unable to act — this, then, {{{ 352d }}} is how I understand things to stand, and not as you first laid it down; but whether the just also live better than the unjust and are happier, which is what we proposed to examine afterward, still needs to be examined. Well, they do appear so even now, at least as it seems to me, from what we've said; all the same, it must still be examined better. For our argument is not about just any chance matter, but about the way one ought to live.
