## The First City and Its Needs

{{{ 369b }}} Very much so.

Does it seem, then, that we should undertake to carry this through? For I think it is no small task — so give it your consideration.

It has been considered, said Adeimantus — so do not do otherwise.

A city comes into being, then, I said, as I imagine, since each of us happens to be not self-sufficient, but ⟨is⟩ in need of many things — or what other origin do you suppose founds a city?

None, he said. {{{ 369c }}}

So it is, then, that one person joins another — one for this need, another for that — and, since we need many things, we gather many people together into a single settlement as partners and helpers, giving this common dwelling the name city; isn't that right?

Quite so.

Does one person, then, give a share to another — if he gives anything — or receive a share, believing it is better for himself?

Certainly.

Come, then, I said, let us build a city in speech, starting from the beginning — and it seems our need will be what builds it.

How could it not? {{{ 369d }}}

And surely the first and greatest of our needs is the provision of food, so that we may exist and live at all.

Yes, absolutely.

Second comes the need for shelter, and third for clothing and things of that kind.

So it is.

Come, then, I said, how will the city provide for so much? Won't there be one man who is a farmer, another a builder, and another a weaver? Or shall we add a shoemaker as well, or someone else to look after the needs of the body?

Certainly.

So the most basic city would consist of four or five men. {{{ 369e }}}

So it appears.

What then? Must each of these men contribute his own work as something common to all — the farmer, for instance, being one man, preparing food for four and spending four times the time and labor on producing food, and sharing it with the others — or should he instead take no notice of them and produce only a fourth of this {{{ 370a }}} food for himself alone in a fourth of the time, spending the other three parts of his time, one on providing a house, another on a cloak, another on shoes, sparing himself the trouble of dealings with others, and doing his own work by himself for himself?

And Adeimantus said, But perhaps, Socrates, this way is easier than the other.

By Zeus, that's not strange at all, I said. For now that you've said it, I realize myself that, to begin with, each of us is born not quite {{{ 370b }}} like every other, but different in nature, one man fitted for one job and another for another. Or don't you think so?

I do.

Well then? Would one man do finer work practicing many crafts, or when he practices just one?

When he practices one alone, he said.

And surely this too is clear, I think — if a man lets the right moment for some task pass him by, the work is ruined.

Clear enough.

For I do not think the task waits upon the leisure of the man performing it — rather he must {{{ 370c }}} follow closely upon what is being done, not treat it as some incidental matter.

Necessarily.

So it is from this that each thing is produced in greater abundance, and better, and more easily, when one man does one thing in accordance with his nature, at the right time, and is free from other occupations.

Absolutely so.

So then, Adeimantus, we need more than four citizens for the provisions we mentioned. For the farmer, it seems, will not make his own plow, if it's to be a good one, {{{ 370d }}} nor a hoe, nor any of the other tools used in farming. Nor will the builder, either — he too needs many things. And the same goes for the weaver and the shoemaker; or is that not so?

True.

So carpenters, smiths, and many other craftsmen of that kind become partners with us in our little town, and make it a sizable place.

Quite so.

But it would still not be very large, even if we added to them cowherds and shepherds and other herdsmen, so that {{{ 370e }}} the farmers might have oxen to plow with, the builders, together with the farmers, draft animals for hauling, and the weavers and shoemakers hides and wool.

Nor would it be a small city, he said, that has all these things.

But then again, I said, to found the city itself in a place where it will need no imports is close to impossible.

Yes, impossible.

So it will need still others besides, who will bring it what it needs from another city.

It will.

And surely, if our agent goes empty-handed, carrying none of what the people he deals with need {{{ 371a }}} in return for what we need from them, he will come back empty-handed. Isn't that so?

So it seems to me.

So we must produce at home not only enough for ourselves, but also goods of the kind and quantity that those others need.

Yes, we must.

So our city needs more farmers and more of the other craftsmen too.

Yes, more.

And it will need other agents too, presumably — those who will import and export each commodity. And these are merchants, are they not?

Yes.

So we will need merchants too.

Certainly.

And if the trade is carried on by sea, {{{ 371b }}} it will need a great many others besides, skilled in the business of seafaring.

Yes, a great many indeed.

And what about this? Within the city itself, how will they share with one another what each produces? It was for the sake of just this, in fact, that we formed a partnership and founded the city in the first place.

Clearly, he said, by selling and buying.

So a marketplace will arise for us from this, and money as a token for exchange.

Certainly. {{{ 371c }}}

So if the farmer brings something he has produced to the marketplace, or some other craftsman does, and does not arrive at the same time as those who want to exchange something with him, will he sit idle in the marketplace, away from his own work?

Not at all, he said, but there are those who, seeing this, set themselves to this service — in cities that are rightly governed, generally those who are weakest in body and useless for any other work. For they must {{{ 371d }}} stay right there in the marketplace, giving money in exchange for goods to anyone who needs to sell, and in turn giving goods in exchange for money to anyone who needs to buy.

This need, then, I said, brings retailers into being for us in the city. Or do we not give the name retailers to those who station themselves in the marketplace to serve buying and selling, and the name merchants to those who travel from city to city?

Certainly. {{{ 371e }}}

And there are, I think, still other agents as well — those who are not quite fit to be partners in matters of the mind, but have bodily strength enough for hard labor; these, then, selling the use of their strength, and calling the price for it a wage, are called, I believe, wage-earners — isn't that so?

Certainly.

So wage-earners too, it seems, make up part of the city's full complement.

I think so.

Has our city, then, Adeimantus, now grown to the point of being complete?

Perhaps.

Where, then, could justice and injustice ever be found in it? And alongside which of the things we've considered does it arise? {{{ 372a }}}

I myself, he said, cannot see it, Socrates — unless it lies somewhere in these people's need of one another.

But perhaps, I said, you are right — and we must look into it, and not shrink from the task.

So let us first consider in what manner these people, thus provided for, will live. Doing nothing other than producing grain, wine, clothing, and shoes? And once they have built houses, for the most part in summer they will work naked and unshod, and in {{{ 372b }}} winter suitably clothed and shod — and they will feed themselves by preparing barley-groats from their barley and flour from their wheat, baking some and kneading others, setting out fine loaves and cakes on a reed or on clean leaves, and, reclining on beds strewn with yew and myrtle, they will feast, themselves and their children together, drinking their wine, wearing garlands, singing hymns to the gods, enjoying one another's company happily, and not having {{{ 372c }}} more children than their means allow, guarding against poverty or war.

And Glaucon broke in and said, It seems you are making these men feast without any relish at all.

You're right, I said. I forgot that they will also have a relish — salt, clearly, and olives and cheese, and they will boil onions and greens, the sort of things people boil in the country. And we will also set out for them, I suppose, desserts of figs, chickpeas, and beans, and they will roast myrtle-berries and acorns {{{ 372d }}} by the fire, drinking moderately as they do — and living their lives this way, in peace and good health, they will grow old and, in due course, hand down a similar life to their children.

And he said, But if you were founding a city of pigs, Socrates, what else would you fatten them on but this?

But then, Glaucon, I said, how should it be?

As is customary, he said — those who are not to live in discomfort should recline on couches, I think, and dine {{{ 372e }}} from tables, and have the relishes and desserts that people have nowadays too.
