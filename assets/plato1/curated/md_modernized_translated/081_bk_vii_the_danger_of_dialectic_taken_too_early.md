## The Danger of Dialectic Taken Too Early

What in particular? So he asked. {{{ 537e }}}

Don't you notice, I said, how great a harm is now arising in connection with dialectic?

What sort of harm? So he asked.

They get filled, I said, with lawlessness.

Very much so, he said.

Then do you think, I said, that what happens to them is anything to wonder at, and do you not forgive them?

In what way exactly? So he asked.

For example, I said, suppose a child who had been secretly substituted for another were reared amid great {{{ 538a }}} wealth and in a great and distinguished family, with many flatterers, and on becoming a man he perceived that he was not the child of those claiming to be his parents, and could not find those who had truly begotten him — can you guess how he would be disposed toward the flatterers and toward those who had substituted him, both in the time when he did not know about the substitution, and again in the time when he did? Or do you want to hear me guess?

I do, he said.

I guess, then, I said, that during the time when he did not know the truth, he would honor his {{{ 538b }}} supposed father and mother and other kin more than the flatterers, would be less likely to look on while they went in need of anything, would be less likely to do or say anything unlawful toward them, and would be less disobedient to them in matters of importance than to the flatterers.

Likely enough, he said.

Once he became aware of the truth, then, I guess in turn that he would relax his honor and devotion toward them, and intensify it toward the flatterers, and would obey them far more than before {{{ 538c }}}, and would now live according to their ways, associating with them openly, and would care nothing at all for that father and the others accounted as his kin — unless he were quite decent by nature.

Everything you say, he said, is just what would happen. But how does this image apply to those who take hold of arguments?

Like this. We have, I suppose, beliefs from childhood about what is just and noble, in which we have been brought up as if by parents, obeying and honoring them.

We do. {{{ 538d }}}

So there are also other practices, opposite to these, having their own pleasures, which flatter our soul and draw it toward themselves, but do not persuade those who are in any degree decent — such people honor those ancestral things instead, and obey them.

That is so.

What then? I said — when a question comes to a man in that condition and asks him what the noble is, and when, after he has answered what he heard from the lawgiver, the argument refutes him, and by refuting him often and in many ways casts him down into the opinion that this thing is no more {{{ 538e }}} noble than shameful, and likewise about the just and the good and whatever he held most in honor, after this, what do you think he will do regarding honoring and obeying them?

He is bound, he said, to honor them no longer in the same way, nor to obey them.

So when, I said, he no longer regards these things as honorable and belonging to him as before, and does not find the truth, is there any other {{{ 539a }}} life he will reasonably turn to except the one that flatters?

There is not, he said.

He will seem, I think, to have become lawless instead of law-abiding.

Necessarily.

Is this experience, then, I said, likely for those who take up arguments in this way, and, as I was just saying, deserving of much forgiveness?

And of pity too, he said.

Then, so that this pity does not arise concerning your thirty-year-olds, must one take up arguments with caution in every way?

Very much so, he said. {{{ 539b }}}

Then is this not one major precaution, that they not taste of arguments while young? For I think it has not escaped you that when young men first get a taste of arguments, they misuse them as if for sport, always turning them to contradiction, and, imitating those who refute, themselves refute others in turn, delighting like puppies in dragging and tearing with argument at whoever is nearby.

Extremely so, he said.

So when they have refuted many themselves and have been refuted {{{ 539c }}} by many in turn, they fall violently and quickly into believing nothing of what they believed before — and from this, both they themselves and philosophy as a whole come into disrepute with everyone else.

Most true, he said.

But the older man, I said, will not be willing to share in such madness — he will imitate the one who is willing to engage in dialectic and examine the truth, rather than the one who plays and contradicts for the sake of amusement, and he himself will be more moderate {{{ 539d }}}, and will make the practice more honorable rather than less.

That is right, he said.

And were not all the things said earlier stated as a precaution about this very thing — that the natures to whom one imparts arguments should be orderly and steady, and not, as happens now, that any chance person with no qualification at all approaches it?

Certainly, he said.

Is it enough, then, to remain continuously and intensely engaged in the pursuit of arguments, doing nothing else, but training in a manner that mirrors the bodily exercises, for twice as many years as then? {{{ 539e }}}

Do you mean six, he said, or four?

Never mind, I said, set it at five. For after this they will have to be sent back down by you into that cave again, and compelled to hold command in matters of war and whatever offices belong to the young, so that they do not fall behind the others even in experience — and further, in these {{{ 540a }}} very tasks they must be tested to see whether they will stand firm when pulled in every direction, or whether they will be shaken somewhat.

And how long a time, he said, do you set for this?

Fifteen years, I said. And when they have turned fifty, those who have come through safely and excelled in every way, in practical affairs as well as in knowledge, must at last be led to the goal, and compelled to lift up the soul's beam of sight and look toward that very thing which provides light to everything, and having seen the good itself, using it as a model, they must each in turn order the city and private citizens {{{ 540b }}} and themselves for the remainder of their life, spending the greater part of it occupied with philosophy, but when their turn comes, toiling at public affairs and ruling, each for the city's sake, doing this not as something noble but as something necessary, and thus, having always trained up others like themselves, leaving them behind as guardians of the city, they will depart to dwell in the Isles of the Blessed — the city shall make monuments and sacrifices {{{ 540c }}} for them at public expense, if the Pythia also concurs, as to divine spirits, but if not, as to blessed and godlike men.

You have fashioned altogether admirable rulers, Socrates, he said, like a sculptor.

And the female rulers too, I said, Glaucon — for you must not think that what I have said applies to the men any more than to the women, whoever among them are born with natures equal to the task.

Quite right, he said, if indeed they are to share equally in everything with the men, as we described.
