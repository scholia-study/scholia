## Turning to the Soul: Is It One or Many?

{{{ 434d }}} It seems to me to be so and not otherwise, he said.

Let us not yet assert this too firmly, I said, but if this form, applied to each individual man, is agreed to be justice there as well, we shall concede it at once — for what else could we say? But if not, we shall then look into something else. Now let us carry through the examination we had in mind: that if we tried to study it first in something larger that possesses justice ⟨rather⟩ than there in the individual, we might more easily catch sight of what it is like in one man. And {{{ 434e }}} it seemed to us that this was the city, and so we founded it to be as good as we could make it, well aware that in a good city at least justice would be found. So let us carry back to the individual what appeared to us there, and if it is confirmed, well and good; but if something different shows up in the one, we shall go back again to the city and test it there, and {{{ 435a }}} perhaps, by examining the two side by side and rubbing them together, we might make justice flash out as if from fire-sticks, and once it has come to light we shall fix it firmly in our own minds.

That is the right approach, he said, and that is how we must proceed.

Now then, I said, is a thing that one would call by the same name, when it is larger and when it is smaller, unlike in that respect in which it is called the same, or alike?

Alike, he said. {{{ 435b }}}

Then a just man, so far as the form of justice itself is concerned, will not differ at all from a just city, but will be alike.

Alike, he said.

But the city, we agreed, was just when the three kinds of natures in it each did its own work, and moderate, courageous, and wise, in turn, through certain other affections and dispositions belonging to those same classes.

True, he said.

Then we shall claim the same for the individual too, my friend: that having these same {{{ 435c }}} forms in his own soul, he rightly deserves, because of the same affections as theirs, the same names as the city.

That is altogether necessary, he said.

Here again, my good man, I said, we have stumbled into a trifling question about the soul — whether it has these three forms in it or not.

It does not seem to me trifling at all, he said, for perhaps, Socrates, the saying is true that fine things are difficult.

So it appears, I said. And be well assured, Glaucon, that my {{{ 435d }}} opinion is this: we shall never grasp this with exactness by the kind of methods we are now using in our arguments — for the road that leads to it is another, longer and fuller one — but perhaps adequately enough, given what we have already said and considered.

Well, is that not enough to be content with? So he said — for me, at any rate, it would do well enough for the present.

But it will fully satisfy me too, I said.

Do not give up, then, he said, but keep looking. {{{ 435e }}}

Is it not then, I said, a great necessity for us to agree that the same forms and dispositions that are in the city are present also in each of us? For surely they cannot have gotten there from anywhere else. For it would be absurd to suppose that the spirited element in cities has come from anywhere but the private individuals who bear that reputation — such as those around Thrace and Scythia and practically the whole region to the north — or that love of learning, which one would {{{ 436a }}} most readily charge to our own region, or love of money, which one would say belongs above all to the Phoenicians and the Egyptians, has come from anywhere else.

Quite so, he said.

This, then, is so, I said, and there is nothing hard about seeing it.

Certainly not.

But here now is the difficult question: whether we do each of these things with this same part, or, there being three parts, with a different one for each — learning with one, feeling anger with another of the parts within us, and desiring, in turn, with some third part, the pleasures connected with nourishment and procreation and all {{{ 436b }}} that is akin to them — or whether we act with the whole soul in each of these cases whenever we set out to do them. These are the things that will be hard to define in a way that does justice to the argument.
