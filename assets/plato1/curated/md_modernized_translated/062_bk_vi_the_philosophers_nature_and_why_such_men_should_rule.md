## The Philosopher's Nature, and Why Such Men Should Rule

{{{ 484a }}} The philosophers, then, Glaucon, I said, and those who are not — only after going through a discussion of some length have we managed, with some difficulty, to bring to light who each of the two are.

Perhaps, he said, it is not easy to do briefly.

It does not seem so, I said — for my part, at least, it still seems to me that the matter would come out better if we needed to speak of this alone, without having to go through the many things still remaining, on our way to seeing how the life {{{ 484b }}} of the just man differs from that of the unjust.

What then, he asked, comes next for us?

What else, I said, than what follows? Since the philosophers are those capable of grasping what is always the same in the same respects, while those who cannot, but instead wander among things many and various, holding to them in every sort of way, are not philosophers, which of the two, then, must be the leaders of a city?

How then should we put it, he asked, to state the matter reasonably?

Whichever of the two, I said, prove capable of guarding the laws and pursuits of cities — these we should establish as {{{ 484c }}} guardians.

Rightly, he said.

And is this clear, I said — whether one ought to set a blind guardian or a keen-sighted one to watch over anything at all?

And how, he said, is that not clear?

Do they seem, then, to differ at all from the blind — those who are really deprived of the knowledge of each thing that is, who have no clear pattern in their soul, and who cannot look off, as painters do, to what is most true, and, by always referring back {{{ 484d }}} to it and studying it as exactly as they can, lay down in that way the customs here as well, about what is fine and just and good, if they need laying down, and guard and preserve those already laid down?

No, by Zeus, he said, not by much.

Shall we then set these up as guardians rather than those who have come to know each thing that is, and who fall short of them in nothing in point of experience, nor lag behind in any other part of virtue?

It would surely be absurd, he said, to choose others, if indeed they are not lacking in the rest — for they would be ahead in this very thing, which is just about the greatest of all. {{{ 485a }}}

Then should we not say this — in what way the same men will be able to have both those qualities and these?

Certainly.

Well then, as we were saying when we began this discussion, we must first come to understand their nature thoroughly — and I think, if we agree sufficiently about that, we shall also agree both that the same men are capable of having these qualities, and that none but these ought to be the leaders of cities.

How so?

Let this much be agreed among us {{{ 485b }}} about the natures of philosophers — that they always love that kind of learning which reveals to them something of that being which always is and is not driven to wander by becoming and destruction.

Let it be agreed.

And moreover, I said, that they love the whole of it, and give up no part of it willingly, whether smaller or greater, more honored or less honored — just as we went through before in the case of the lovers of honor and those given to erotic love.

You are right, he said.

Consider this next, then — whether those who are to be as we described must also have, in addition to {{{ 485c }}} this, something further in their nature.

What sort?

Truthfulness — never willingly to admit falsehood in any way, but to hate it, and to cherish truth.

That is likely, he said.

It is not merely likely, my friend, but wholly necessary — that anyone whose nature is erotically drawn to something should cherish everything akin and related to the beloved.

Rightly, he said.

Could you find anything more akin to wisdom than truth?

How could I? So he said.

Is it then possible for the same nature to be both philosophic and {{{ 485d }}} a lover of falsehood?

Not at all.

Then the one who is truly a lover of learning must, from earliest youth, strive after all truth as much as possible.

Absolutely.

But surely, when someone's desires incline strongly toward one thing, we know that in him they are the weaker toward everything else — like a stream channelled off in that direction.

Of course.

So in the man whose desires have flowed toward learning and everything of that sort, they would be concerned, I think, with the pleasure of the soul itself by itself, and would forsake those that come through the body — if he is a philosopher not in pretense but {{{ 485e }}} in truth.

That is a great necessity.

Such a man is moderate, moreover, and by no means fond of money — for the ends for which money is pursued at such great expense are ends it befits someone other than him to be in earnest about.

So it is. {{{ 486a }}}

And surely this too must be considered, whenever you are about to judge a nature as philosophic or not.

What is that?

Take care it does not escape you that it has a share of illiberality — for pettiness, I think, is the thing most opposed to a soul that is always to reach out for the whole and the all, both divine and human.

Most true, he said.

Then do you think that a mind possessed of magnificence and the contemplation of all time and all being could regard human life as something great?

Impossible, he said. {{{ 486b }}}

Then will not such a man also consider death nothing terrible?

Least of all.

A cowardly and illiberal nature, then, it seems, could have no share in true philosophy.

I do not think so.

What then? Could a man who is orderly, not fond of money, not illiberal, not boastful, and not cowardly, ever become hard to deal with or unjust?

He could not.

And this too you will examine, when you are considering whether a soul is philosophic or not, right from the time it is young — whether it is just and gentle, or hard to get on with and savage.

Certainly. {{{ 486c }}}

Nor, I think, will you leave this out either.

What is that?

Whether he learns easily or with difficulty. Or do you expect that anyone would ever come to love a thing well enough when the doing of it is painful to him and, for all his labor, he gets little done?

That could not happen.

And what if he were unable to retain anything he learned, being full of forgetfulness? Could he possibly be anything but empty of knowledge?

How could he?

Laboring, then, to no purpose, do you not think he will be forced in the end to hate both himself and that sort of activity?

How could he not? {{{ 486d }}}

Then let us never admit a forgetful soul among those adequately philosophic, but let us require that it have a good memory.

By all means.

But surely we would say that a nature which is unmusical and ungainly draws toward nothing else but disproportion.

Of course.

And do you think truth is akin to disproportion, or to proportion?

To proportion.

Then, besides the other qualities, let us look for a mind that is by nature proportioned and graceful, one whose native disposition will make it easy to lead toward the form of each thing that is.

How could it not? {{{ 486e }}}

What then? Do we seem to you, in any way, to have gone through things that are not necessary, or that fail to follow from one another, for a soul that is to have an adequate and complete share of what is? {{{ 487a }}}

Most necessary, he said.

Is there then any way you could find fault with such a pursuit, which no one could ever pursue adequately unless he were by nature possessed of a good memory, ease in learning, magnificence, grace, and were a friend and kinsman of truth, justice, courage, and moderation?

Not even Momus, he said, could find fault with that.

Well then, I said, once such men are brought to completion by education and age, would you not entrust the city to them alone?
