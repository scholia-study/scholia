## Medicine, Judges, and Lingering Illness

{{{ 406c }}} As you would expect, I said, from someone who does not know that Asclepius did not reveal this kind of medicine to his descendants out of ignorance or inexperience, but because he knew that in every well-ordered city each person has some task assigned to him which he must perform, and no one has the leisure to be sick and under treatment for his whole life. This is something we notice, absurdly enough, in the case of craftsmen, but fail to notice in the case of the rich and those who seem to be prosperous.

How so? He asked. {{{ 406d }}}

A carpenter, I said, when he is sick, expects to get a drug from the doctor that will make him vomit up the disease, or to be purged from below, or to be treated with cautery or the knife and so be rid of it; but if someone prescribes him a long regimen — wrapping his head in felt caps and all that goes with them — he quickly says that he has no leisure to be sick and that it is not worth living like that, attending to his illness and neglecting the work in front of him. And after that, having said {{{ 406e }}} goodbye to such a doctor, he goes back to his usual regimen, and once he has recovered his health he lives carrying on his own affairs; but if his body is not equal to bearing it, he dies and is rid of his troubles.

Yes, he said, for a person like that it does seem fitting to use medicine that way. {{{ 407a }}}

Is that, I said, because he had some work to do, and if he did not do it, life was not worth living?

Clearly, he said.

But the rich man, as we say, has no such work set before him, of a kind that makes life unlivable if he is forced to give it up.

No, that is certainly not said of him.

You have surely heard, I said, how Phocylides says that once a man already has a livelihood, he should practice virtue.

I think, he said, even before that too.

Let us not quarrel with him about this, I said, but let us instead teach ourselves whether the rich man {{{ 407b }}} must practice this, and life is not worth living for him if he does not — or whether nursing an illness is an impediment to the carpenter's attention to his craft, and to the other crafts, but no impediment at all to Phocylides' injunction.

Yes, by Zeus, he said. This excessive care of the body that goes beyond gymnastic is pretty much the biggest obstacle of all; for it makes trouble for household management, for military service, and for holding settled office in the city.

But the biggest thing is that it also makes any kind of learning difficult, along {{{ 407c }}} with reflection and private study, since it is forever suspecting some headache or dizziness and blaming philosophy for bringing it on, so that wherever virtue is practiced and tested by such study, this excessive care is a hindrance every time; for it always makes a person think he is sick, and, as if in labor, never stop worrying about his body.

That is likely, he said.

So shall we not say that Asclepius, knowing all this, revealed medicine for those who are healthy in body by nature and regimen but {{{ 407d }}} have some localized disease within them — treating this sort of constitution by expelling their illness with drugs and surgery, then prescribing their usual regimen so as not to damage their civic duties — but that for bodies diseased through and through, he did not attempt to prolong a wretched life by regimens that drain a little here and pour in a little there, nor to let such people beget children as sickly as themselves, but instead held that a man unable to live out his {{{ 407e }}} natural span should not be treated, on the ground that this profits neither himself nor the city?

You are making Asclepius out to be a statesman, he said.

Clearly, I said — and as for his sons, since he was that kind of man, {{{ 408a }}} do not you see how at Troy too they showed themselves good in battle, and practiced medicine in the way I mean? And surely you remember that for Menelaus too, from the wound Pandarus gave him—
+ they sucked out the blood and sprinkled soothing remedies on it,
but as to what he should eat or drink after that, they prescribed nothing more than they did for Eurypylus, since they thought the drugs were enough to heal men who, before their wounds, were healthy and {{{ 408b }}} disciplined in their regimen, even if it happened that they drank a posset on the spot, whereas for a man sickly and undisciplined by nature they thought life was of no benefit either to himself or to others, and that the craft should not exist for such people, nor should they be treated, not even if they were richer than Midas.

You are making Asclepius's sons out to be quite refined people, he said.

It is fitting, I said — and yet, disagreeing with us here, the tragic poets and Pindar say that Asclepius was the son of Apollo, but that, persuaded by gold, he healed a rich man who was already {{{ 408c }}} at the point of death, and that this is precisely why he was struck by lightning. But we, in accordance with what has been said before, will not believe them on both counts; rather, we will say that if he was a son of a god, he was not greedy for gain; but if he was greedy for gain, he was not a son of a god.

Quite right, he said, as far as that goes. But what do you say about this, Socrates? Should not we have good doctors in the city? And presumably the best such doctors would be those who {{{ 408d }}} have treated the most healthy people and the most sick ones, and likewise the best judges, in turn, would be those who have had dealings with natures of every kind.

Yes indeed, I said, I mean good ones. But do you know whom I consider such people?

I will, if you tell me, he said.

But I will try, I said; you, though, put two unlike matters into a single question.

How so? He asked.

Doctors, I said, would become most skilled if, starting from childhood, in addition to learning the craft they had dealings with as many and as diseased bodies as possible, and if they themselves suffered every {{{ 408e }}} illness and were not entirely healthy by nature. For I do not think they treat body with body — since otherwise it would never be possible for bodies themselves to be, or to become, bad — but rather soul treats body, and it is not possible for a soul that has become and remains bad to treat anything well.

Rightly said, he said. {{{ 409a }}}

But a judge, my friend, governs soul with soul, and it is not right for that soul to have been raised among wicked souls from youth and to have kept company with them, and to have gone through committing every injustice itself, so that it can shrewdly infer other people's wrongs from its own, the way one infers diseases of the body; rather, it must have grown up inexperienced in and untouched by bad character while still young, if it is to judge just matters soundly by being itself fine and good. That is exactly why decent people, while young, appear simple-minded and are easily deceived {{{ 409b }}} by unjust people, since they do not have within themselves examples that correspond to what the wicked feel.

Yes, indeed, he said, that is very much what happens to them.

For this very reason, I said, the good judge must be not young but old, a late learner of what injustice is like — not having perceived it as something native, present in his own soul, but having studied it over a long time as something foreign in foreign souls, so as to discern clearly what sort of evil it is by nature, making use of knowledge, not {{{ 409c }}} of experience of his own.

That kind of judge, he said, does seem to be a most noble one, at any rate.

And good too, I said, which is what you were asking; for whoever has a good soul is good. But that clever, suspicious fellow — who has himself done much wrong and thinks himself cunning and wise — whenever he is among people like himself, appears clever, being extremely wary and looking to the models within himself; but whenever he comes near good men, and men now older, he appears {{{ 409d }}} foolish in turn, distrusting at the wrong moments and failing to recognize a sound character, since he has no model of that kind in himself. But since he meets with wicked people more often than with decent ones, he ends up seeming wise rather than ignorant, both to himself and to others.

That is absolutely true, he said.

So then, I said, we should not look for that sort of person as our good and wise judge, but for the one described earlier; for wickedness could never come to know both virtue and itself, whereas virtue, as a nature is educated over time, will come to {{{ 409e }}} know both itself and wickedness together. So it is this person, as it seems to me, who becomes wise, not the bad one.

I think so too, he said.

So then, will not you legislate for the city both the kind of medicine we described and this kind of judicial practice together, ones that will care for {{{ 410a }}} those citizens who are well-formed in body and soul, but as for those who are not, will let die the ones who are so in body, while those who are badly formed and incurable in soul they will put to death themselves?

This has certainly been shown to be the best course, he said, both for the sufferers themselves and for the city.

And as for the young, I said, it is clear that they will be careful about ever needing to resort to the courts, so long as they make use of that simple musical education which we said produces moderation.

What else? He said.
