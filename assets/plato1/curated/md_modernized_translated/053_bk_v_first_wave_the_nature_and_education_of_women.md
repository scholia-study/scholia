## First Wave: The Nature and Education of Women

For people who are by nature and by education such as we have described, there is, in my opinion, no other correct possession and use of children and women than the one that follows the same impulse with which we first set out; and we undertook, I believe, in our discussion, to establish the men as guardians of a herd.

Yes. {{{ 451d }}}

Let us follow along, then, assigning a similar breeding and rearing, and let us consider whether that is fitting for us or not.

How? He said.

Like this. Do we think the females of the guardian dogs should join in guarding whatever the males guard, and hunt together with them, and do everything else in common, or should the females stay at home indoors as incapable, because of bearing and rearing the puppies, while the males labor and have all the care of the flocks? {{{ 451e }}}

Everything in common, he said, except that we treat the females as weaker and the males as stronger.

Is it possible, then, I said, to use some animal for the same tasks, if you do not give it the same rearing and education?

It is not possible.

If, then, we are going to use the women for the same tasks as the men, we must also teach them the same things. {{{ 452a }}}

Yes.

Now to the men, at least, music and gymnastic were given.

Yes.

Then these two crafts, and the things that concern war, must be given to the women as well, and used in the same way.

That is likely, from what you say, he said.

Perhaps, I said, measured against custom, much of what is now being said would appear ridiculous, if it is to be carried out as it is being described.

Very much so, he said.

What, I said, do you see as the most ridiculous of these things? Or is it clear that it is the women exercising naked in the wrestling schools with {{{ 452b }}} the men, not only the young ones, but even the older ones already, just like the old men in the gymnasiums, when, wrinkled and not pleasant to look at, they nonetheless love their exercise?

By Zeus, he said; it would indeed appear ridiculous, at least as things now stand.

Then, I said, since we have set out to speak, we must not fear the jibes of the wits — however many and of whatever kind they would make about such a change taking place, both concerning the gymnasiums {{{ 452c }}} and concerning music, and not least concerning the bearing of arms and the riding of horses.

You are right, he said.

But since we have begun to speak, we must go forward to the rough part of the law, begging these people not to do their own work but to be serious, and reminding them that it is not long since it seemed shameful and ridiculous to the Greeks, as it still does to most of the barbarians now, for men to be seen naked, and that when the Cretans first began the practice of gymnasiums, and then the Spartans, {{{ 452d }}} it was open to the wits of that time to make comedy of all these things. Or do you not think so?

I do.

But when, I believe, it appeared to those who practiced it that stripping was better than covering up all such things, then what looked ridiculous to the eyes faded away before what reason showed to be best; and this showed too that a man is foolish who thinks anything ridiculous other than the bad, and who, trying to raise a laugh, fixes his eyes on some sight as ridiculous other than that of the foolish {{{ 452e }}} and the bad, and who is in earnest about the beautiful with a target set up for himself other than that of the good.

Certainly, he said.

Must we not, then, first come to agreement about this concerning them, whether it is possible or not, and allow a dispute to anyone, playful or serious, who wishes to dispute it: whether {{{ 453a }}} the female human nature is capable of sharing with the male sex in all its works, or in none at all, or in some but not others, and to which of these the business of war belongs? Would not someone who began most finely in this way also, in all likelihood, end most finely?

Very much so, he said.

Do you want us, then, I said, to argue against ourselves on behalf of the other side, so that the case for that position is not besieged with no one to defend it? {{{ 453b }}}

Nothing prevents it, he said.

Let us then say on their behalf: Socrates and Glaucon, there is no need for others to dispute against you; for you yourselves, at the beginning of the founding of the city you were settling, agreed that each single person must do the one work that is his own according to nature.

We did agree, I believe; how could we not?

Is there any way, then, that a woman does not differ very greatly from a man in nature?

How could she not differ?

Then is it not fitting to assign a different work to each of the two, {{{ 453c }}} the work that accords with his or her own nature?

Of course.

How, then, are you not now making a mistake and contradicting yourselves, when you claim, in turn, that the men and the women must do the same things, though they have natures set apart as widely as can be? Will you have anything to say in your defense against this, my remarkable friend?

Offhand, he said, it is not at all easy; but I will need you, and I do need you now, to interpret the argument on our behalf, whatever it is.

These, Glaucon, I said, and many other such things, {{{ 453d }}} are what I long foresaw and feared, and why I hesitated to touch the law concerning the possession and rearing of women and children.

No, by Zeus, he said; for it does not seem an easy matter.

No, it is not, I said. But the matter stands like this: whether one falls into a small swimming pool or into the middle of the largest sea, one swims none the less.

Certainly.

Then we too must swim and try to save ourselves out of the argument, hoping either for some dolphin to take us up or for some other rescue against all odds. {{{ 453e }}}

So it seems, he said.

Come then, I said, let us see if we can find the way out somehow. For we agree that a different nature must pursue a different occupation, and that the nature of woman and of man is different; yet we now say that natures which are different must pursue the same occupations. Is this the charge brought against us?

Precisely so. {{{ 454a }}}

Truly noble, Glaucon, I said, is the power of the craft of contradiction.

Why is that?

Because, I said, it seems to me that many fall into it even unwillingly, and think they are not disputing for victory but conversing, owing to their inability to examine what is said by dividing it according to forms, so that they chase after the contradiction of what was said by going on the word alone, using contentiousness rather than dialectic with one another.

That does indeed happen to many people, he said; but surely this does not apply to us at present, does it? {{{ 454b }}}

It certainly does, I said; at any rate we run the risk of touching on contradiction unwillingly.

How so?

The point that a nature which is ⟨not⟩ the same must not receive the same occupations we have pursued quite bravely and contentiously, going by the mere word, but we have not examined at all what form of the other and the same nature we were then defining, or to what it was directed, when we assigned different occupations to a different nature but the same occupations to the same.

No, indeed, he said, we did not examine it. {{{ 454c }}}

So then, I said, it is open to us, it seems, to ask ourselves whether the bald and the long-haired have the same nature and not the opposite, and, once we agree that it is the opposite, then, if the bald are cobblers, not to allow the long-haired to be, and if the long-haired are, not to allow the others.

That would be ridiculous, he said.

Is it ridiculous, I said, for any reason other than that at that time we were not setting down the same and the different nature in every sense, but were keeping in view only {{{ 454d }}} that form of difference and likeness which bears on the occupations themselves? For example, we said that a medical man and a medical woman, [being] such in soul, have the same nature; or do you not think so?

I do.

But a medical man and a carpenter, a different one?

Certainly.

Then, I said, if the class of men and the class of women appear to differ with respect to some craft or other occupation, we shall say that this must be assigned to each accordingly; but if they appear to differ only in this respect, that the female bears young {{{ 454e }}} while the male begets, we shall say that it has not yet been shown any the more that a woman differs from a man with respect to what we are speaking of, and we shall still suppose that our guardians and their women must pursue the same occupations.

And rightly so, he said.

Then after this do we not require the one who argues the opposite {{{ 455a }}} to teach us this very thing: for what craft, or what occupation among those concerned with the founding of a city, the nature of a woman and of a man is not the same but different?

That is only fair.

Perhaps, then, just as you yourself said a little earlier, someone else too might say that it is not easy to answer adequately on the spot, but that it is not at all difficult after due consideration.

He might well say so.

Do you want us, then, to ask the one who makes such objections to follow along {{{ 455b }}} with us, in case we can somehow show him that there is no occupation belonging to a woman in particular with regard to the management of a city?

Certainly.

Come then, we shall say to him, answer: did you not mean, when you spoke this way, that one person is well suited to something and another poorly suited, in that the one learns something easily, the other with difficulty? And that the one, from brief instruction, is highly inventive in what he learned, while the other, even after much instruction and practice, cannot even retain what he learned? And that in the one the body adequately serves the mind, while in the {{{ 455c }}} other it works against it? Are there any marks other than these by which you defined the person well suited for each pursuit and the one not suited?

No one, he said, will name any others.

Do you know, then, of any practice among human beings in which the class of men does not have all these advantages over the class of women? Or should we go on at length, speaking of weaving, and of the care of cakes and boiled dishes, in which the female class is thought to amount to something, and in which it is most ridiculous {{{ 455d }}} of all when it is bested?

You speak the truth, he said, that the one class is, so to speak, far outdone by the other in nearly everything. Many women, however, are better than many men at many things; but on the whole it is as you say.

There is, then, my friend, no occupation of those who manage a city that belongs to a woman because she is a woman, nor to a man because he is a man, but the natures are alike scattered among both creatures, and by {{{ 455e }}} nature a woman shares in all occupations, and a man in all, though in all of them a woman is weaker than a man.

Certainly.

Shall we, then, assign everything to men, and nothing to women?

How could we?

But there is, I believe, as we shall say, a woman skilled in medicine, and one who is not, and one skilled in music, and one unmusical by nature.

Of course. {{{ 456a }}}

[And] is there not also one skilled in gymnastic, and one skilled in war, while another is unwarlike and does not love exercise?

I think there is.

What about this? One who loves wisdom, and one who hates it? And one who is spirited, while another lacks spirit?

These too.

There is, then, also a woman fit to be a guardian, and one who is not. Or was it not just such a nature that we selected also for the men who are to be guardians?

Just such a one, indeed.

The nature of woman and of man, then, is the same with regard to guarding the city, except in so far as the one is weaker and the other stronger.

So it appears. {{{ 456b }}}

Then such women must be selected to live with such men and to guard alongside them, since they are capable and akin to them in nature.

Certainly.

And must not the same occupations be assigned to the same natures?

The same.

We have come round, then, back to our earlier position, and we agree that it is not against nature to assign music and gymnastic to the women of the guardians.

Certainly.

We were not, then, legislating anything impossible or like a mere wish, {{{ 456c }}} since we were laying down the law according to nature; rather, it is what is now done contrary to this that happens, it seems, more against nature.

So it seems.

Now was not our inquiry whether what we were saying was possible and best?

It was.

And has it not been agreed that it is possible?

Yes.

And must it not now, after this, be agreed that it is best?

Clearly.

Then, at least with a view to a woman's becoming fit to be a guardian, we shall not have one education making men for us and a different one making women, especially since {{{ 456d }}} it takes up the same nature to begin with?

Not a different one.

What, then, is your opinion concerning something of this sort?

Concerning what?

Whether you suppose, in your own mind, that one man is better and another worse; or do you consider them all alike?

Not at all.

In the city we were founding, then, do you think our guardians, having received the education we described, will have turned out better men, or the cobblers, trained in the cobbler's craft?

You ask a ridiculous question, he said.

I understand, I said. What of this? Are these not the best of all the {{{ 456e }}} other citizens?

By far.

What of this? Will these women not be the best of all the women?

This too, he said, by far.

Is there anything better for a city than for the best possible women and men to come to be in it?

There is not.

And will not music and gymnastic, when present as {{{ 457a }}} we have described, bring this about?

How could they not?

Then we were laying down not only a possible law for the city, but also the best one.

Just so.

The women of the guardians, then, must strip, since they will clothe themselves in virtue instead of garments, and must share in war and in the rest of the guarding of the city, and must do nothing else; but of these very duties the lighter parts must be given to the women rather than to the men, on account of the weakness of their sex. But the {{{ 457b }}} man who laughs at naked women exercising for the sake of what is best, plucking an unripe fruit of laughable wisdom, himself knows nothing, it seems, either of what he is laughing at or of what he is doing; for this is most finely said, and always will be: that the beneficial is beautiful, and the harmful is shameful.

Certainly.

Shall we say, then, that in speaking of the law concerning women we have escaped one wave, as it were, so that we are not completely swamped in laying down that our {{{ 457c }}} guardians, men and women alike, must pursue everything in common, but that the argument somehow agrees with itself that what it prescribes is both possible and beneficial?
