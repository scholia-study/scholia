## Truthfulness, Moderation, and What May Be Said of Men

Far more so, he said.

Then we will not accept such things about the gods from Homer either—
+ inextinguishable laughter broke out among the blessed gods,
+ as they watched Hephaestus hurrying through the halls—
not to be accepted, by your own argument.

If you want to put it down as mine, he said—for it certainly {{{ 389b }}} must not be accepted.

But indeed truth too must be prized highly. For if we were right just now in saying that falsehood is truly useless to the gods, but useful to human beings as a kind of medicine, clearly such a thing must be given to doctors, and ordinary people must not touch it.

Clearly, he said.

It belongs, then, to the rulers of the city, if to anyone, to lie for the sake of enemies or citizens, for the city's benefit, but all the rest must not touch such a thing; {{{ 389c }}} rather, we will say that for a private person to lie to such rulers is the same fault as, or a greater one than, a sick man's failing to tell his doctor, or a man in training his trainer, the truth about the condition of his own body, or someone's not telling a helmsman the real situation of the ship and the crew—how he himself or one of his fellow sailors is faring.

Very true, he said. {{{ 389d }}}

If then he catches anyone else in the city lying—
+ … among those who are craftsmen,
+ a seer, or a healer of ills, or a shaper of timbers—
he will punish him for introducing a practice as subversive and ruinous to a city as it would be to a ship.

Provided, he said, that the deed follows the word.

What next? Will our young men not need moderation?

How could they not?

And for the multitude, are not these the greatest parts of moderation: being obedient {{{ 389e }}} to rulers, and being themselves masters of the pleasures of drink and sex and food?

So it seems to me.

Such things, I think, we will say are well spoken, the sort of thing Diomedes says in Homer—
+ Good fellow, sit in silence, and yield to my word,
and what follows on from that—
+ … the Achaeans advanced, breathing fury,
+ in silence, in fear of their commanders, …
and whatever else is of that kind.

Well said.

What next? Are things like these—
+ You drunken sot, with the eyes of a dog and the heart of a deer—
{{{ 390a }}} and what comes after them—are these well said, and all the other insolent outbursts of private men against rulers that anyone has uttered in prose or in verse?

Not well said.

For I do not think such things are fitting for the young to hear, at least with a view to moderation; though if they afford some other pleasure, that is no surprise. Or how does it seem to you?

It seems so to me, he said.

What next? To portray the wisest of men as saying that this seems to him the finest of all things, when—
+ … beside them the tables are heaped full
{{{ 390b }}}
+ of bread and meat, and the cup-bearer, drawing wine from the bowl,
+ carries it round and pours it into the cups—
does that seem to you a fit thing for a young man to hear, with a view to his self-mastery? Or take the line—
+ to die of hunger is the most pitiful thing, and to meet one's end that way?
Or take Zeus, who, while the other gods and men were sleeping, alone stayed awake over the plans he had made, yet easily {{{ 390c }}} forgot all of it because of his desire for sex, and was so overwhelmed at the sight of Hera that he did not even want to go to their chamber, but wanted to lie with her right there on the ground, saying that he was gripped by desire as he had never been, not even when they first came together as lovers without their parents' knowing—or take also the binding of Ares and Aphrodite by Hephaestus for similar reasons.

By Zeus, no, he said—that does not seem fitting to me. {{{ 390d }}}

But wherever, I said, endurance in the face of everything is both told of and displayed by men of note, that we must watch and hear, such as this—
+ he struck his breast, and rebuked his heart with these words:
+ Hold firm, my heart; you have borne something more shameless than this before.

Certainly, he said.

Nor indeed must we allow our men to be bribe-takers or lovers of money. {{{ 390e }}}

By no means.

Nor should they sing that—
+ gifts persuade the gods, and gifts persuade revered kings—
nor should Phoenix, the tutor of Achilles, be praised for speaking with restraint when he advised him to defend the Achaeans once he had taken gifts, but not to lay aside his wrath without them. Nor will we consider it fitting, nor will we agree, that Achilles himself was so fond of money that he took gifts from Agamemnon, and, after taking payment, released the {{{ 391a }}} corpse, but was otherwise unwilling to do so.

It is certainly not right, he said, to praise such things.

And I hesitate, I said, for Homer's sake, to say that it is not even pious to assert these things about Achilles, or to believe them when others say so—and likewise how he spoke to Apollo—
+ You have hurt me, Far-Worker, most destructive of all the gods;
+ I would take vengeance on you, if only I had the power—
{{{ 391b }}} and how he defied the river, though it was a god, and stood ready to fight it, and again how he said of the sacred hair of the other river, Spercheius, that he would give it to the hero Patroclus to carry with him, though Patroclus was already dead, and that he actually did this—none of it is to be believed; nor again the dragging of Hector around the tomb of Patroclus, and the slaughter of the captives upon the pyre—all of this together we shall deny was truly told, nor shall we allow our people {{{ 391c }}} to be persuaded that Achilles, son of a goddess and of Peleus, who was most moderate of men and third in descent from Zeus, and who was raised by Chiron the wisest of all, was so full of turmoil as to harbor in himself two opposed afflictions, illiberality together with love of money, and, besides these, arrogance toward gods and men.

You are right, he said.

Let us then, I said, neither believe these things nor allow them to be said—that Theseus, son of Poseidon, and Pirithous, son of {{{ 391d }}} Zeus, set out on such dreadful abductions, nor that any other child of a god and hero would ever have dared such terrible and impious deeds, of the sort now falsely charged against them—but let us compel the poets to say either that these are not their deeds, or that these men are not children of gods, and not to assert both, nor let them attempt to persuade our young that the gods beget evil, and that heroes are no better than men; for, as we said before, these things are {{{ 391e }}} neither pious nor true, since we have shown, I believe, that it is impossible for evil to come from the gods.

Of course.

And indeed they are harmful to those who hear them; for everyone will forgive himself for being wicked, once persuaded that such things are done, and were done, even by—
+ … those close in kin to the gods,
+ ⟨those⟩ near to Zeus, whose ancestral altar, on the ridge of Ida,
+ stands in the upper air,
and—
+ the blood of the divine has not yet faded from them.
For this reason, such tales must be stopped, lest they breed in our young too great {{{ 392a }}} a readiness for wickedness.

Quite so, he said.

What kind of discourse, then, I said, is still left for us, in determining what sort should be told and what should not? For it has been stated how we must speak about the gods, and about spirits and heroes and those in Hades.

Certainly.

Then would what remains not be about human beings?

Clearly so.

It is impossible, my friend, for us to determine this, at any rate for the present.

How so?

Because I think we shall have to say that both poets and prose-writers {{{ 392b }}} speak badly about human beings in the most important matters, in that many unjust men are happy, while just men are wretched, and that doing wrong is profitable if it goes undetected, while justice is a good belonging to another but a loss to oneself; and we shall forbid them to say such things, and require them instead to sing and tell tales of the opposite. Or do you not think so?

I know it very well, he said.

Then if you agree that I speak rightly, shall I say that you have agreed to what we have long been seeking?

You have grasped it correctly, he said.
