## How Such a City Could Actually Come to Be

{{{ 540d }}} What then? I said — do you agree, about the city and the constitution, that we have not been uttering mere wishes, but things difficult, yet in some way possible, and in no other way than as we have said — whenever those who are truly philosophers become rulers in a city, whether several or one, and look down on the honors now current, considering them ignoble and worth nothing, but value {{{ 540e }}} most highly what is right and the honors that come from it, and, greatest and most necessary of all, what is just, and by serving and increasing this thoroughly reorder their own city?

How so? So he asked.

All those, I said, who happen to be older than ten years {{{ 541a }}} in the city, they will send out into the countryside, and taking their children away from the present ways, which even their parents have, will raise them up in their own manners and laws, such as we went through then; and so, most quickly and most easily, the city and constitution we have described, once established, will itself be happy, and will benefit most the people among whom it comes to be?

Very much so, he said; and however it might come about, if it ever does come about, {{{ 541b }}} you seem to me, Socrates, to have spoken well.

So then, I said, have our arguments not already said enough about this city and the man who resembles it? For it is clear, I suppose, what sort of man we shall say he must be.

Clear, he said, and what you ask seems to me to have reached its conclusion.
