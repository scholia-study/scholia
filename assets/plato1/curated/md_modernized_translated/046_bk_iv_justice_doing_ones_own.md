## Justice: Doing One's Own

{{{ 432b }}} I quite agree, he said.

Well then, I said — the three things have now been made out for us in the city, or so it would seem at any rate; but the remaining form, on account of which a city might still have a share of virtue — whatever could that be? For clearly this is justice.

Clearly.

So then, Glaucon, we must now post ourselves in a ring, like huntsmen around a thicket, and keep our minds on it, so that justice does not slip away somewhere and vanish out of sight. {{{ 432c }}} For clearly it is somewhere around here — so keep watch, and be eager to catch sight of it, in case you should happen to see it before I do and can tell me.

Would that I could, he said. But rather, if you make use of me as a follower, one able to see what is pointed out, you will be making quite reasonable use of me.

Follow, then, I said, after praying along with me.

I will do that, he said; only lead the way.

And indeed, I said, the place appears hard to travel and full of shadow; it is dark, at any rate, and difficult to search out. But all the same we must go on. {{{ 432d }}}

We must indeed, he said.

And catching sight of something, I said: Aha, aha, Glaucon — we seem to have some kind of track, and I think it will not altogether get away from us.

Good news, he said.

Truly, I said, our condition has been a foolish one.

What sort of thing?

It seems, my good man, that all this while it has been rolling about at our feet from the very start, and we simply did not see it — we were quite ridiculous, like people who sometimes {{{ 432e }}} go looking for the very thing they are holding in their hands, and we, instead of looking at it, kept gazing off somewhere in the distance, which is perhaps just why it escaped our notice.

How, he said, do you mean that?

In this way, I said — it seems to me that all along, both speaking of it and hearing of it, we failed to understand ourselves, namely that we were in a manner already saying it.

That is a long preamble, he said, for someone eager to hear it. {{{ 433a }}}

Well, I said, listen and see whether there is anything in what I say. For what we laid down from the beginning as something that must be done throughout, when we were founding the city — this, as it seems to me, is justice, or at any rate justice is some kind of it. We laid it down, I think, and said many times, if you remember, that each single person ought to practice one of the occupations connected with the city, the one for which his nature was best fitted.

We did say that.

And indeed, that doing one's own and not meddling is justice — this too we have heard {{{ 433b }}} said by many others, and have often said ourselves.

We have indeed said it.

This, then, my friend, I said, when it comes about in a certain way, is likely to be justice: the doing of one's own. Do you know from what I infer this?

No, but tell me, he said.

It seems to me, I said, that what remains in the city, of the things we have examined — moderation, courage, and practical wisdom — is this: whatever gave all of those the power to come into being, and, once they had come into being, preserves them for as long as it {{{ 433c }}} is present. And yet we said that justice would be whatever was left over from those, if we found the three.

Yes, that must be so, he said.

But indeed, I said, if we had to judge which of these, once present, would do the most to make our city good, it would be hard to decide whether it is the like-mindedness of rulers and ruled, or the preservation, among the soldiers, of a lawful opinion about what is to be feared and what is not — whatever such things really are — or the {{{ 433d }}} practical wisdom and watchfulness present in the rulers, or whether what makes it good above all is this, present alike in child and woman, slave and free, craftsman, ruler, and ruled: that each person, being one, was doing his own and not meddling.

Hard to decide, he said — how could it not be?

So it seems, then, that the power by which each person in the city does his own is a match, for the city's virtue, for its wisdom and its moderation and its courage.

Very much so, he said.

Then would you not set down as justice whatever is a match for these in the {{{ 433e }}} virtue of the city?

Quite so.

Consider it this way too, and see whether it will seem so — will you assign to the rulers in the city the task of judging lawsuits?

What else?

And will they judge, aiming at anything more than this — that each person should neither hold what belongs to another nor be deprived of what is his own?

No, but at this.

As being just?

Yes.

So in this way too, then, it might be agreed that the having and doing of what is one's own and belongs to {{{ 434a }}} oneself is justice.

That is so.

See now whether you agree with what I think. If a carpenter undertakes to do the work of a shoemaker, or a shoemaker that of a carpenter, or they exchange with one another their tools or their honors, or the same man undertakes to do both, with everything else interchanged in this way, does it seem to you that this would do any great harm to a city?

Not really, he said.

But whenever, I think, a man who is a craftsman, or by nature some other sort of {{{ 434b }}} money-maker, is then puffed up by wealth or numbers or strength or something else of that kind and undertakes to pass into the class of the warrior, or when someone among the warriors passes into the class of counsellor and guardian though unworthy of it, and these exchange with one another their tools and their honors, or when the same man undertakes to do all these things at once — then, I think, you too believe that this exchange and meddling among them is ruin for the city.

Yes, absolutely.

So then, meddling and exchange among the three classes, {{{ 434c }}} passing into one another, is the greatest harm to the city, and would most correctly be called, above all, wrongdoing.

Entirely so.

And will you not say that the greatest wrongdoing against one's own city is injustice?

How could it not be?

This, then, is injustice. But let us put it again this way: when the money-making, auxiliary, and guardian classes each do their own in the city, that — being the opposite of the other — would be justice, and would make the city just?
