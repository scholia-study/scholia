## The Imitator Knows Nothing

Most true, he said.

Then, I said, after this we must examine tragedy and its leader, Homer, since we hear from some {{{ 598e }}} that these men know all crafts, and all human matters relating to virtue and vice, and even the divine things; for the good poet, if he is to compose well about the things he composes, must necessarily compose with knowledge, or else be unable to compose at all. So we must examine whether, in encountering these imitators, they have been deceived and, seeing their works, do not perceive {{{ 599a }}} that these are three removes from reality and easy to produce for one who does not know the truth — for they produce phantoms, not realities — or whether there is also something in what they say, and the good poets really do know the things about which they seem, to the many, to speak well.

Yes indeed, he said, it must be examined.

Do you think, then, that if someone were able to make both things — the thing to be imitated and the image of it — he would let himself take the making of images seriously and set that up as {{{ 599b }}} the best thing in his own life?

I do not.

But surely, I think, if he really had knowledge of the truth about the very things he imitates, he would be far more serious about the deeds themselves than about the imitations, and would try to leave behind many fine achievements of his own as monuments, and would be more eager to be the one praised than the one praising.

I think so, he said; for the honor and the benefit are not equal.

So then, let us not require an account from Homer {{{ 599c }}} or any other of the poets about the other matters, asking whether any of them was actually a doctor and not merely an imitator of doctors' talk, or whom any poet, old or new, is said to have made healthy, as Asclepius did, or what pupils in medicine he left behind, as Asclepius left his descendants; nor again let us ask them about the other crafts, but let all that go — yet about the greatest and finest things Homer undertakes to speak of, wars and generalship and the governance of cities, and the {{{ 599d }}} education of man, it is surely fair to question him, asking: dear Homer, if you are not third from the truth where virtue is concerned — a maker of an image, which is what we defined an imitator to be — but are second, and were able to recognize what practices make men better or worse, in private and in public life, tell us which city was better governed because of you, as Sparta was because of Lycurgus, and as many cities, great and small, were {{{ 599e }}} because of many other men? But what city credits you with having been a good lawgiver and having benefited it? Italy and Sicily credit Charondas, and we credit Solon; but you — who credits you? Will he be able to name anyone?

I do not think so, said Glaucon; at any rate nothing of the sort is told, even by the Homeridae themselves. {{{ 600a }}}

But is any war in Homer's time remembered as having been well fought under his command or his counsel?

None.

But what of the many shrewd and inventive ideas for the crafts or for other undertakings that are told of a wise man's practical work, as again in the case of Thales of Miletus and Anacharsis the Scythian?

Nothing of the sort at all.

But then, if not in public life, is Homer, while he was alive, said to have been a leader in education for certain people in private, who loved him for his company {{{ 600b }}} and handed down to those after them a certain Homeric way of life — just as Pythagoras himself was especially loved for this, and his successors, even now, calling their way of life Pythagorean, seem somehow to stand out from the rest?

Nor is anything of that sort said either, he said. For Creophylus, Socrates, Homer's companion, would perhaps appear even more laughable than his name where education is concerned, if what is said about Homer is true. For it is said that Homer was much neglected {{{ 600c }}} by that very man, while he was alive.

So it is indeed said, I replied. But do you think, Glaucon, that if Homer had really been able to educate men and make them better — being able, in these matters, not merely to imitate but to know — he would not have made many companions and been honored and loved by them, given that Protagoras of Abdera, and Prodicus of Ceos, and a great many others, are able to convince their contemporaries, by meeting with them privately, {{{ 600d }}} that they will not be able to manage even their own household or city unless these men themselves oversee their education, and are so intensely loved for this wisdom that their companions all but carry them around on their heads — whereas his own contemporaries would have let Homer, if he really had been able to benefit men in respect of virtue, or Hesiod, go about merely reciting their verses, rather than clinging to them more than to gold, and {{{ 600e }}} compelling them to stay at home with them, or, if they could not persuade them, following them about themselves wherever they went as attendants, until they had received a sufficient share of education?

In every way, he said, you seem to me, Socrates, to speak the truth.

Then shall we lay it down, beginning from Homer, that all the poetic imitators are imitators of images of virtue and of the other things they compose about, and do not touch the truth, but, as we were just saying, the painter will make something that seems {{{ 601a }}} to be a shoemaker, though he himself understands nothing of shoemaking — and he makes it for people who understand no more than he does, and who judge only from colors and shapes?

Yes indeed.

So too, I think, we shall say that the poet, understanding nothing but only imitating, lays a kind of coloring, in names and phrases, over each of the crafts, so that to others just like him, who judge from the words alone, it seems — whether someone speaks about shoemaking in meter and rhythm and harmony, or about generalship, or about {{{ 601b }}} anything else whatsoever — that it is very well spoken; so great by nature is the enchantment these very things possess. For I think you know how the poets' works appear when stripped of the colors of music and spoken just by themselves. For you have surely seen it.

I have, he said.

Then, said I, it is like the faces of people who are youthful but not beautiful, the way they appear once their bloom has faded?

Very much so, he said.

Come now, consider this: the maker of the image, the imitator, we say, understands nothing of what is, but only of what appears — is that not {{{ 601c }}} so?

Yes.

Then let us not leave the matter half-stated, but look at it properly.

Speak, he said.

A painter, we say, will paint reins and a bit?

Yes.

But a leatherworker and a smith will make them?

Certainly.

Does the painter, then, understand what the reins and the bit ought to be like? Or not even the makers, the smith and the leatherworker, but only the one who knows how to use them, the horseman?

Most true.

Shall we not say, then, that this holds for everything? {{{ 601d }}}

How so?

That for each thing there are these three crafts — one that will use it, one that will make it, one that will imitate it?

Yes.

Then the virtue and beauty and rightness of each implement and living creature and action are relative to nothing other than the use for which each has been made or has naturally arisen?

So it is.

It is quite necessary, then, that the one who uses each thing be the most experienced with it, and that he report to the maker what good or bad effects it has in the use he puts it to; just as, I suppose, a flute player reports {{{ 601e }}} to the flute maker about the flutes that serve him well in playing, and will instruct him what sort to make, and the other will comply.

How could it not?

So the one who knows reports about good and bad flutes, while the other will make them, trusting him?

Yes.

Then, regarding the same implement, the maker will have correct belief about what is fine and bad in it, from associating with the one who knows and being compelled {{{ 602a }}} to listen to the one who knows, while the user has knowledge.

Certainly.

But the imitator — will he have knowledge, from using them, about the things he depicts, whether they are fine and correct or not; or correct opinion, from being necessarily in the company of the one who knows and instructed in what he ought to depict?

Neither.

So the imitator will neither know nor have correct opinion about the things he imitates, with respect to what is fine or bad in them.

It seems not.

The one who imitates in poetry would be a fine one indeed, as regards wisdom about the things he composes.

Not entirely. {{{ 602b }}}

But he will imitate all the same, not knowing about each thing in what way it is bad or good; but, as it seems, whatever appears fine to the many, who know nothing, this is what he will imitate.

What else?

This much, then, as it appears, has been fairly well agreed between us: that the one who imitates knows nothing worth mentioning about the things he imitates, but that imitation is a kind of play and not a serious matter, and that those who take up tragic poetry, in iambics and in epic verse, are all imitators to the greatest degree possible.

Yes indeed.
