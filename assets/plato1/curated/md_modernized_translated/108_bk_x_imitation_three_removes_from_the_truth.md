## Imitation: Three Removes From the Truth

{{{ 595a }}} And indeed, I said, many other things about it make me think that we founded the city altogether rightly, and not least do I say so when I reflect on poetry.

What sort of thing? He said.

Our refusing to admit any of it that is imitative; for now, more than ever and more plainly, as it seems to me, it is clear that it must not be admitted, since the {{{ 595b }}} kinds of the soul have each been distinguished separately.

How do you mean?

Speaking just among you here — for you will not report me to the tragic poets and all the other imitators — all such things seem to be a maiming of the understanding of those who hear them, of all who lack the remedy of knowing what they really are.

And with what in mind, he said, do you say that?

It must be said, I replied; and yet a certain affection and reverence for Homer, which I have had since childhood, holds me back from saying it. For he seems to have {{{ 595c }}} been the first teacher and leader of all these fine tragic poets. But a man must not be honored above the truth, so, as I say, it must be said.

Quite so, he said.

Listen, then — or rather, answer.

Ask.

Could you tell me at all what imitation actually is? For I do not altogether grasp, myself, what it is supposed to be.

So I'm the one who will grasp it, he said.

That's nothing strange, I said, since in many cases those who see more dimly have seen things sooner than those {{{ 596a }}} whose sight is keener.

That is so, he said; but with you present, I would not even be able to bring myself to say what appears to me, if anything does — so look at it yourself.

Do you want us, then, to begin our inquiry here, from our usual method? For we are accustomed to positing some single form for each set of many things to which we apply the same name. Or don't you follow?

I follow.

Let us, then, posit now whichever of the many things you like. For instance, if you {{{ 596b }}} like, there are surely many beds and tables.

Of course.

But there are, I think, two forms for these implements — one of a bed, one of a table.

Yes.

And don't we also usually say that the craftsman of each implement, with his eye on the form, makes in that way the things we use — the one beds, the other tables — and likewise for everything else? For surely no craftsman makes the form itself — how could he?

Not at all.

But consider this other craftsman too — say what you would call him. {{{ 596c }}}

Which one?

The one who makes everything that each and every artisan makes.

That's an extraordinary and amazing man you're talking about.

Not yet, but soon you will say so even more. For this same artisan is able to make not only all implements, but also everything that grows out of the earth; and he fashions all living creatures, himself along with the rest, and besides these he fashions earth and sky and gods and everything in the sky and everything under the earth in Hades. {{{ 596d }}}

That's an utterly amazing sophist you're talking about, he said.

You don't believe me? I said. Tell me — does it seem to you that there could be no such craftsman at all, or that he might become the maker of all these things in one way, but not in another? Or don't you realize that you yourself would be able to make all these things too, in a certain way?

And what, he said, is this way?

It's not difficult, I said — in fact it can be produced in many ways, and quickly, but the quickest way, I suppose, is this: if you're willing to take a mirror {{{ 596e }}} and carry it around everywhere, you will quickly make the sun and the things in the sky, quickly the earth, and quickly yourself and the other animals and implements and plants and everything else that was just mentioned.

Yes, he said, things that appear, but surely not things that really are, in truth.

Good, I said, and you come exactly to the point that was needed. For the painter too, I think, is one of these sorts of craftsmen. Isn't he?

Of course he is.

But you will say, I think, that what he makes is not true. And yet in a certain way the painter too makes a bed — or does he not?

Yes, he said, he too makes only an apparent one. {{{ 597a }}}

And what about the bed-maker? Weren't you just saying that he does not make the form, which we say is what a bed is, but rather a particular bed?

Yes, I did say that.

So if he does not make what really is, he would not be making that which is, but something like that which is, though not itself a thing that is — and if someone were to say that the bed-maker's work, or that of any other artisan, is completely real, wouldn't he risk speaking untruly?

He would not be, he said, at least as it would seem to those who spend their time on arguments of this kind.

Then let us not be surprised if this too turns out to be something dim in relation to truth. {{{ 597b }}}

No, let's not.

Do you want us, then, I said, to use these very examples to investigate this imitator — what exactly he is?

If you like, he said.

So these turn out to be three beds in all: one that exists in nature, which we would say, I think, god made. Or who else?

No one, I think.

And one that the carpenter made.

Yes, he said.

And one that the painter made. Isn't that so?

Let it be so.

Painter, bed-maker, god — these three preside over three forms of beds.

Yes, three. {{{ 597c }}}

Now god, whether because he did not wish it, or because some necessity lay upon him not to produce more than one bed in nature, made in just this way that one alone, the very thing that a bed is; and two or more such were neither planted by god, nor will they ever grow.

How so? He asked.

Because, I said, if he made only two, then once again a single one would appear, whose form both of those two would in turn possess, and that one would be what a bed is, and not the two.

Right, he said. {{{ 597d }}}

So god, I think, knowing this, and wanting to be really the maker of a bed that really is, and not the maker of some particular bed nor some particular bed-maker, made it grow as one by nature.

It seems so.

Do you want us, then, to call him the grower of this, or something of that sort?

That's fair, he said, at least since it is by nature that he has made this and everything else too.

And what about the carpenter? Isn't he a craftsman of a bed?

Yes.

And is the painter too a craftsman and maker of that kind of thing?

Not at all.

Then what will you say he is, in relation to a bed? {{{ 597e }}}

This, he said, seems to me the most fitting name for him — an imitator of what those others are craftsmen of.

Very well, I said; so you call an imitator the one who is third in generation from nature?

Certainly, he said.

Then the tragic poet too will be this, if indeed he is an imitator — third by nature from the king and from the truth — and so will all the other imitators.

So it seems.

We have, then, agreed about the imitator. But tell me this about {{{ 598a }}} the painter: does he seem to you to try to imitate each of those things themselves as they are in nature, or the works of the craftsmen?

The works of the craftsmen, he said.

As they are, or as they appear? That, too, you must still determine.

What do you mean? He asked.

Like this: does a bed, whether you view it from the side or straight on or from any angle at all, become in any way different from itself, or does it not differ at all, but only look different? And likewise for everything else?

Just so, he said; it appears different, but it does not differ at all. {{{ 598b }}}

Consider this very point: which of the two is painting directed at, in each case? Is it to imitate the thing as it is, in reality, or the thing as it appears — is it an imitation of appearance, or of truth?

Of appearance, he said.

The imitative craft, then, is far removed from the truth, and it seems that this is why it produces everything — because it grasps only a small part of each thing, and that part is a mere image. For instance, we say, the painter will paint us a shoemaker, a carpenter, the other craftsmen, {{{ 598c }}} without understanding anything of any of their crafts — but nevertheless, if he were a good painter, by painting a carpenter and showing it from a distance, he could deceive children and foolish people into thinking it was truly a carpenter.

Of course he could.

But here, my friend, is what I think we must bear in mind about all such cases: whenever someone reports to us that he has met a man who knows all the crafts, and everything else that each individual person knows, with nothing that he does not know more {{{ 598d }}} precisely than anyone, we must suppose that the person telling us this is rather naive, and that, it seems, he has been deceived by meeting some wizard and imitator, so that the man seemed to him to be all-wise, because he himself was unable to distinguish knowledge, ignorance, and imitation.
