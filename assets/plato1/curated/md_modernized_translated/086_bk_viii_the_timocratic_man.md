## The Timocratic Man

And rightly, he said.

Who then is the man who answers to this constitution? How does he come to be, and what sort of man is he?

I think, said Adeimantus, that as far as love of victory goes he comes close to our Glaucon here. {{{ 548e }}}

Perhaps that much is so, I said; but it seems to me that these traits are not natural to him.

What traits?

He must, I said, be somewhat more self-willed and somewhat less musical, yet a lover of music, and fond of listening, though {{{ 549a }}} by no means eloquent. Such a man would be harsh toward slaves — not out of contempt for slaves, as the properly educated man is — yet gentle toward the free, thoroughly obedient to rulers, fond of rule and fond of honor, thinking himself fit to rule not on the strength of speech, nor of anything of that kind, but of deeds of war and of the business of war, and being something of a lover of exercise and of hunting.

Yes, he said, that is the character of that constitution.

And would not such a man, I said, while young {{{ 549b }}} despise money, but the older he becomes, always embrace it more, because he shares in the money-loving nature, and is not pure toward virtue, since he has been deprived of the best guardian?

Of what? Adeimantus asked.

Of reason, I said, blended with music; this alone, once it has come to be in a man, lives on in him as the savior of virtue throughout his life.

Well said, he replied.

And such, I said, is the timocratic young man, resembling the city of that kind. {{{ 549c }}}

Quite so.

He comes to be, I said, in some such way as this: sometimes he is the young son of a good father who lives in a city not well governed, a father who avoids honors and offices and lawsuits and all such meddling, and who is willing to be at a disadvantage in order to avoid trouble—

In what way does this happen? he asked.

Whenever, I said, he first hears his mother complaining that her husband is not among the rulers, and that on this account {{{ 549d }}} she is worse off among the other women, and then, when she sees that his father is not much concerned about money and does not quarrel and hurl abuse in private lawsuits or in public but bears all such things with easy unconcern, and when he himself perceives that his father always keeps his mind on his own affairs and neither greatly honors nor slights her, hears her vexed at all of this and saying that his father is unmanly and far too slack, and harping on all the {{{ 549e }}} other things, and the sort of things, that women love to go on about in such cases.

Very much so, said Adeimantus, many things, and all typical of women.

You know, I said, that the household servants of such people sometimes say things of this sort privately to the sons — servants who are thought to be well-disposed — and that if they see someone owing money whom the father does not prosecute, or doing some other wrong, they urge the boy on, so that once he becomes a man he will punish all such people {{{ 550a }}} and be more of a man than his father. And when he goes out, he hears and sees other such things: those who mind their own business in the city being called simpletons and counted for little, while those who do not mind their own business are honored and praised. Then indeed the young man, hearing and seeing all such things, and again hearing his father's words and seeing, close up, his practices as against those of others, drawn {{{ 550b }}} by both of these — his own father watering and increasing the rational part in his soul, while the others do the same to the appetitive and the spirited, because he is not by nature a bad man, but has kept the bad company of others — drawn, as I say, by both of them, he came to the middle, and handed over the rule within himself to the part that is middling, victory-loving, and spirited, and became a high-minded, honor-loving man.

You seem to me, he said, to have given a full account of his origin.
