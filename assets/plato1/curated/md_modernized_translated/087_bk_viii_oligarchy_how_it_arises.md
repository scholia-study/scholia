## Oligarchy: How It Arises

{{{ 550c }}} Then we have, I said, both the second constitution and the second man.

We have, he said.

Then after this shall we speak, in Aeschylus's phrase, of a different man ranged against a different city — or rather, following our plan, of the city first?

Certainly, he said.

And the one that comes after such a constitution would be, I think, oligarchy.

And what arrangement do you mean by oligarchy? he said.

The constitution based on a property assessment, I said, {{{ 550d }}} in which the rich rule and the poor have no share in office.

I understand, he said.

Then must we not first tell how it changes from timocracy into oligarchy?

Yes.

And indeed, I said, even a blind man can see how it changes.

How so?

It is that storehouse, I said, each man's, filling up with gold, that destroys such a constitution. For first they find ways to spend on themselves, and twist the laws to this end, disobeying them, they and their wives alike.

Likely, he said. {{{ 550e }}}

Then, I suppose, one man seeing another and falling into rivalry with him, they made the mass of themselves like that.

Likely.

From there, then, I said, they press on further into money-making, and the more they hold this in honor, the less honor they give virtue. Or is not virtue set apart from wealth in this way — as if each lay in the pan of a balance, always tipping to opposite sides?

Very much so, he said. {{{ 551a }}}

When wealth is honored in a city, then, and the rich along with it, virtue and good men are held in less honor.

Clear.

What is always honored is practiced, then, while what is dishonored is neglected.

So it is.

So instead of men who love victory and honor, they end up becoming money-makers and lovers of money, and they praise and admire the rich man and bring him into office, while they dishonor the poor man.

Very much so.

Then at that point they establish a law setting the boundary of an oligarchic constitution, {{{ 551b }}} fixing a sum of money as the requirement — more where the oligarchy is stronger, less where it is weaker — and declaring in advance that whoever does not have property reaching the assessment set shall have no share in office; and they carry this out either by force of arms, or else, even before resorting to that, by frightening people into establishing such a constitution. Or is it not so?

Just so.

This, then, so to speak, is the arrangement.

Yes, he said; but what then is the character of the constitution? And what {{{ 551c }}} are the faults we said it has?

First, I said, this very point — what sort of boundary it has. For consider: if someone were to appoint ships' pilots in this way, based on property assessments, and refuse the post to a poor man even if he were the more skilled pilot—

A wretched voyage, he said, they would sail.

Then is it not likewise so with any other office whatsoever [or any]?

I think so, at least.

Except the city? I said — or is it also so with the city?

Most of all, he said, by far, seeing that this office is the hardest and the greatest. {{{ 551d }}}

That, then, would be one fault of oligarchy, and of such a size.

So it appears.

What next? Is this one any less than that?

What is it?

That such a city must necessarily be not one but two — one of the poor, one of the rich — dwelling in the same place, always plotting against each other.

No less at all, by Zeus, he said.

And moreover, this too is not good — that they are unable, perhaps, to wage any war, since they are forced either, if they make use of {{{ 551e }}} the armed populace, to fear that mass more than the enemy, or, if they do not make use of it, to show themselves, in the actual fighting, as truly oligarchic — that is, few — and at the same time to be unwilling to contribute money, since they love money.

Not good.

What next? The thing we found fault with long ago — the same men farming, {{{ 552a }}} making money, and fighting all at once in such a constitution — does that seem to be right?

Not in any way at all.

Consider then whether, of all these evils, this constitution is the first to admit the greatest one.

What is it?

That it is permitted to sell off all one's property, and for another to acquire it, and, having sold it, to go on living in the city as none of the city's parts — neither money-maker nor craftsman nor horseman nor hoplite — but called poor and without resources. {{{ 552b }}}

The first, he said.

Well, this sort of thing is certainly not prevented in cities ruled by oligarchy; for otherwise some would not be excessively rich while others were altogether poor.

Rightly.

But consider this: when such a man, being rich, was spending, was he then any more of use to the city for the things we were just now speaking of? Or did he only seem to be one of the rulers, while in truth he was neither a ruler nor a servant of the city, but a spender of what lay ready to hand? {{{ 552c }}}

So it was, he said; he seemed to be one, but was nothing else than a spender.

Do you want us, then, I said, to say of him that, just as a drone arises in a honeycomb, a disease of the hive, so such a man arises in a household as a drone, a disease of the city?

Certainly, he said, Socrates.

Then, Adeimantus, has god made all the winged drones stingless, while of these that go on foot, some are stingless and some have terrible stings? And from the stingless come those who end up beggars in old age, while from {{{ 552d }}} those with stings come all who are called wrongdoers?

Most true, he said.

Then it is clear, I said, that in a city where you see beggars, there are, somewhere hidden in that same place, thieves and cutpurses and temple-robbers and craftsmen of all such evils.

Clear, he said.

What then? Do you not see beggars present in cities ruled by oligarchy?

Nearly everyone, he said, except those who rule. {{{ 552e }}}

Then must we not suppose, I said, that there are also many wrongdoers among them who have stings, whom the rulers hold in check by force and vigilance?

We must indeed, he said.

Shall we not say, then, that such men arise there because of lack of education, bad upbringing, and the arrangement of the constitution?

We shall say so.

Well then, such, at any rate, would be the city ruled by oligarchy, having so many evils — and perhaps even more.

Pretty much so, he said.
