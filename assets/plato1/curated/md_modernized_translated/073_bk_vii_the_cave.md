## The Cave

{{{ 514a }}} After this, I said, compare our nature — in respect of education and its lack — to a condition like the following. Picture people in a dwelling underground, shaped like a cave, with its entrance open toward the light and running the length of the whole cave; people who have lived there since childhood, chained by the legs and by the neck, so that they stay in place and see only what is straight in {{{ 514b }}} front of them, unable to turn their heads around in a circle because of the chain; with light reaching them from a fire burning above and far off behind them; and between the fire and the prisoners, a path running along above, and alongside this path, picture a low wall built up, like the screens showmen set up in front of the audience, above which they display their marvels.

I see, he said.

Now picture people carrying alongside this wall {{{ 514c }}} all sorts of objects that project above it, and statues of men {{{ 515a }}} and figures of other animals worked in stone and wood and every kind of material, and, as you would expect, some of the carriers talking and others silent.

You are describing a strange image, he said, and strange prisoners.

Like us, I said; for in the first place, do you think such people would have seen anything of themselves or of one another besides the shadows the fire throws on the wall of the cave facing them?

How could they, he said, if they were compelled {{{ 515b }}} to hold their heads motionless throughout their lives?

And what about the things carried past? Isn't it the same with them?

What else?

If, then, they were able to talk with one another, don't you think they would take whatever they saw to be the real things?

Necessarily.

And what if the prison also had an echo coming from the wall facing them? Do you think that, whenever one of those passing by spoke, they would take what was speaking to be anything but the shadow passing by?

No, by Zeus, not I, he said. {{{ 515c }}}

Then surely, I said, such people would take nothing to be true except the shadows of the manufactured objects.

Quite necessarily, he said.

Consider, then, I said, what their release and cure from both the chains and their folly would be like, if something of this sort were to happen to them by nature: whenever one of them was released and compelled suddenly to stand up, turn his neck, walk, and look up toward the light, and in doing all this felt pain, and because of the glare was unable to make out clearly the things whose {{{ 515d }}} shadows he had until then been seeing, what do you think he would say, if someone told him that before he had been seeing nothing but nonsense, but now, being nearer to what is and turned toward things more fully real, he was seeing more correctly, and, further, if that person, pointing to each of the things passing by, were to compel him by questioning to say what it is? Don't you think he would be at a loss, and consider what he formerly saw more true than what is now being shown to him?

Far more so, he said. {{{ 515e }}}

And then, if someone compelled him to look directly at the light itself, wouldn't his eyes hurt, and wouldn't he flee, turning away toward the things he is able to see clearly, and consider these to be really clearer than what is being shown to him?

So it is, he said.

And if, I said, someone dragged him away from there by force, up the rough, steep ascent, and did not let him go before hauling him out into the light of the sun, wouldn't he suffer {{{ 516a }}} pain and resent being dragged, and when he came to the light, wouldn't he find his eyes so full of glare that he could not see even one of the things now said to be true?

No, he said — not suddenly, at least.

He would need time to grow accustomed, I imagine, if he is going to see the things above. And first he would most easily make out shadows, and after that the reflections of people and of other things in water, and later the things themselves; and from these he would more easily gaze, by night, at the things in the sky and at the sky itself, looking at the light of the {{{ 516b }}} stars and the moon, than by day at the sun and the sun's light.

How could he not?

Last of all, I think, he would be able to look upon the sun — not its images in water or in some place not its own, but the sun itself, by itself, in its own region — and see what it is like.

Necessarily, he said.

And after this he would go on to reason that this is the one that provides the seasons and the years, and governs {{{ 516c }}} everything in the visible region, and is in some way the cause of all the things they used to see.

Clearly, he said, that is what he would come to after those earlier stages.

Well then? Don't you think that, recalling his first dwelling, and the wisdom there, and his former fellow prisoners, he would count himself fortunate for the change and pity them?

Very much so.

And if there were honors and praises among them at that time, from one another, and prizes for the one who saw most sharply the things passing by, and who best remembered which of them were accustomed to pass earlier, which later {{{ 516d }}}, and which together, and who from these was most able to divine what was about to come, do you think he would be desirous of such things and envy those among them who were honored and held power, or would he rather have felt what Homer describes, and want badly to work the soil as a hired hand to another man, one with no land of his own, and to endure anything whatsoever, rather than hold those opinions and live in that way? {{{ 516e }}}

Yes, he said, I think he would accept enduring anything at all, rather than live in that way.

And consider this too, I said. If such a person went back down and sat again in the same seat, wouldn't he have his eyes full of darkness, coming suddenly from the sun?

Certainly, he said.

And if he had to compete again in judging those shadows against the men who had been prisoners all along, while his eyes were still dim, {{{ 517a }}} before they had settled — and the time needed to grow accustomed were not at all short — wouldn't he become a laughingstock, and wouldn't it be said of him that he went up and came back with his eyes ruined, and that it isn't even worth trying to go up? And the one who tried to release them and lead them up — if they could somehow get their hands on him and kill him — wouldn't they kill him?

Indeed they would, he said.
